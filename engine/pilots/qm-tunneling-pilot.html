Guided inquiry

1 · Predict

With the starting settings the packet's mean energy 〈E〉 = k₀²/2 ≈ 1.1 lies below the barrier height V₀ = 2. Predict: what happens to the probability when the packet reaches the barrier?

2 · Run and observe the split

Press Play and watch |ψ|² meet the barrier. Ripples appear where the incident and reflected parts of the wavefunction interfere, and a small packet emerges on the far side even though 〈E〉 < V₀.

Let the run finish: this step completes once the packet has cleared the barrier and R + T is final.

3 · Make T tiny — but never zero

Raise V₀ to 4 or more and let a full run finish. The transmitted probability T collapses toward zero as the barrier towers over 〈E〉 — but it never quite reaches zero.

This step completes after a finished run with V₀ ≥ 4 and T < 0.02.

4 · Thin barriers leak more

Now keep V₀ fixed and change the barrier width w, then let a run finish. Compare T across widths: when 〈E〉 < V₀, T falls roughly exponentially as w grows, so thin barriers leak far more probability.

This step completes after a finished run at a different w.

About this simulation

A normalized Gaussian wavepacket is prepared at x₀ = −40 with mean momentum k₀ and evolves under the Schrödinger equation with a rectangular barrier of height V₀ and width w centered at x = 0 (natural units, ℏ = m = 1).

The filled curve is the probability density |ψ|². R and T are the probabilities found on the left and right of the barrier; probability damped in the absorbing boundary zones is added to the side where it was absorbed, so R + T + (barrier region) always sums to 1.

The dashed line marks the packet's mean energy 〈E〉 = k₀²/2 on the same energy scale as the barrier. The readout also shows the closed-form plane-wave transmission probability evaluated at 〈E〉 — the packet's finite energy spread makes the measured T differ from it by a few percent (more where T is very small).
