Guided inquiry

1 · Read the dipole picture

Field lines leave the positive charge radially, curve through space, and land on the negative charge. The green rings are equipotentials — surfaces of constant V. Check by eye: every crossing between a line and a ring is a right angle.

The one line launched straight away from the negative charge escapes to the boundary instead — not every line finds the far charge.

2 · Like charges repel the lines too

Switch the configuration to two like charges. Now no line connects the charges: the lines bend away from the midpoint, where the two fields cancel (a stagnation point). The equipotentials merge into peanut-shaped curves around both charges.

3 · Line count encodes charge

Raise q₁ and watch the number of lines leaving that charge grow in proportion — that is Gauss's law in cartoon form. Changing the separation reshapes the geometry but never changes how many lines each charge owns.

About this simulation

The electric field of 1–2 point charges by Coulomb superposition in natural units (k = 1, charge in units of e). Field lines are traced by integrating the normalized field direction with fixed arc-length RK4 steps; they start on a small circle around each charge, with a count proportional to |q|, and stop on a charge, at the boundary, or at a stagnation point.

Equipotential curves V(x, y) = const are extracted by marching squares and refined onto the exact level; electrostatics guarantees they cross field lines at right angles, since E = −∇V.

Everything here is static: these are pictures of the field's geometry. Nothing flows along a field line, and a released test charge would follow the Lorentz force, not the line it starts on.
