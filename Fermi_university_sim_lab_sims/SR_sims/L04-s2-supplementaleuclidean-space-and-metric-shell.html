Simulation 2 — the Euclidean space & metric demo from Lecture 2, extended with controls that translate the origin and rotate the axes (guided-inquiry mode).
Concept
The Euclidean distance between two points is a scalar invariant — unchanged by translations or rotations of the coordinate system, even though each point's coordinates change.
Serves
Identify translations & rotations as coordinate transformations; use the Euclidean metric to define invariants; distinguish scalars from vectors; derive the spatial distance (interval) as a scalar invariant; treat invariance as the guide for constructing physical observables.
Inquiry
Are Δx, Δy invariant under translation? Under rotation? What combination of them is invariant under both — and why?
Misconception
"If the coordinates change, the distance computed from them must change too." (Encoded as prediction choices a/b; rebutted only on selection.)
Kick-off
Students formulate their own definition of "physical observable" from the simulation evidence before the formal one is given (Claudia's prompt).

Guided Inquiry

1 · Two points on a plane

Let’s set things up. Points A and B sit at fixed places on a plane, and a Cartesian coordinate system is laid over them. Its origin can be shifted by (Tx, Ty), and its axes can be turned through an angle θ.

Keep in mind that every number on screen — each point’s coordinates, the differences Δx′ and Δy′, and the distance d — is read in whichever coordinate system you’re currently using.

2 · Shifting the origin

First, we’ll slide the grid. The origin of the coordinate system is moved while A and B stay exactly where they are. Which quantities do you think change?

3 · Turning the axes

Now for a twist. The origin stays put and the axes are turned through θ instead. What do you think happens to the distance d?

4 · Both at once

Next, combine both moves. The origin is shifted and the axes are turned together. Which quantities do you think read the same as they did in the original coordinate system?

5 · When is d zero?

One last experiment. Bring A and B together until they very nearly coincide, then separate them again. Which statement about d do you think is true?

6 · The Euclidean metric

Let’s bring it all together. The rule d² = Δx′² + Δy′² is the Euclidean metric: it turns coordinate differences into a length. Two properties make it the right rule — it’s invariant under any shift of the origin or turn of the axes, and it’s positive-definite, vanishing only for coincident points.

The Physics

Euclidean space & the metric · a complete revision sheet · Lecture 4, scalar invariants

The distance between two points isn't a number you read off the axes. It's the one number the axes can't touch.

01

What actually changes when you move the axes?

The same two points read in two Cartesian frames — geometry computed from the sim's defaults A = (−1, −1), B = (2, 3).
  • A and B never move. The sliders transform the coordinate system: Tx, Ty shift the origin, θ turns the axes. This is a passive transformation — the labels change, the plane does not.
  • Every number in the readout is a reading in whichever system is currently in use. That is why the sim renames them x′, y′ and O′ the moment you touch a control.
  • So the coordinates of A and B change, and nothing physical has happened. The question this lecture asks is: what did survive?
The map the sim applies, in its own convention: shift the origin by (Tx, Ty), then turn the axes through θ.
02

Which numbers survive which move?

  • Shift the origin: every coordinate moves by the same amount, so the shift cancels in Δx′ = x′B − x′A. A separation does not know where you put the origin.
  • Turn the axes: Δx′ and Δy′ mix into each other. They are the components of a vector, and components are frame-dependent by construction.
  • Only d comes through both. A quantity that survives every one of these transformations is what the course calls a scalar.
Apply the full map to a separation and Tx, Ty are simply absent — only the rotation acts on Δx, Δy.

03

How do you get a length out of coordinates?

The Euclidean metric δij: the recipe that turns a pair of components into one length.
Tx Ty θ

These are the sim's own Tx, Ty and θ sliders — drag here and the plane follows. Drag A or B in the sim and reopen this sheet to pick up the new points.

  • Run θ through a full 360° and the d readout never moves a digit. That is the whole claim of this lecture, live on screen.
  • The sum of squares is positive-definite: d is never negative, and d = 0 only when both differences vanish — that is, only when A and B are the same point.
04

Why does the cancellation work — and when does it fail?

The sim's own proof line: cos²θ + sin²θ = 1 is the entire mechanism. Nothing about where A and B sit enters it.
  • Turning the axes is an orthogonal change of basis. It is built to preserve sums of squares — so the metric survives by construction, not by luck.
  • δij is not a scalar. It survives translations, rotations and parity; go from Cartesian to polar and it changes. "Unchanged under the transformations you happen to be using" is not the same as invariant.
  • Where the recipe fails: take the points (r, φ) = (1, 0°) and (1, 90°). The true distance is d² = 1² + 1² = 2, but squaring and adding the polar differences gives Δr² + Δφ² = 0 + (π/2)² = 2.47. Polar components need a different metric.
  • The rule the course is building towards: things we can observe must not depend on our choice of coordinates, so the laws of physics are written in scalars. The distance d is the first one you meet.
Cartesian → Cartesian is the special class of maps that leaves the metric itself alone.
05

Where does this take you?

Lecture 4c's own example — one particle, read in two frames whose axes differ by 45°. Alice's frame carries the notes' tilde, on her axes and on Ṽ; the sim marks the same thing with a prime.
  • Speed is a scalar too. Bob writes V = (v/√2)(1, 1); Alice, whose axes are turned, writes Ṽ = (v, 0). Different components, identical δijViVj = v². Both see the particle moving at speed v.
  • Lecture 4(d) runs the same check on a distance. Alice puts P at her origin and Q on her own x axis at (√2, 0). In the sim: A = (1, 0), B = (0, 1), d = √2 = 1.41. Shift the origin onto A (Tx = 1, Ty = 0) and turn the axes θ = 135°: A′ = (0, 0), B′ = (1.41, 0), and d still reads 1.41.
  • The same machine produces every observable you will meet: pick a metric, contract the components with it, and what comes out is what all observers agree on.
  • Lecture 5 keeps the machine and swaps the metric — time joins the sum with the opposite sign, and the invariant is no longer a distance but a spacetime interval.
The course's sign convention: ΔS² < 0 timelike, = 0 lightlike, > 0 spacelike. Its sign, not its size, decides what can reach what.
06

The traps they'll set for you

  • "The coordinates changed, so the distance must change too." No — the changes in Δx′ and Δy′ cancel in the sum of squares. Every coordinate on screen can churn while d holds at 5.00.
  • "Δx and Δy are invariant." Only under a shift of the origin. Turn the axes and they mix: they are vector components, not scalars.
  • "Turning the axes turns the points." Nothing on the plane moves. This is a passive transformation — axes turn, A and B stay exactly where they were.
  • "The metric is invariant, so it's a scalar." δij survives Cartesian → Cartesian only; a scalar must survive any coordinate change.
07

Ninety-second self-test

You drag A onto B. What does the metric give — and can d ever come out negative?

d² = Δx′² + Δy′² is a sum of squares: it is never negative, and it reaches 0 exactly when both differences vanish, i.e. when the two points coincide. That property is called positive-definiteness, and it holds in every frame because d does.

Leave A and B at their defaults and set θ = 53°. What do Δx′ and Δy′ read, and what does d read?

Δx′ = 5.00 and Δy′ = 0.01: the x′-axis lies almost along AB, so nearly the whole separation sits in one component. The exact zero is at θ = atan(4/3) = 53.13°, between two slider steps, where 5² + 0² = 3² + 4² = 25. d still reads 5.00. The legs are frame-dependent; their sum of squares is not.

Defaults for A and B; set Tx = 3, Ty = −2, θ = 0. Predict A′, B′, Δx′, Δy′ and d before you look.

A′ = (−4, 1) and B′ = (−1, 5): every coordinate moved. But Δx′ = 3.00, Δy′ = 4.00 and d = 5.00, exactly as before, because the shift cancels in every difference. Only turning the axes can change the legs.
