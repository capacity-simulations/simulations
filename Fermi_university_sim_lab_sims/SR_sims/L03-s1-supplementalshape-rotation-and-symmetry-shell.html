Guided Inquiry

1 · Five rigid shapes, one angle

Let’s get to know the panels. Each one holds a rigid shape, turned about its own centre through the same angle θ. The dashed outline records where that shape sat at θ = 0°, and the white dot marks one point on the shape, so you can always tell which way it’s facing.

2 · What a turn changes

First, something simple. A rigid shape is turned about its centre through some angle. Which of these do you think is changed by the turn?

3 · The triangle at θ = 120°

Now look closely at the triangle. At θ = 120° its outline lies exactly on its dashed θ = 0° outline. So where do you think its white dot is?

Here’s a handy cue: a shape pulses gold whenever θ is one of its symmetry angles — the moment it sits back on its dashed outline — and that angle is added to the list beneath its panel.

4 · Counting the angles

Time to generalise. Including θ = 0°, how many angles in one full turn do you think bring a regular n-gon back onto its own outline?

5 · Where the marked point can go

Now follow just one point. As θ is swept continuously from 0° to 360°, what path do you think the white dot traces out?

6 · From rotations to boosts

Let’s name what you counted earlier. That count is the shape’s order of rotational symmetry — 3, 4, 5 and 6 here. And every angle maps a circle onto itself, so its symmetry is continuous rather than a list.

Here’s where it gets exciting: a Lorentz boost is the spacetime version of this. It changes an event’s t and x while leaving t² − x² fixed — and that minus sign is why a boosted event moves along a hyperbola, not a circle.

θ is sweeping now, so watch each symmetry angle get recorded under its shape.

The Physics

Rotations & symmetry · a complete revision sheet · Lecture 3, Coordinate Transformation

A symmetry isn't a transformation that does nothing. It's one that moves every point and still leaves the object looking untouched.

01

What does a turn actually change?

The triangle at θ = 40°, drawn from the sim's own vertex table. The dashed outline is θ = 0°; P is the white reference dot.
  • A rigid turn carries the whole shape at once, so every side length, every corner angle and the area survive it.
  • What does change is the coordinates of each point — where the shape sits, not what it is.
  • Because the turn preserves distance from the centre, the marked point can only move on the circle of its own radius r. The invariant already decides the path.
The sim's rotation, exactly as its formal strip writes it — and the matrix it is.
Distance is a scalar: both descriptions give the same d. Lecture 4 says the same thing by saying the metric δij is untouched.
02

When is a turn a symmetry?

A regular n-gon repeats every 360°/n — the sim's own symmetry-angle formula.
  • A symmetry angle is one where the shape lands back on its own dashed θ = 0° outline. The sim marks the moment with a gold pulse and adds the angle to the list under that panel.
  • At θ = 120° the triangle is indistinguishable from where it started — yet the white dot sits on a different corner. Every point moved; the object did not.
  • Counting starts at k = 0: doing nothing is a symmetry too — the identity. So the list is 3 angles for the triangle, 4 for the square, 5 for the pentagon, 6 for the hexagon.
03

How many angles does a shape answer to?

The order is the length of that list — and the ±1° window is the sim's own match test.
θ

This is the sim's real rotation slider — drag here and all five panels turn.

  • Twice as many corners → half the step, twice as many symmetry angles. Order and step are reciprocal.
  • The circle breaks the list: as n → ∞ the step goes to zero, so every angle works. Its panel reads "Every angle" and its order is ∞ — a continuous symmetry, not a long discrete one.
04

Am I turning the shape, or turning the axes?

  • This sim runs an active rotation: the axes are nailed down and the object moves. That is the version you can see.
  • Lecture 3 does the opposite — a passive rotation. P never moves. Bob's axes are replaced by Alice's, turned by θ, and only the components change.
  • Same matrix, opposite sign: Rpassive(θ) = Ractive(−θ), which for a rotation is just the transpose. Mix them up and Alice's components come out turned the wrong way.
  • Both leave d alone, which is exactly why the sim's lesson transfers to the notes unchanged.

05

Why is a shape sim in a relativity course?

  • Rotations are the prototype symmetry transformation: a change of description that leaves the invariant alone. Special relativity is the same move on a different invariant.
  • The invariant chooses the orbit. Euclid's x² + y² is positive for every direction, so the orbit closes into a circle — which is why the marked point comes home every 360°.
  • Spacetime's invariant is s² = −c²t² + x². The minus sign opens the circle into a hyperbola: a boost slides an event along one branch and never brings it back.
  • Put γ = cosh ζ and v/c = tanh ζ and the boost is a rotation, with cos → cosh and sin → sinh. There is no "full turn" for a boost: ζ runs to infinity as v → c.
One sign is the whole difference between a shape that repeats and an event that never returns.
Lecture 10's boost as a spacetime rotation, with rapidity ζ playing the part of θ.
06

The traps they'll set for you

  • "At a symmetry angle nothing has happened." Everything happened — every corner moved one place around. The shape is unchanged; its coordinates are not. That gap is the definition of a symmetry.
  • "All the coordinates changed, so the shape changed." Coordinates are a description. Lengths, angles and area are built from the invariant d, and a rotation leaves d alone.
  • "θ = 0° doesn't count, so an n-gon has n − 1 symmetry angles." The identity is a symmetry. Including k = 0 the count is exactly n.
  • "The lecture's R(θ) is the matrix this sim uses." The notes turn the axes, the sim turns the object: Rpassive(θ) = Ractive(−θ). Same angle, transposed matrix.
07

Ninety-second self-test

At θ = 0° nothing has been turned. Is that a symmetry?

Yes — it is the identity, the k = 0 entry of θk = k·360°/n. That is why a regular n-gon has n symmetry angles and not n − 1, and why every list in the sim starts at 0°.

Double the number of corners and halve the angle you turn by. Does a matching shape still match?

Yes. The step halves too — 360°/2n instead of 360°/n — so k = θ/step is unchanged. The match depends only on that ratio, never on θ alone.

Bob turns the triangle by +40°; Alice turns her axes by +40°. Do they write the same coordinates for the marked point?

No — opposite ones. Bob (active) gets R(+40°)·P, Alice (passive) gets R(−40°)·P. They do agree on r = √(x² + y²), because that is the scalar neither transformation can touch.
