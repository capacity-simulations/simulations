The straight (inertial) worldline between two events maximizes the proper time elapsed — opposite to Euclidean intuition about the "shortest path."

Guided Inquiry

1 · Two travellers, one reunion

Two travellers leave together at E₁ and meet again at E₂. A goes straight; B swings out through space and returns. Whose own clock — whose proper time τ — reads more at the reunion?

The τ totals are live now: read them on the diagram and beside each traveller's name.

2 · B's point of view

B could insist that it was A who moved away and came back. Between two inertial observers, time dilation is symmetric. So why do these two clocks disagree at all?

On the diagram, A runs from E₁ to E₂ as one unbroken segment; B turns at its waypoint.

3 · One segment at a time

B's journey is two straight segments, each with its own Δτ. Push B's turning point further out in space, leaving E₁ and E₂ where they are. What happens to those two Δτ values?

Select B in the Worldline controls, then drag its amber turning point sideways: each Δτ falls, and the total τ_B with them.

4 · A second observer

A second observer glides past at speed v and describes the same two journeys, redrawing every worldline. Which numbers does that observer get the same as we do?

Switch the Frame to S′ and slide v/c: the Δτ column holds while Δt′ moves.

5 · Maximal ageing

Between two timelike-separated events the straight, inertial worldline records the greatest proper time; every excursion through space subtracts from it. That is the twin paradox — B's clock reads less because B's worldline is bent — and τ, unlike coordinate time, is the same in every frame.

The Physics

Worldline length & proper time · a complete revision sheet · Lecture 9

Proper time isn't a coordinate you read off the grid. It's the length of your own path through spacetime — and in this geometry the straight path is the longest one.

01

Why do two clocks disagree when they meet again?

The sim's opening state, drawn from its own defaults: E₁ = (0, 0), E₂ = (0, 5), B's turn at (1.5, 2.5).
  • Both travellers leave E₁ and arrive at E₂ — the same two events, and every frame agrees they happened.
  • What each one carries is a clock, and a clock reads the proper time τ along its own worldline. τ is an arc length, not a difference of coordinates.
  • So two worldlines joining one pair of events can record different totals. At the sim's defaults τA = 5.00 against τB = 4.00.
  • Nothing about the endpoints decides it. The path does.
One timelike segment, in the sim's units with c = 1.
02

Why does a detour cost time?

The sim's signature: (+,−), so the timelike side is the positive one.
  • The interval subtracts the spatial step. Widen Δx at fixed Δ(ct) and Δτ falls — the exact reverse of a Euclidean detour.
  • Each of B's legs runs at v = 0.60c in frame S, so γ = 1.25 and Δτ = Δ(ct)/γ = 2.50/1.25 = 2.00.
  • Push a leg onto the light cone (|Δx| → Δ(ct)) and Δτ → 0: a light ray carries no proper time at all.
  • The sim will not let you drag past that — every segment is kept timelike, so every Δτ stays real.
One of B's legs: 2.50 up, 1.50 across, 2.00 on the carried clock. Longer on the page, shorter on the clock.
Lecture 9's form of the same statement — the moving clock runs slow by exactly γ.
Your lecture notes write the interval with the opposite overall sign. Same Δτ, same physics — only the bookkeeping flips.
03

How much time does the bend actually cost?

A sum over legs — and it can never beat the single straight segment between the same two events.
B's turn, x v/c

These drive the sim itself: the turn slider moves B's amber waypoint, v/c is the sidebar's own slider. The sim clamps the turn at the light cone, so the slider stops where Δτ would go imaginary.

  • Slide the turn back to x = 0 and B's worldline becomes A's — same path, same reading. Every unit of excursion is paid for.
04

Which numbers survive a change of observer?

The sim's own boost, c = 1. Δt′ is a difference of coordinates; Δτ is not.
  • τ is an invariant of the worldline. Switch the Frame to S′ and sweep v/c: the Δτ column of the per-segment table does not move.
  • Δt′ does move. At v/c = 0.50, B's legs take Δt′ = 2.02 and 3.75 in place of 2.50 and 2.50 — and still read Δτ = 2.00 and 2.00.
  • Because E₁ and E₂ sit at the same x in the default layout, A and B span the same coordinate time in any one frame, yet read different clocks. Drag E₂ sideways and even that coincidence goes.
  • The corner is not a matter of opinion either: every frame sees B's bend, so no boost can make the two travellers interchangeable.
05

Where does nature run this?

Four worldlines over the same Δ(ct) = 5 — the closer to the light cone, the less the carried clock records.
  • Cosmic-ray muons. A muon's own clock gives it 2.2 μs. At v = 0.999c, γ = 22.4, so the ground frame gets 49 μs of it — about 14.7 km of atmosphere, which is why they reach sea level at all.
  • Flown atomic clocks (Hafele–Keating, 1971). Clocks taken around the world came back tens to hundreds of nanoseconds off the clock that stayed. Part of that split is this chapter's kinematic term; the rest is gravitational and belongs to general relativity.
  • GPS. Satellite clocks lose ≈ 7 μs a day to their motion and gain ≈ 45 μs a day to weaker gravity — a net +38 μs/day the system corrects for. Left alone, fixes would drift by kilometres within a day.
  • The twin paradox is this chapter. No new physics is needed: one twin's worldline is bent, and the bent one reads less.
06

The traps they'll set for you

  • "The traveller who took the longer path through space must have aged more." Spacetime subtracts the spatial step, so a detour costs time: A reads 5.00, B reads 4.00.
  • "Time dilation is symmetric, so the two effects cancel." Symmetric only while both fly inertially. B changes frames at the corner and A never does — and the reunion is a single event with a single answer.
  • "B ages less because B was the one moving." Motion is relative; the bend is not. What separates them is straight versus bent, not rest versus motion.
  • "τ depends on which frame you compute it in." It cannot — τ is what a carried clock physically reads. Only the coordinate time Δt′ changes under a boost.
07

Ninety-second self-test

Drag B's turning point to x = 0. What does B's clock read?

5.00 — the same as A. With no spatial step, Δτ = Δ(ct) on both legs and B's worldline is A's. Zero excursion costs zero time.

Keep each of B's legs at Δ(ct) = 2.50 but halve the spatial step to Δx = 0.75. What is each Δτ, and τB?

Δτ = √(2.50² − 0.75²) = √5.6875 ≈ 2.39, so τB ≈ 4.77. Closer to A's 5.00 — but a bent worldline can never reach it.

Boost to S′ at v/c = 0.50. Which changes: τB = 4.00, or a leg's Δt′ = 2.02?

Δt′. It is a coordinate difference, Δt′ = γ(Δ(ct) − βΔx), so it depends on the observer. τ is built from Δ(ct)² − Δx², which the boost leaves alone — it is the same 4.00 in every frame.
