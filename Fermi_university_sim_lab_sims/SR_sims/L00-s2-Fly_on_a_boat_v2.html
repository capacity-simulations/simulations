Guided Inquiry

1 · A fly in a moving cabin

Let’s meet everyone. A ship glides along the coast at constant speed. Inside its closed cabin, Alice & Bella release a fly from rest at height h above the floor, while on the beach Galileo watches the same fly through the window.

Gravity g pulls the fly downward, and that’s all — nothing pushes it horizontally. Keep that in mind.

2 · Where does it land?

Here’s our first prediction. The fly is released from rest, high above the cabin floor, and the ship keeps gliding at the same speed the whole time. Where on the floor do you think it touches down?

3 · A faster ship

Now we’ll speed things up. The ship is re-set to glide at 9 m/s instead of 4, still perfectly steady, and Alice and Bella repeat the same drop. What do you think they measure now?

4 · The same fall from the beach

Time to step off the ship. Galileo watches that same drop from the beach, through the cabin window. What path do you think he sees the fly follow through the air?

5 · What both observers share

So Alice records a straight vertical drop, and Galileo records a forward-leaning parabola — one fly, one fall. What do you think — which measurement do the two of them agree on?

6 · Galilean relativity

Let’s give these ideas their names. The rule connecting the two descriptions is Galilean velocity addition: a velocity measured from the beach equals the velocity measured in the cabin plus the ship’s velocity. And the fact that every inertial observer gets a different path but the same physics is the equivalence of inertial frames.

Here’s one to wonder about: replace the fly with a pulse of light, and ask whether the beach observer measures c plus the ship’s speed. That’s where special relativity begins.

The Physics

Lecture 0 · Prelude — Equivalence · one fall, two equivalent observers

Galileo's parabola isn't a different fall from Alice & Bella's straight drop. It's the same fall plus the ship — and nothing inside the cabin can tell you which of the two observers is the moving one.

01

Why doesn't the cabin slide out from under the fly?

One release, two observers — both paths computed from the sim's defaults: vboat = 4.0 m/s, h = 2.5 m, g = 9.8 m/s².
  • The fly leaves the hand already carrying the ship's horizontal velocity. Letting go adds nothing horizontal and takes nothing away.
  • Gravity points straight down, so no horizontal force ever acts. Whatever vx was at release, it stays that for the whole fall.
  • In the cabin frame that inherited vx is zero — the fly lands directly below. In the beach frame it is vboat, so the fly travels downrange while it falls.
Cabin frame (Alice & Bella) — xc never changes; only y does.
Beach frame (Galileo) — the identical vertical fall, with a constant horizontal drift added.
02

What actually changes when the observer changes?

The Galilean transformation between the two frames. Lecture 6's box: time is absolute, space is shifted by motion.
  • Exactly one coordinate moves: x is shifted by vboat t. The height y and the time t are copied across untouched.
  • The shift is linear in t, so a vertical line in the cabin frame becomes a parabola in the beach frame. The bend comes from the coordinates, not from a force.
  • Differentiate the shift once and you get the sim's velocity rule — the course writes it ũ = u − V, with V = vboat the cabin's velocity relative to the beach.
  • Set vboat = 0 and the transformation is the identity: the two observers then agree on every single number.
Galilean velocity addition, in the sim's labels and in the lecture's notation.
The beach-frame path at t = T/4, T/2, 3T/4 — the amber arrows are identical by construction, the pink ones grow as −gt.
03

How long is the fall, and how far does the ship carry it?

The fall time is shared; only the landing coordinate is frame-dependent.
vboat h g

These are the sim's real sliders — drag here and the boat follows.

  • vboat is nowhere in T. Only h and g set the fall time, which is why the 9 m/s run measures exactly what the 4 m/s run measured.
  • T grows as √h, not h: double the release height and the fall takes about 1.41× longer, not twice as long.
04

If the two paths disagree, which observer is right?

The accelerations agree, so Newton's law reads the same for Alice & Bella and for Galileo.
  • Both are right. The lecture's word is equivalent, not identical: different numbers, the same physics, neither observer privileged.
  • x and vx are labels — they depend on who is writing them down. T, vy(t), the acceleration and the landing event itself are physics — both observers report them identically.
  • Because F = ma survives the transformation, no experiment inside the closed cabin can reveal the ship's steady speed. That is the equivalence of inertial frames.
  • What is not negotiable here is the Galilean assumption underneath it all: tc = tb, one absolute time for everyone. Lecture 6 marks it as an assumption for a reason.
05

Where does nature run this — and where does it stop working?

  • Drop a coin on a cruising airliner (≈ 250 m/s) and in the ground frame it travels about 179 m during the same 0.71 s fall — and still lands at your feet.
  • The Earth carries every laboratory around the Sun at 29.8 km/s: roughly 21 km per 0.71 s drop. No dropping experiment has ever noticed.
  • This is Galileo's own 1632 argument about the ship's cabin, with Alice & Bella inside and Galileo on the beach.
  • Now release a pulse of light forward instead of a fly. The same addition would hand Galileo c + vboat. Lecture 0 leaves that open on purpose — Lecture 7 is where nature refuses.
Carried distance in the ground frame for one default 0.71 s fall — and the question that starts the rest of the course.
06

The traps they'll set for you

  • "Galileo sees the fly fall straight down, or drift backward." Neither — the fly keeps the ship's horizontal velocity, so from the beach the path leans forward on a parabola.
  • "A faster ship makes the fall take longer." T = √(2h/g) contains no vboat. At 9 m/s the cabin measurement is identical to the 4 m/s one.
  • "Something must push the fly forward to bend the path." No horizontal force exists in either frame. The forward motion is inherited at release; gravity alone does the bending.
  • "One of them is really moving and the other is really at rest." Only the relative velocity is physical. Nothing inside the closed cabin distinguishes the two.
07

Ninety-second self-test

Set the boat speed to 0. What do the two observers measure now?

Exactly the same thing. The transformation xc = xb − vboat t becomes the identity, so both record a straight vertical drop, landing at x = 0 after T = √(2h/g). The two frames have collapsed into one.

Double the release height h and leave g alone. What happens to T, and to the distance Galileo sees the fly travel?

Both grow by √2 ≈ 1.41, not by 2. T = √(2h/g), and the beach-frame carry is xb(T) = vboat T, so it inherits the same √h scaling. At the defaults: T goes 0.714 s → 1.010 s and the carry 2.86 m → 4.04 m.

Alice & Bella report vx = 0; Galileo reports vx = vboat. Which one has made a mistake?

Neither. vx is frame-dependent, and the two readings are related by ũ = u − V. What both observers agree on is vy(t) = −gt, the fall time T, the acceleration −g, and the fact that the fly hits the cabin floor. They are equivalent observers, not identical ones.
