About This Simulation

A 3D rendering of the classic light-clock thought experiment: a single photon bounces between two mirrors mounted inside a moving train, and one physical scene is watched from two reference frames — switch between them with the toggle in the sidebar.

Train frame — the camera rides inside the glass compartment. The photon simply bounces straight up and down between the mirrors while the night landscape streams past the windows.

Platform frame — the camera stands still on the station platform. The train (visibly length-contracted by 1/γ) rushes past, and the very same photon must now chase the moving mirrors, tracing a longer diagonal zigzag path.

Rest-frame tick: TA = 2h / c · Platform-frame tick: TB = 2hγ / c = γ · TA · γ = 1 / √(1 − β²) , θ = arcsin β.

Because the speed of light c is the same in both frames, the longer diagonal path must take longer — so the moving clock ticks slowly. The digital readouts and the graph in the sidebar show the train clock tA = tB/γ falling behind in real time.

Adjust β = v/c and the mirror separation h with the sliders. The run resets automatically when the train leaves the station scene.

Guided Inquiry

1 · The light clock

Here’s our clock. A single photon bounces between two mirrors a height h apart inside a train moving at speed v, and one round trip is one tick. Observer A rides along with the mirrors, observer B stands on the platform, and light travels at c for both of them.

2 · Timing one tick

Let’s start with the obvious question. A and B time the same round trip of the same photon. What do you think — who records the longer time for it?

Now switch to the platform frame and follow one bounce with your eye.

3 · Where the difference comes from

So from the platform, B’s reading runs ahead of A’s. But why? What do you think produces that difference?

Follow one bounce from the platform and you’ll see it: the photon meets the mirror further down the track than it left.

4 · Raising the speed

Now let’s turn up the speed. At β = 0.10 one tick stretches by only 0.5 %. The speed slider goes all the way to β = 0.95. Make a guess — by roughly what factor does a tick stretch there?

Try it: drag β from 0.10 up to 0.95 and watch γ and the two clock readings.

5 · A friend says…

"A's clock runs slow in B's frame, so A's is the truly slow one."

Your friend sounds pretty sure. So let’s ride with A instead and look back at B’s clock. What do you think A finds?

One thing to bear in mind: the panel shows one comparison only, A’s clock against the platform’s synchronised clocks. This scene doesn’t draw A’s own row of clocks.

6 · Proper time

Let’s give that shorter reading its name. It’s the proper time Δτ — the time read by the one clock present at both events — and every other inertial frame records Δt = γ·Δτ.

And here’s a teaser for later: mutual time dilation between two inertial frames is symmetric, but the twin paradox isn’t — and only because one twin accelerates.

The Physics

Time dilation · a complete revision sheet · light clock on a train

A moving clock is not running wrong. It is the same photon crossing a longer path at the same speed c — so the platform has to spend more of its own time on one tick.

01

Why does one tick take longer from the platform?

One photon, one pair of mirror hits, two cameras — both paths computed from the sim's defaults β = 0.50, h = 1.00.
  • One tick is defined by two events: the photon leaves the bottom mirror and the photon returns to it. Every frame times those same two events.
  • In the train frame the mirrors are at rest, so the photon goes straight up and back down: a path of length 2h.
  • In the platform frame the mirrors slide along the track while the light is in flight, so the very same photon traces a zigzag — a longer path.
  • Nothing was done to the clock. The speed of light refused to change, so the time had to.
The postulate that forces the whole result: c is not measured differently by the rider and the platform.
Train frame A: the clock sits at both events (ΔxA = 0), so tA is the proper time Δτ.
02

Which clock reads the shorter time?

Platform frame B, half a trip: the light path is the hypotenuse, the mirror gap and the train's travel are the legs.
The right angle is the whole argument: the hypotenuse cannot be shorter than the vertical leg, so tB ≥ tA.
  • The shorter reading always belongs to the frame where the clock does not move — here the train, observer A. That reading is the proper time Δτ.
  • Every other inertial frame has to cover extra horizontal distance at the same c, so it records more time for the same two events.
  • The tilt is the sim's θ (beam from vertical) readout: sin θ = β. At β = 0.50 the beam leans 30.0°; at β = 0 it is vertical and both clocks agree.
  • Note what is not claimed: nobody's clock malfunctions, and no signal-travel time is involved. Each frame reads the clock standing beside the event.
03

How much slower, exactly?

Solve the triangle for tB: the mirror gap h cancels out and only β survives.
β h

These are the sim's real sliders — drag here and the train follows.

  • γ depends on β alone. Moving h changes how long a tick is, never the ratio between the two clocks.
  • The growth is not linear: γ = 1.005 at β = 0.10 but 3.20 at β = 0.95, the slider's maximum. Negligible, then enormous — which is why nobody noticed for two centuries.
04

If each frame calls the other slow, who is right?

Lecture 11's Lorentz transform. Put the clock at rest in the primed frame (x′ = 0) and Δt = γΔt′ drops out — the primed frame here is the train, so Δt′ = tA and Δt = tB.
  • Both are right. Ride with A and B's clock is the slow one, by the same γ. Between two inertial frames time dilation is strictly reciprocal.
  • There is no contradiction because they are not comparing the same pairs of events. "At the same time" is frame-dependent, and each frame uses its own.
  • The course's quick check: Δt = 10 s of platform time at v = 0.6c gives Δt′ = Δt/γ = 8 s on the moving clock.
  • What every frame agrees on is the interval, not the time. For a timelike pair, −s² = c²Δτ², and Δτ is the reading of the one clock present at both events.
Sign convention as in lectures 8, 9 and 11: s² = −c²Δt² + Δx². The right-hand form is the sim's own formal-strip line — the same statement, written for Δτ.
05

Where does nature run this?

Cosmic-ray muons at β = 0.998, γ = 15.8: one journey, two honest accounts — a stretched lifetime on the ground, a contracted atmosphere on board.
  • Muons. Proper lifetime τ = 2.2 μs, so without dilation they would travel βcτ = 0.66 km and die 14 km up. The ground frame measures γτ = 35 μs and a mean flight of 10.4 km, so a large fraction arrives.
  • The muon's own account uses length, not time. Its clock is fine; the 15 km of air is contracted to 0.95 km, crossed in 3.2 μs — about 1.4 lifetimes. Same survival, different bookkeeping.
  • GPS. Satellites move at 3.9 km/s, so β ≈ 1.3×10⁻⁵ and γ − 1 ≈ β²/2: their clocks lose about 7.2 μs per day to motion alone. (Gravity pushes the other way and wins; the net correction is +38 μs/day.)
  • The sim shows the length side too: the carriage is drawn contracted and the L/L₀ readout prints 1/γ — 0.866 at β = 0.50.
Length needs both ends read at one instant, and frames disagree about which instant that is — that is where contraction comes from.
06

The traps they'll set for you

  • "It's just signal delay — light takes time to reach the platform observer's eye." No. Each frame reads the clock sitting right beside the event, so travel time is already subtracted. The difference survives.
  • "The train's clock mechanism runs down." Nothing is wrong with the clock. Any clock riding the train — atomic, mechanical or biological — keeps step with the photon, because what changed is the path light covers.
  • "One of them must really be the slow one." Each inertial frame measures the other's clock slow by the same γ. Picking a winner means picking a privileged frame, which is exactly what relativity denies.
  • "So the twin paradox is symmetric too." It is not. The travelling twin changes frames at the turnaround; that acceleration breaks the symmetry. Mutual dilation between two inertial frames never does.
07

Ninety-second self-test

Set β = 0. What do the two clocks and the beam do?

γ = 1, so tB = tA and the two readings stay locked together. The zigzag collapses onto the vertical bounce and θ = arcsin 0 = 0°; L/L₀ = 1. Time dilation is not switched off by a rule — it just has nothing to do.

Double h and drop β from 0.80 to 0.40. What happens to one platform tick?

tB = 2hγ/c, so it grows by 2 × (1.091 / 1.667) ≈ 1.31 — about 31% longer, even though the dilation factor fell from 1.67 to 1.09. Tick length and dilation are different quantities: h sets the first, β alone sets the second.

Why is tA, and not tB, the proper time of this tick?

Because the train clock is present at both events — emission and return happen at the same place in frame A, ΔxA = 0. Proper time is defined by that single-clock condition, and (cΔτ)² = (cΔt)² − Δx² then makes it the smallest reading any inertial frame can get.
