About: Time Dilation — Light Clock (3D)

About This Simulation

A 3D rendering of the classic light-clock thought experiment: a single photon bounces between two mirrors mounted inside a moving train, and one physical scene is watched from two reference frames — switch between them with the toggle in the sidebar.

Train frame — the camera rides inside the glass compartment. The photon simply bounces straight up and down between the mirrors while the night landscape streams past the windows.

Platform frame — the camera stands still on the station platform. The train (visibly length-contracted by 1/γ) rushes past, and the very same photon must now chase the moving mirrors, tracing a longer diagonal zigzag path.

Rest-frame tick: TA = 2h / c · Platform-frame tick: TB = 2hγ / c = γ · TA · γ = 1 / √(1 − β²) , θ = arcsin β.

Because the speed of light c is the same in both frames, the longer diagonal path must take longer — so the moving clock ticks slowly. The digital readouts and the graph in the sidebar show the train clock tA = tB/γ falling behind in real time.

Adjust β = v/c and the mirror separation h with the sliders. The run resets automatically when the train leaves the station scene.

Guided Inquiry

1 · The light clock

A single photon bounces between two mirrors a height h apart inside a train moving at speed v; one round trip is one tick. Observer A rides with the mirrors, observer B stands on the platform, and light travels at c for both.

2 · Timing one tick

A and B time the same round trip of the same photon. Who records the longer time for it?

Switch to the platform frame and follow one bounce.

3 · Where the difference comes from

From the platform, B's reading runs ahead of A's. What produces that difference?

Follow one bounce from the platform: the photon meets the mirror further down the track than it left.

4 · Raising the speed

At β = 0.10 one tick stretches by only 0.5 %. The speed slider reaches β = 0.95. By roughly what factor does a tick stretch there?

Drag β from 0.10 up to 0.95 and watch γ and the two clock readings.

5 · A friend says…

"A's clock runs slow in B's frame, so A's is the truly slow one."

Ride with A instead, and look at B's clock. What does A find?

The panel shows one comparison only: A's clock against the platform's synchronised clocks. This scene does not draw A's own row of clocks.

6 · Proper time

The shorter reading is the proper time Δτ — the time read by the one clock present at both events; every other inertial frame records Δt = γ·Δτ.

Mutual time dilation between two inertial frames is symmetric. The twin paradox is not, and only because one twin accelerates.
