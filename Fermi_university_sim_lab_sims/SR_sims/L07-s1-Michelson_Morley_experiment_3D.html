About: Michelson–Morley Interferometer (3D)

About This Simulation

This interactive 3D simulation reproduces the Michelson–Morley experiment (1887) as it was actually built: the optics are bolted to a massive stone slab that floats on liquid mercury in an annular cast-iron trough on a brick pier, so the whole apparatus can be rotated smoothly.

Light from the source is split by a half-silvered mirror at 45° (the beam splitter, BS). One beam passes through a compensating plate to mirror M₁; the other reflects to mirror M₂. The compensator is an identical, unsilvered copy of the splitter, so both beams traverse the same thickness of glass — without it, white-light fringes could not be seen. The returning beams recombine at BS and interfere in the telescope detector.

In the 19th century, physicists believed light traveled through a medium called the “luminiferous aether.” If so, the Earth’s motion through this aether would create a “wind” that changes the effective speed of light differently in each arm, producing a measurable fringe shift when the slab is rotated.

Aether Prediction mode shows the expected fringe shift using Galilean velocity addition — this prediction is wrong.

Actual (Relativity) mode shows the real result: no fringe shift at any angle. FitzGerald and Lorentz first explained this with an ad-hoc length contraction of the arm along the motion; Einstein’s Special Relativity (1905) explained it fundamentally — the speed of light is the same in all directions for every inertial observer.

β (v/c) is fixed at Earth’s real orbital speed (β ≈ 9.93×10⁻⁵). The effect is minuscule: with ~11 m arms the predicted round-trip difference is only a few hundred attoseconds, so the readout shows it in scientific notation / attoseconds (and it is exactly 0 in Actual mode — the null result). Guided Inquiry

1 · The interferometer

A single beam is split at a half-silvered mirror into two beams that travel equal lengths L down perpendicular arms, reflect off mirrors A and B, and recombine. If light were carried by a stationary ether, the Earth's orbital motion of about 30 km/s would blow that ether through the apparatus at speed v, giving β = v/c ≈ 1×10−4.

The table is held at θ = 0°, with arm A lying along the wind and arm B across it.

2 · Out and back along the wind

Light runs the length of arm A with the wind, reflects, and returns against it. Compared with the same round trip in still ether, how long does the trip take?

3 · Across the wind

Arm B points across the wind. Compared with the same round trip in still ether — and with arm A's trip along the wind — what does arm B take?

4 · Turning the table

The stone table floats on mercury, so it can be turned to any angle θ. At θ = 90° arm B lies along the wind and arm A across it. What does the ether model predict for A − B as θ runs from 0° to 90°?

Turn the table and follow the A − B difference.

5 · What the experiment found

In 1887 Michelson and Morley turned the table through full revolutions, day and night, across the seasons. Their apparatus could resolve about one fortieth of the predicted 0.4-fringe swing. What did they measure?

6 · The null result

With c the same in every direction for every inertial observer, both arms take exactly 2L/c whatever the angle, so A − B = 0 at every θ. That is Einstein's second postulate, the assumption special relativity is built on.

Turn the table through a full revolution: the difference stays zero.

The Physics

The Michelson–Morley experiment · a complete revision sheet · Lecture 7, Speed of Light

The null result isn't an experiment that failed. It's the measurement that forced Galilean velocity addition out of physics.

01

What was the ether wind supposed to do?

Lecture 7's starting point: under the Galilean transformation an observer moving at V through the medium should measure light at c − V. The speed of light should be relative.
  • If light is carried by a stationary ether, the Earth's orbital motion blows that ether through the apparatus as a wind of speed v ≈ 29.8 km/s. The sim fixes β ≡ v/c = 9.93×10−5 — the real value, not an exaggerated one.
  • The beam splitter BS (a half-silvered mirror at 45°) cuts one beam in two, sends the halves down perpendicular arms of equal length L to mirrors M1 and M2, and recombines them in the telescope.
  • A compensating plate in arm A — an unsilvered twin of the splitter — makes both halves cross the same thickness of glass. Without it there are no white-light fringes to read.
  • The instrument never measures a speed. It measures the difference of two round-trip times, converted into a position of the fringes.
The apparatus at the sim's default θ = 0°: arm A along the wind, arm B across it, both of length L = 11.0 m. β is 10−4 — far too small to draw.
02

Which arm loses more time?

Arm A, along the wind: out at c + v, back at c − v.
  • The two legs do not cancel. The crawl against the wind lasts far longer than the sprint with it is short, so the round trip beats 2L/c in neither direction — it is always longer.
Arm B, across the wind: aimed straight over it would be swept downstream, so it must be aimed upstream — only √(c² − v²) is left to carry it across.
  • So both arms are slowed. Arm B is slowed by half of arm A's excess, to first order — not by nothing. What the experiment hunts for is the leftover.
  • Lecture 7's plane analogy has the same ordering for the same reason: 400 km at 100 km/h in a 60 km/h wind takes 8 h in still air, 10 h across the wind, 12.5 h along it.
  • At the sim's defaults the excesses over 2L/c are 724 as (arm A) and 362 as (arm B), leaving A − B = 362 as — the number in the round-trip panel at θ = 0°.
Both excesses computed from the sim's own arm-time expression at L = 11.0 m, β = 9.93×10−5. The wind is drawn enormously exaggerated; at the true β the triangle would be indistinguishable from a straight line.
03

How many fringes should that move?

The leftover: what survives after both arms have been slowed.
One whole fringe per wavelength of optical path difference — the interferometer's exchange rate from time to position.
L λ

These are the sim's own Arm length L and Wavelength λ sliders — drag here and the apparatus follows.

  • A 362 as difference is 10−16 s — nothing can clock it. The fringes do it instead: the path difference is read as a displacement of the pattern, in units of λ.
  • Double L → double the shift. Halve λ → double it. β is not a slider — it is pinned at Earth's orbital value, so those two are the only levers you have.
04

Why does the table have to turn?

  • You cannot read the difference off directly. The two arms of a real apparatus are never exactly equal, and there is no "wind off" setting to compare against — the absolute fringe position tells you nothing.
  • Turning the slab swaps the arms' roles. An arm at angle ϕ to the wind takes t(ϕ); arm B always sits at ϕ + 90°.
One arm, any angle to the wind. Both arms of the sim are evaluated with this.
Because sin² and cos² swap, the difference runs as cos 2θ: full at 0°, zero at 45°, reversed at 90°.
  • Only the change under rotation is observable. That is why the sim's Δℓ and ΔN are referenced to θ = 0° and the fringe plot swings between 0 and −0.394 — not ±0.394.
  • The optics are bolted to a stone slab floating on mercury, so the whole thing turns smoothly without flexing and the fringes can be counted continuously as it goes.
05

What did 1887 actually measure?

  • With about 11 m of folded path and λ = 550 nm the predicted swing was roughly 0.4 of a fringe. Michelson and Morley could resolve about one fortieth of that.
  • They turned the slab through full revolutions, by day and by night, across the seasons. No shift, at any orientation. The fringes stayed put.
  • FitzGerald and Lorentz rescued the ether by postulating that the along-wind arm contracts by exactly √(1 − β²) — which reproduces the null result and explains nothing.
  • Einstein's reading is the sim's Actual (Relativity) mode: c is the same in every direction for every inertial observer, so both arms take exactly 2L/c — a proper time, since emission and return happen at the same place in the apparatus — whatever θ is.
  • The choice the course draws from it: the null result puts the principle of relativity against Galilean velocity addition and absolute time. Keep the first; give up the second.
The second postulate, in the form this experiment tests.
The dashed curve is the sim's own fringe plot at L = 11.0 m, λ = 550 nm. The blue band is drawn to scale at ±0.01 fringe — the prediction is 39× larger than the noise it had to hide in.
06

The traps they'll set for you

  • "Aether mode shows what happens." It shows what Galilean velocity addition predicts, and the sim marks it ⚠ because every number in it is wrong. Actual (Relativity) mode is the measurement.
  • "The out-and-back legs cancel." They don't. c − v costs more time than c + v saves, so any round trip through a wind is longer than the same trip in still ether.
  • "A sideways wind doesn't matter." It does. To cross straight over, the beam must be aimed upstream, leaving √(c² − v²) — arm B loses half of arm A's excess, not zero.
  • "The null result just means they weren't sensitive enough." The apparatus resolved about 1/40 of the predicted 0.4-fringe swing. The shift is absent, not hidden.
  • "The experiment measures A − B directly." Only the change under rotation is observable, because the arms are never exactly equal — hence Δℓ and ΔN referenced to θ = 0°.
07

Ninety-second self-test

Switch the ether wind off — set β = 0. What happens to t∥, t⊥ and ΔN?

Both collapse to 2L/c and ΔN = 0 at every θ — the aether prediction becomes the relativistic one. The wind, not the interferometer, was carrying the entire effect.

Double the arm length L and double the wavelength λ. What happens to the predicted 0° → 90° swing?

Nothing: the swing is 2Lβ²/λ, so doubling both leaves 0.394 fringes. Drag both sliders in chapter 03 and watch the number hold — then halve λ alone and it doubles.

In Actual (Relativity) mode the difference is zero at every angle. In whose frame is c being measured?

In every inertial frame — including the one the apparatus is momentarily at rest in at each orientation. That is the second postulate. There is no preferred frame in which the arms are "really" unequal and something conspires to hide it.
