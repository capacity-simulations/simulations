The Physics

Solar system orbits · a complete revision sheet · ellipses & varying speed

An orbit isn't a circle run at constant speed. It's a fall that keeps missing the Sun — an ellipse, fast in close, slow far out.

01

What shape is an orbit — and where is the Sun?

Mercury's orbit, computed from the sim's own numbers (a = 0.387 AU, e = 0.206) — beside the circular myth.
  • Every orbit here is an ellipse with the Sun at one focus — Kepler's first law. The centre of the ellipse is empty.
  • e measures the offset, not the squash. Mercury's e = 0.206 pushes the Sun ae ≈ 0.08 AU off centre — 21% of the orbit's size — yet the curve is only ~2% wider than it is tall.
  • The sim's camera is tilted (about 50–55° from overhead), so every ring on screen, even Venus's e = 0.007, is drawn roughly 40% flatter than it is. Judge an orbit by where the Sun sits inside it, or drag upward on the view to look from overhead: the rings round out.
  • One force does all of it: gravity, pulling straight at the Sun, weakening as 1/r².
The only force in the sim. In AU and years, GM☉ = 4π² — chosen so Kepler's third law reads T² = a³.
The exact curve the sim draws; θ is measured from perihelion, and the Sun sits at the focus r = 0.
02

Where does a planet run fastest?

Kepler's second law — the Sun–planet line sweeps equal areas in equal times. Same statement: angular momentum is conserved.
  • Falling inward from aphelion, gravitational potential energy becomes kinetic — speed peaks at perihelion, the closest point.
  • Climbing back out pays it all back — slowest at aphelion. No energy is lost either way.
  • The two ends bookend the whole lap: rperivperi = rapovapo, so Mercury runs (1+e)/(1−e) ≈ 1.52× faster in close than far out.
Mercury at its true e = 0.206 (a = 0.387 AU), from Kepler's equation: each shaded sector is 7.3 days (T/12) of travel. Equal times sweep equal areas, so the short, fat sector near the Sun needs the higher speed.

04

Why doesn't the sim just draw ellipses?

Velocity Verlet — the symplectic stepper the sim actually runs, sub-stepped to h ≤ 2×10⁻⁴ yr so Mercury stays stable at fast time-rates.
  • The moving dots never look up an ellipse: the sim integrates Newton's law step by step from position and velocity — classical mechanics as prediction by differential equation.
  • Symplectic matters. Verlet respects the orbit's energy, so ellipses close on themselves lap after lap; a naive Euler step spirals outward.
  • In numbers, at the sim's step cap h = 2×10⁻⁴ yr from the same start: Euler grows Mercury's a by about 6% in one lap (0.387 → 0.412 AU) and by half in ten; Verlet keeps the energy within 7×10⁻⁶ of its start, lap after lap.
  • The drawn curve is the analytic ellipse; the dot is the integration. That they agree, orbit after orbit, is the check.
05

How do the eight planets compare?

Kepler's third law in the sim's units, and the same law rewritten as a speed.
  • T² = a³: Mercury laps in 0.24 yr; Neptune, at 30.1 AU, needs 165 yr.
  • v ∝ 1/√a: Earth orbits at 29.8 km/s; Neptune, 30× farther out, at ~5.4 — √30 ≈ 5.5× slower, exactly the sim's card-5 answer.
  • In the Full view the sim compresses the spacing between orbits for legibility — each orbit's shape, e, and Sun-at-focus placement stay exact.
All eight sim planets on log–log axes — Kepler's third law is the straight line of slope −½.
06

The traps they'll set for you

  • "Orbits are circles run at constant speed." The sim's dashed ghost runs exactly that — and real Mercury pulls ahead of it near the Sun and falls behind far out.
  • "The Sun sits at the centre." It sits at a focus; the centre is empty. For Mercury the offset is 21% of the orbit's size.
  • "Big e means an egg shape." At e = 0.206 the curve is within ~2% of a circle. The flattening you see in the sim is the tilted camera; the real signature of e is the off-centre Sun.
  • "Farther planets move faster — they have farther to go." Slower: v ∝ 1/√a. The lap is longer and the pace is slower, which is why T grows as a3/2.
07

Ninety-second self-test

Venus has e = 0.007. Select it and watch v (now). How much does its speed change over a lap, and why?

Barely: vis-viva gives 35.26 km/s at perihelion and 34.78 km/s at aphelion, a 1.4% swing. As e → 0, r = a everywhere and v = √(GM/a) is constant. A circle is the one orbit really run at constant speed.

Orbit A is 4× wider than orbit B. Compare their periods and speeds.

T² = a³ gives 43/2 = 8× the period; v ∝ 1/√a gives half the speed. Both are the same third law.

Mercury slows from 59 to 39 km/s between perihelion and aphelion. Where does the energy go?

Into gravitational potential energy. The total E depends only on a and is constant, so kinetic and potential energy just trade — the fall back in returns every bit.
