Guided Inquiry

1 · Watch the race

Let’s start with a race. Press 🚀 Launch (or just watch the opening shot): all three models fly together — vacuum, linear drag, and quad drag — as solid paths you can toggle on and off. Notice that with drag the descent is steeper than the ascent — an asymmetry the vacuum parabola never shows — and the range is shorter too.

2 · Predict the best angle

Now for the big question. With air drag on (b > 0), which launch angle θ do you think gives the longest shot on flat ground?

3 · Hunt the peak

Let’s test that against the data. Sweep the θ slider and watch Range, while Best so far keeps your record. Near the top the peak is flat, so bracket it: does 35° beat 45°? Does 40°?

Of those three, which range do you find is largest?

4 · Read it on the curve

Now you can see the whole picture. The Range vs angle plot is open: ● is your θ, and ★ is the computed optimum. At these defaults the ★ sits near 36° — clearly below the 45° reference line.

5 · What moves the ★

So what moves the ★? At stronger drag it sits even lower. Try raising b further (or raise m and match b) and watch it slide — the flight depends on b and m only through b/m, so b = 0.30, m = 2.0 kg flies identically to b = 0.15, m = 1.0 kg. Then set b = 0 and watch the ★ snap back to 45°. The "45° rule" is a vacuum-only theorem (here drag is linear, F = −b·v; real quadratic drag gives the same conclusions).

6 · A faster shot

One last experiment. Hold b and m fixed and raise the launch speed v₀ from 18 to 30 m/s. What do you think happens to the best angle?

The Physics

Projectile motion with air drag · a complete revision sheet · why 45° is wrong

Maximum range at 45° isn't a fact about projectiles. It's a fact about empty space — the moment air pushes back, the best angle drops.

01

Where does the asymmetry come from?

The sim's default launch, every path computed from the exact model it flies. The paired defaults (b = 0.30, c = 0.016) make the two drag arcs nearly coincide.
  • In vacuum only gravity acts: the arc is a parabola — time up equals time down, descent mirrors ascent.
  • Drag adds a force that always points against the velocity: −b·v (linear) or −c|v|v (quad). It bleeds speed for the whole flight.
  • Coming down the ball is slower, so the descent is steeper and covers less ground than the climb (18.5 m against 26.3 m), even though it takes longer (1.94 s against 1.62 s). Range shrinks from 85.7 m to about 44.9 m.
Vacuum: the symmetric parabola — the reference every drag flight is measured against.
Linear drag: τ = m/b is the velocity-decay time; vT = mg/b the terminal speed of a free fall.
02

Why does the best angle drop below 45°?

sin 2θ peaks at 2θ = 90°. This — and only this — is where the "45° rule" is a theorem.
  • Drag charges by the second: every moment aloft costs speed. Steep arcs hang longest, so they pay the most.
  • Flat-and-quick beats high-and-floaty: at the defaults the best whole degree is θ★ = 36°, reaching 46.6 m against 44.9 m at 45°.
  • The peak is flat — 35° and 40° differ by 0.3 m. The sim finds ★ by bisection on y(T) = 0, then scans every whole degree from 5° to 85° — no fitted curves.
  • Faster shots pay more: drag grows with speed, so raising v₀ pushes θ★ further down — about 39° at 18 m/s, 36° at 30 m/s.

04

Why can the ball never pass x = v₀ₓτ?

Horizontal motion with linear drag: exponential decay toward a hard limit.
x(t) at the sim's defaults (θ = 45°, v₀ = 29 m/s, b = 0.30 kg/s, m = 1 kg), from the closed form above: the ball lands at T = 3.56 s, x = 44.85 m, long before the 68.4 m wall. The dashed tail is where x(t) would head if the ground weren't there. The sim doesn't draw the wall; this plot does.
  • The horizontal velocity never reverses — it just decays with time constant τ = m/b. The whole horizontal budget is v₀ₓ·τ.
  • At the defaults that wall stands at 68.4 m: no launch angle carries this ball past it while b = 0.30 kg/s.
  • Set b = 0 and τ → ∞: the wall retreats to infinity and the vacuum theorem — 45°, symmetric parabola — returns exactly.
05

Which law does real air actually use?

Quadratic drag — no closed form; the sim integrates it with RK4 at dt = 2 ms. c → 0 recovers the vacuum solution exactly.
  • At ball-throwing speeds real air resistance is closer to quadratic: −c|v|v grows with the square of the speed. The linear law is the tractable stand-in that keeps a closed form.
  • What c = 0.016 kg/m is: c = ½ρCdA, so with air (ρ = 1.2 kg/m³) and a sphere's Cd ≈ 0.47 it is a ball about 13 cm in radius, roughly basketball-sized. The sim's m = 1 kg makes it a heavy one.
  • Each law has its own terminal speed: mg/b = 32.7 m/s (linear) and √(mg/c) = 24.8 m/s (quad) at the defaults.
  • Terminal speed is why raindrops arrive gently instead of like bullets: a 2 mm-wide drop tops out near 6.5 m/s. Free fall saturates, exactly as in the figure.
  • Both drag models agree on the headline: the trajectory is asymmetric, range is capped, and the best angle sits below 45° (★ near 36° linear, 40° quad, at the defaults).
Speed of a free fall from rest, computed for both drag laws at the sim's defaults.
06

The traps they'll set for you

  • "45° is always the best angle." Only in vacuum. R = v₀²sin2θ/g is a vacuum formula; with drag on, the sim's ★ sits near 36°.
  • "Throw higher to win the lost distance back." Hang time is exactly what drag taxes — every extra second aloft bleeds more speed. Flatter wins.
  • "Drag just scales the parabola down." No — it breaks the symmetry: the descent is steeper than the climb, and the apex sits past the horizontal midpoint (26.3 m of 44.9 m at the defaults).
  • "Mass doesn't matter — Galileo said so." With air it does: the flight depends on b/m, so at the same b a heavier ball flies closer to the vacuum arc.
07

Ninety-second self-test

Slide b to 0 with the range-vs-angle plot open. Where does the ★ go, and why?

To 45° exactly. With b = 0 the range is v₀²sin2θ/g, which peaks where 2θ = 90°. The "45° rule" is a vacuum-only theorem, and the sim's b = 0 limit reproduces it exactly.

Double b and double m together: m = 1.0 kg, b = 0.15 → m = 2.0 kg, b = 0.30. How does the flight change?

It doesn't. Linear drag enters only through b/m: τ = m/b and vT = mg/b are both unchanged. Check it at θ = 45°, v₀ = 29 m/s: m = 1.0 kg with b = 0.15 and m = 2.0 kg with b = 0.30 both read Range (linear drag) = 59.61 m.

With drag on, which takes longer — the climb to the apex or the fall back down?

The fall: 1.94 s down against 1.62 s up at the defaults. The ball descends slower (its speed is capped near vT), yet covers less ground — which is why the path looks steeper coming down. In vacuum the two halves are mirror images.
