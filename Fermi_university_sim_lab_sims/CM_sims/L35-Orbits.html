The Physics

Orbits · a complete revision sheet · the four conic sections

A planet's path isn't "just an ellipse". Circle, ellipse, parabola, hyperbola are one family of curves — and a single number, the eccentricity, walks through all four.

01

Why are there exactly four shapes?

The sim's four presets, computed from r = p/(1+e cos θ) at p = 1.5. Every member of the fixed-L family passes through the same two points (dots), where r = p.
  • One force does everything here: inverse-square gravity, always pulling the body straight at the Sun — no steering, no thrust.
  • Solve that force and only one family of paths comes out: the conic sections, with the Sun at a focus — off-centre, never at the middle.
  • The whole family is walked by one number, the eccentricity e. The sim's slider is that number.
  • Why four? The sign of ε (ch 02) gives three fates — bound, knife-edge, escape. Inside “bound” sits one special case, e = 0, where the focus lands on the centre: the circle. Three fates plus that one special case make four names.
The faint outline under the live trail is exactly this curve — the sim samples it point by point.
02

What decides bound or unbound?

One number for the whole flight — kinetic minus potential, per kilogram. The sim's ε readout is this.
  • ε < 0: the body cannot reach infinity — the well is deeper than its speed. The path closes: circle or ellipse.
  • ε = 0: a dead heat. The body barely escapes, arriving at infinity with zero speed — the parabola.
  • ε > 0: escape with speed to spare — the hyperbola. The sim tags the getaway "→ ∞".
The sim's classification: e and sign(ε) always agree, so the conic pill and the sign(ε) readout move together.
ε = GM(e²−1)/2p across the slider's whole range — one smooth curve, one sharp edge at e = 1.
03

The fixed-L family the slider drives: p never changes, so e alone sets both the shape and the energy.

Bound orbits only: ε alone sets the size, and the size alone sets the clock — the sim's period estimate is exactly this.
04

Why are they called conic sections?

  • The right-hand panel is not a metaphor. Slice a double cone with a plane and the cut's outline is a circle, ellipse, parabola or hyperbola — the same four curves gravity draws.
  • Tilt is everything: keep the plane shallower than the cone's side and the cut closes; hit exactly parallel and it opens into a parabola; go steeper and the plane meets both nappes — the hyperbola's two branches.
  • The slider tilts the cutting plane as you drag e; drag the cone panel itself to rotate the view and check the cut really closes — or really doesn't.
  • How much tilt buys which e? For a real cone, e = sin β / sin βgen (β measured from the base). The panel's cone has βgen ≈ 54°, so the e = 0.6 ellipse needs sin β = 0.6 × sin 54° → β ≈ 29°. The panel tilts its plane in step with the slider (β = 54° × e, 33° at e = 0.6) — exact at the two points that matter, e = 0 (level cut) and e = 1 (parallel to the side); a guide in between.
β is the tilt of the cutting plane, βgen the slope of the cone's side — the panel colours the live cut by this very test.
05

Which conics does the sky actually use?

  • Planets: near-circles. Earth rides e = 0.017; Mercury, the "eccentric" one, only 0.206.
  • Comet Halley: e = 0.967 — bound, but barely (ε just below 0). It crawls at aphelion beyond Neptune, then returns every 76 years.
  • Interstellar visitors: 'Oumuamua came through on e ≈ 1.2, Borisov on e ≈ 3.4 — ε > 0, one pass, never again.
  • A perfect parabola (ε exactly 0) is a knife-edge no real object sits on — which is why the sim snaps the slider onto e = 1: it is the only way to land there.
Catalogue eccentricities on the slider's scale, drawn to e ≈ 1.3. The slider itself runs on to 2.2 — past everything here except Borisov (e ≈ 3.4).
06

The traps they'll set for you

  • "A planet's path is just an ellipse." The ellipse is one member of a four-shape family written by the same law — the slider never leaves that family.
  • "The Sun sits at the centre." It sits at a focus, off-centre. The circle is the one case where focus and centre coincide.
  • "With ε > 0 the orbit just gets bigger." It stops being an orbit: past the edge the path is open for good — the dashed red ghost in the inquiry is the closed loop that never happens.
  • "e = 1 is a slightly longer ellipse." It is a change of fate, not of degree: e = 0.99 turns back; e = 1.01 never does.
07

Ninety-second self-test

At the parabolic edge (e = 1), how fast is the body moving when it is very far from the Sun?

As slowly as you like: ε = 0 means ½v² = GM/r, so v → 0 as r → ∞. It escapes with nothing to spare — that is what makes it the edge.

Raise e from 0.5 to 1.0 — double it. Does ε double?

No. ε = GM(e²−1)/2p is quadratic in e: with GM = 1 and p = 1.5 it climbs from −0.25 all the way to 0. Energy is not proportional to e — only the sign of ε reads off the fate.

A comet flies past on a hyperbola. Where is the Sun relative to its path?

Still at the focus of the branch it travels, on the concave side of the curve. Focus geometry comes from r = p/(1+e cos θ), which holds for all four conics — even the ones that never close.
