Guided Inquiry

1 · The apparatus

Let’s meet our planet. A globe spins about its axis, west to east. Click anywhere on it to release a parcel of air — and once it’s released, nothing pushes the parcel sideways.

You can set its starting bearing and speed in the controls below.

2 · A parcel in the north

Let’s start in the north. A parcel is released at mid-northern latitudes, heading in whatever direction you like. Seen from the turning globe, what do you think its track over the surface does?

3 · The same release in the south

Now let’s cross the equator. The parcel is released south of it, again with nothing pushing it sideways. Which way do you think its track bends there?

4 · On the equator

So the track bends right in the north and left in the south. Now here’s a tricky one: a parcel is released on the equator, heading due east. What do you think its track does?

5 · Air pulled toward a low

Now let’s build a storm. A low-pressure centre at 25° N pulls parcels straight toward it, and surface drag is on; parcels are released at rest. Which way do you think the inflow winds?

6 · A low on the equator

One last experiment. Shift-drag the low onto the equator and release parcels at rest there. What do you think the inflow does?

The Physics

The Coriolis force · a complete revision sheet · f = 2ω sin φ on a rotating sphere

The Coriolis force isn't a push on the wind. It's bookkeeping: the term a turning planet's frame has to add, with no object doing the steering.

01

Where does the curve come from?

Both tracks computed with the sim's own update rule at its defaults — launch due east, v = 0.35 R/s (globe radii per second).
  • Click the globe and a parcel flies off along the dial bearing. After launch nothing pushes it sideways — in free-parcel mode the only term the sim applies is Coriolis.
  • We watch from the turning globe. In that rotating frame the same motion needs a bookkeeping term proportional to the parcel's velocity — the Coriolis force.
  • Nothing touched the parcel. Only the frame is spinning.
The rotating-frame term — "fictitious" means frame-made, not interaction-made.
Keep the horizontal part: ẑ is the local up, and f collects everything else. This is the exact form the sim integrates.
02

Why does latitude decide?

  • Only the local-vertical part of the spin turns horizontal motion — and that part is ω sin φ. The rest, ω cos φ, points along the ground toward the pole.
  • North: f > 0, every track bends right of its motion. South: f < 0, it bends left. On the equator: sin φ = 0 — no horizontal Coriolis force at all.
  • The yellow |f| dots on the globe are this law drawn on the sphere: size ∝ sin |φ|, biggest near the poles, vanishing at the equator.
  • How much tighter at 60° N? f(60°) = 2 · 3.06 · 0.866 = 5.30 s⁻¹ against 4.33 s⁻¹ at 45°, so at the default v = 0.35 R/s the circle shrinks from r = v/f = 0.081 R to 0.066 R (closing in 1.18 s, not 1.45 s). At 0°, f = 0: no loop at all.
Left: the globe's |f| dots. Right: the spin vector at latitude φ split into ω sin φ (local up) and ω cos φ (north).
03

How hard does it turn?

φ bearing v

Bearing and v are the sim's real sliders — drag either side and the Controls panel follows. v is in globe radii per second (R/s). φ is the latitude where the replay button will release the parcel.

  • Double v → the circle radius r = v/f doubles; the period T = 2π/f doesn't care about v at all. The bearing only sets where on the circle you start.
04

What happens when a real force joins in?

Cyclone mode's exact recipe: a radial pull toward the low, the same Coriolis term, and surface drag.
  • Parcels released at rest fall straight toward the low — but every inbound path is bent right (north of the equator), so the inflow closes anticlockwise: a cyclone.
  • South of the equator f flips sign and the same low winds clockwise.
  • Inside ±5° of the equator — the sim's red band — f is too small to close the spiral. The inflow runs straight in, and no hurricane can spin up.
Rest-released inflow computed from the sim's cyclone dynamics (pull 0.9, drag 0.5).
05

What are the numbers on the real Earth?

  • Earth turns at ω⊕ = 7.29×10⁻⁵ rad/s. The sim's force law runs at ωeff ≈ 3.06 rad/s — about 42,000× Earth — so a deflection you'd wait hours for fits in a lecture minute. The Readouts panel states both exaggeration factors.
  • At 45° a 10 m/s wind turns through an inertial circle of r ≈ 97 km in T ≈ 17 h — Coriolis is a fingerprint of maps and oceans, not of teacups.
  • Real signatures: trade winds, cyclone spin in each hemisphere, ocean gyres — and the missing hurricanes within a few degrees of the equator.
06

The traps they'll set for you

  • "Coriolis is a real push." No — it's the bookkeeping of a rotating frame. No object exerts it and it has no reaction partner; in a non-rotating frame the term is simply absent.
  • "It's the same everywhere on the globe." f = 2ω sin φ: strongest at the poles, zero at the equator, opposite sign in the south.
  • "The equator should feel it most — the surface moves fastest there." Horizontal motion is turned by the vertical part of the spin, ω sin φ, not by surface speed. On the equator f = 0.
  • "It speeds the parcel up." −f ẑ × v is perpendicular to v: it steers, does no work, and leaves the speed alone.
07

Ninety-second self-test

A parcel is launched due east exactly on the equator. What does its track do?

It runs straight: f = 2ω sin 0 = 0, so there is no horizontal Coriolis force there. The same vanishing f is why the sim's red band is a no-hurricane zone.

Double the launch speed at fixed latitude. What happens to the inertial circle?

Its radius doubles — r = v/f — but the period stays T = 2π/f: the force also grows with v, so the parcel rounds a bigger loop in the same time.

A ground observer in the north insists something pushed the parcel right. What would an inertial observer say?

No object pushed it sideways: the only sideways term, −f ẑ × v, is the rotating frame's bookkeeping and is absent in a non-rotating frame. On a flat turntable you can watch that inertial path directly: a straight line.
