Concept
A particle in V = ½kx² oscillates as x(t) = A·cos(ωt + φ) with ω = √(k/m); the period T = 2π√(m/k) is independent of amplitude, while KE and PE trade off at constant total E = ½mv² + ½kx² = ½kA².
Serves
Reading potential-energy diagrams; SHM as the linear-restoring-force special case; energy conservation and turning points where V(x) = E.
Inquiry
Two balls released together from 0.30 m and 0.60 m — which returns first? Which slider CAN change the period? In which well do the recorder traces dephase?
Misconception target
"Larger amplitude ⇒ longer period." True in a constant-force well V = F₀|x| (T ∝ √A) — false for the spring, where F = −kx grows with the swing and amplitude cancels exactly.
Verified values
k = 20 N/m, m = 0.5 kg → ω = 6.3246 rad/s, T = 0.9935 s at BOTH A = 0.3 m and A = 0.6 m; E(A = 0.3 m) = ½kA² = 0.90 J, all potential at x = ±0.30 m.

5 · Predict — four times the mass

6 · Amplitude sets energy, not the clock

In the ✓ Spring well F = −kx grows with the swing, so A cancels from T = 2π√(m/k): only the spring constant k and the mass m move the clock. Amplitude still matters — it sets the energy E = ½kA² and the turning points x = ±A. A period that ignores amplitude is the fingerprint of simple harmonic motion.

The Physics

The harmonic oscillator · a complete revision sheet · why period ignores amplitude

A bigger swing isn't a longer trip on the clock. The spring pulls the far ball harder in exact proportion to its extra distance — and the trade cancels, tick for tick.

01

Why do both balls come home together?

Both panels computed at k = 20 N/m, m = 0.50 kg — turning points differ, zero crossings don't.
  • Ball 1 releases from rest at A₁ = 0.30 m, ball 2 from twice as far at A₂ = 0.60 m — same well V = ½kx², same mass.
  • The x(t) chart recorder shows both traces crossing zero together, cycle after cycle: T₁ = T₂ ≈ 0.993 s.
  • Same k + same m ⇒ same clock. The release point never enters it.
The exact solution the sim animates (φ = 0: released from rest at x = A) — no integrator anywhere.
02

What cancels amplitude out of the clock?

The restoring force is a rule of proportion — and the mass scales it into an acceleration with no A in sight.
  • F = −kx: pull the ball twice as far and the spring pulls back exactly twice as hard, at every stage of the swing.
  • Ball 2's trip is twice as long, but so is its acceleration everywhere along it — the two scalings cancel to the tick.
  • The cancellation needs linearity. Any force law that grows faster or slower than x leaves amplitude in the clock (chapter 05).

03

So what does move the period?

k m A

These are the sim's real sliders — drag here and the well follows. Watch A move everything except T.

  • Stiffer spring, quicker clock: T ∝ 1/√k. More inertia, slower clock: T ∝ √m. Quadruple m → T doubles.
04

What does amplitude actually buy?

Energy conservation — the sim's energy ledger. Turning points sit where the E-line meets the well.
  • Amplitude sets the energy budget E = ½kA² and the turning points x = ±A — never the schedule.
  • At the defaults ball 2 carries four times ball 1's energy (0.90 J vs 3.60 J): double the swing, 2² the energy.
  • KE ⇄ PE trade at constant total — all potential at x = ±A, all kinetic at x = 0, where ball 2 moves exactly twice as fast.
Faster over a longer arc, in perfect proportion — the phase lock seen from the energy side.
05

When would the myth be true?

Cap the force at F₀ and the pull stops growing with the swing — amplitude survives into the period.
  • In the constant-force well V = F₀|x| the far ball gets no extra pull, so "bigger swing, longer period" is true physics — in the wrong well. Motion there is exact parabolic arcs; T ∝ √A.
  • The sim's counterfactual is tuned: F₀ = 8kAref/π² ≈ 4.86 N makes a 0.30 m release match the spring's 0.993 s — a 0.60 m release then takes √2 × longer, 1.40 s, and the traces slip apart.
  • Real pendulums keep the myth's grain of truth: the restoring force follows sin θ, which grows slower than θ, so large swings do run slow — by about θ²/16 in fraction (first-order estimate).
  • Where nature runs the true spring: a plucked string fades — amplitude collapsing — yet the pitch holds. Isochronism is why springs, quartz and small-angle pendulums can keep time.
06

The traps they'll set for you

  • "Larger amplitude ⇒ longer period." Not in a spring well — F = −kx grows with the swing and A cancels from T = 2π√(m/k). True only where the force can't grow (V = F₀|x|).
  • "More energy means a slower cycle." E = ½kA² sets how far and how fast, never how long.
  • "The heavier ball is pulled harder, so it cycles quicker." Inertia wins: T ∝ √m — four times the mass, twice the period.
  • "The formula covers any pendulum swing." Only small angles are spring-like; at large θ the sin θ force lags and the clock genuinely slows.
07

Ninety-second self-test

Halve the amplitude. What happens to the period? To the energy?

T is untouched — there is no A in 2π√(m/k). The energy drops to a quarter: E = ½kA² scales as A².

You want to double the period using one slider. What are your options?

Quadruple m (T ∝ √m) or quarter k (T ∝ 1/√k). Amplitude can't do it at any setting — that is the whole point of the sim.

Both balls cross x = 0 together, and ball 2 is moving faster there. Why doesn't it finish first?

Its peak speed vmax = Aω is exactly twice ball 1's — but so is its distance at every phase of the cycle. Speed and path scale together, so the clock is shared.
