About: the √N law of the one-dimensional random walk

Source

Concept
W independent walkers on a line, all starting at the origin; each tick every walker takes a ±s coin-flip step. The RMS displacement about the start is exactly s√N, and the position distribution is the binomial C(N,k)/2^N on sites 2s apart.
Serves
JEE / NCERT Class 11–12: mean square displacement, addition of independent random variables, the Gaussian limit, and the diffusion estimate behind mean free paths.
Inquiry
One committed prediction before the first run ("how far after 100 steps?"), then a second commitment about ×4 steps; two reference curves appear only at the reveal.
Misconception
"Distance from the start grows in proportion to the number of steps." Random steps mostly cancel; what accumulates is the squared displacement, one s² per step, so the spread grows as √N — four times the steps buy only twice the spread.

Guided Inquiry

1 · The crowd on one point

Let’s start small. Every walker begins on the same point. Press Step everyone once a few times, and look closely: which sites can be occupied after each tick — and what pattern do you notice in the gaps between them?

2 · Commit: after a hundred steps

Time to commit to a guess. Each walker is about to take 100 coin-flip steps of length s = 1. In the RMS sense, how far from the start do you think a typical walker will stand?

3 · Run to a hundred steps

Let’s run it. Press ▶ Play — the run pauses itself at N = 100. If you like, slow steps per second to a few to watch single hops. As N climbs, what does the histogram do?

4 · What the spread reads

Now compare your choice with the spread readout, and with the predicted s√N panel that’s just been revealed. The sample wobbles a few percent about the prediction — don’t worry, that wobble is finite sampling, not a broken law.

5 · Commit: four times the steps

Let’s push further. Suppose the run continues to N = 400 — four times as many steps as at N = 100. What do you predict the spread will be there?

6 · Run to four hundred

Let’s check. Press ▶ Play again — the run continues and auto-pauses at N = 400. Between N = 100 and N = 400, what did the spread readout do?

7 · Straight against √N

Compare your choice with the readouts. You’ll see two dashed curves have now appeared: the same widths replotted against plain N (read them on their own 100–300 tick row), bending over — and y = N·s, the ballistic path if every step went one way.

8 · Sampling noise, not physics

Now let’s play with the crowd size. Drag walkers W to 50, then to 1000. Each drag restarts and automatically replays the run — raise steps per second if you want it to replay quickly. The shaded band is ±3/√(2W): finite-sample wobble at W = 50, lock-on at W = 1000. It’s noise, not physics.

9 · Why the smell is slow

Here’s one from everyday life. A perfume molecule flies at ~500 m/s but random-walks in steps of ~70 nm. Using the √N law, why do you think the scent takes so long to cross a room?

Exact RMS of the N-step binomial
Squares add: each step contributes one s² to the mean square displacement, because the cross terms of independent ± steps average to zero.
The standard result — RMS displacement about the start grows as √N, not N. At s = 1, N = 100 it is exactly 10.
The exact N-step distribution is binomial on a lattice: after an even number of steps only even multiples of s are occupied, so occupied sites are 2s apart (parity).
The bell drawn over the count histogram: sites are 2s apart, so the height per site is W·2s·g(x) with g the normal density of variance s²N — the factor 2 is load-bearing.
The shaded band on the width chart: the sample RMS of W walkers scatters about s√N with relative standard deviation ≈ 1/√(2W) (large N), so ±3/√(2W) is the 3σ envelope.

The Physics

The Random Walker · a complete revision sheet · the √N law and what it costs

Random steps don't cancel to nothing. They cancel the displacement and leave the square behind — which is why a hundred coin-flip steps of length one carry a walker ten, not a hundred.

01

Where does the crowd actually go?

The sim's own default run — W = 200 walkers, s = 1, the fixed seed — after N = 100 ticks. Top: where the walkers stand (the vertical scatter is decorative; position is horizontal only). Bottom: the count per site with the Gaussian the sim draws over it. The ±140 frame is cropped to ±45; nothing reaches beyond ±24 here.
  • W independent walkers all start on the same point. Every tick, each one takes a step of length s left or right — a fair coin, independent of every other walker and of its own past.
  • The mean displacement is zero at every N — "nowhere on average" and "nowhere" are different claims. That zero is the ensemble mean; the centre of an actual W-walker crowd still jitters by about s√N/√W.
  • Parity locks the lattice. After N ticks x/s has the same parity as N, so occupied sites sit 2s apart and the whole occupied set shifts by s on every tick. That is why the histogram bins are 2s wide.
The step rule, and the statement that survives every N.
The exact N-step distribution: binomial on the lattice, not a Gaussian.
02

Why √N, and not N?

Square the step rule. The cross term dies because the new coin knows nothing about where the walker already is.
  • What accumulates is not distance but distance squared: exactly one s² per step, added to whatever was there before.
  • The recursion has no approximation in it. ⟨x²⟩ = Ns² is exact for every N — N = 1 as much as N = 400 — not a large-N law.
  • So four times the steps buy twice the spread. Plotted against √N the width chart is a straight line of slope s; plotted against plain N the same numbers bend over.
  • The red dashed y = Ns is the counterfactual: what the spread would be if every step went the same way. The data abandons it within a few ticks.
The sim's width chart, computed from the same default run. Solid: sample RMS against √N. Dotted: the identical widths against a linear-N axis (its own 100 / 200 / 300 ticks). Shaded: the ±3/√(2W) band at W = 200.

The shaded band is an ensemble statement about the sample, not about the law.
  • The readout is a sample RMS over W walkers of one seeded run — not an ensemble average. It scatters about s√N with relative 1σ ≈ 1/√(2W): 5% at W = 200, 10% at W = 50, 2.2% at W = 1000.
  • Successive N come from the same W walkers, so the trace drifts inside the band rather than re-rolling at every tick. A run that starts 3% low tends to stay low.
  • Steps per second changes nothing physical. It sets how fast ticks arrive, not what a tick does; the trace against N is identical at 1 and at 30.
04

Is the bell real, or is it a fit?

The curve the sim draws over the histogram — and why the 2s is load-bearing.
  • The truth is the binomial: discrete, parity-locked, and strictly bounded by ±Ns. No walker can be beyond the total path it has walked.
  • The Gaussian is its N → ∞ limit (de Moivre–Laplace), which is the central limit theorem for this particular sum. It has tails past ±Ns that nothing in the sim can occupy.
  • The 2s is the site spacing: it converts a probability per occupied site into a density per unit length, so the smooth curve and the discrete bars enclose the same W walkers. Drop it and the curve sits at half height.
  • The sim withholds the overlay until N = 10, where the worst site still differs from W·2s·g by 2.5% of the peak. Below that it would be drawing a lie.
05

Where does nature run this?

Give the tick a duration τ and the √N law becomes the diffusion law. The sim shows no clock — τ is yours to name.
  • A perfume molecule flies at about 500 m/s, but it only gets about λ ≈ 70 nm before a collision points it somewhere new.
  • Crossing a 3 m room needs N = (L/λ)² ≈ 1.8 × 1015 steps, and Nτ with τ = λ/v is about 3 days. (first-order estimate from the sim's own numbers; a real gas has a distribution of speeds and free paths)
  • Rooms still smell in minutes. That is convection carrying the scent, not diffusion — the walk is the slow floor, not the delivery mechanism.
  • Same arithmetic, other names: Brownian motion, heat spreading through a bar, a polymer coil whose size grows as the square root of its length.
Both panels are the same molecule at the same speed. Only the direction is re-rolled.
06

The traps they'll set for you

  • "Distance from the start grows in proportion to the number of steps." Random steps mostly cancel; what accumulates is the square. ×4 steps buys ×2 spread — 100 ticks give 10, 400 give 20.
  • "The mean displacement is zero, so the walkers go nowhere." ⟨x⟩ = 0 and ⟨x²⟩ = Ns² are both true. The crowd stays centred while it spreads; zero mean is a statement about signs, not about distance.
  • "The spread readout is the average distance from the start." It is the root-mean-square, √(Σxᵢ²/W). RMS weights the far walkers more, so it sits above the mean |x|, which is about 0.80 s√N at large N.
  • "The wobble at W = 50 means the law is only approximate." ⟨x²⟩ = Ns² is exact. What wobbles is the W-walker sample RMS, by a relative 1σ of about 1/√(2W). Raise W and the wobble shrinks; the law does not move.
07

Ninety-second self-test

After N = 1 tick, what does the spread read — and what is its sampling scatter?

Exactly s, with zero scatter. Every walker is at +s or −s, so xᵢ² = s² for all of them and √(Σxᵢ²/W) = s whatever W is. It is the one N where the ±3/√(2W) band is the wrong answer; the exact relative scatter carries a factor √(1 − 1/N), which is zero at N = 1.

Double the step length and quarter the number of steps. What happens to the spread?

Nothing. x_rms = s√N → (2s)√(N/4) = s√N. The histogram is not unchanged, though: the sites are now 4 apart instead of 2, so the same width is carved into a quarter as many occupied bins.

Your run sits 3% below s√N the whole way out to N = 400. Is the law wrong?

No — that is one sample of W walkers with a fixed seed, and the same walkers carry their deficit forward, so the trace drifts rather than re-rolling. At W = 200 a 3% offset is well inside the ±15% three-sigma band. Raise W and it closes as 1/√(2W).
