Concept
The 1D two-state chain in the fixed-force (Gibbs) ensemble: ⟨x⟩ = Nℓ·tanh(fℓ/kBT), Ω = C(N, nR), S = kB ln Ω, isotherm f(r) = (kBT/2ℓ)·ln((1+r)/(1−r)).
Serves
Entropy as multiplicity, and entropic (rubber-band) elasticity: a restoring force with no stored energy anywhere.

Guided Inquiry

1 · Forty links, one energy

Forty segments, each pointing right or left along x. No segment stores energy — every arrangement has exactly the same energy. Press ▶ Play: the chain jiggles, and the counter reports how many arrangements share its present length.

2 · Hanging the weight

Suppose you hang a 4 pN weight and let the chain straighten. Energy is identical for every arrangement. What does the arrangement counter do?

3 · Pull

Drag the force slider from 0 up to 4 pN. The weight tautens the cord; the extension tile is now open. Watch the counter as the skyline straightens and the jiggling end settles about the ⟨x⟩ tick. What did the counter do?

4 · The count is the resistance

Compare the counter with the coiled reference pinned beneath it: orders of magnitude gone, while ⟨x⟩ has climbed. Straightening only cost arrangements. No energy moved — so what, exactly, pulled back?

5 · Warm the loaded chain

The 4 pN weight hangs at 300 K. Suppose you warmed the chain to 450 K with a hairdryer, weight untouched. Does the hanging weight sag, stay, or lift?

6 · The hairdryer

Drag T from 300 K to 450 K, weight untouched, watching the weight's height, ⟨x⟩ and the counter. The force–extension plot is now open, with the 300 K isotherm pinned. What did the weight do?

7 · Tension is proportional to T

Contraction lifted the weight while the counter climbed — the chain retreats to where the arrangements are. On the plot the live 450 K curve sits exactly ×1.50 (= T/300) above the pinned 300 K reference, and the hotter curve's kBT/ℓ marker sits higher; read both at r = 0.60 in the isotherm panel. Why does hotter jiggling favor the coil?

8 · The endpoint is a limit

Set f = 20 pN and T = 150 K: the counter reads 1 and S = 0, yet ⟨x⟩ stays strictly inside the contour — watch 1 − r stay finite in the extension tile. Ω = 1 needs f → ∞. Entropy is the spring.

The Physics

Entropic elasticity · a complete revision sheet · the two-state chain under a hanging weight

A rubber band's pull isn't stored energy. It's a shortage of arrangements — every configuration of this chain has exactly the same energy, and straightening it throws five orders of magnitude of them away.

01

If nothing stores energy, what pulls back?

Both arrangements are ones the sim's own Markov chain actually visits, at its default N = 40, ℓ = 1 nm.
  • The chain is N = 40 segments of ℓ = 1 nm, each pointing right or left along x. As the sim's plot title says, every arrangement has the same energy — there is no bond to stretch and no angle to bend.
  • The microstate is which segments point right. The macrostate is only how many, nR, and that alone fixes the length: x = (2nR − N)ℓ.
  • The arrangement counter reports Ω = C(N, nR), and it is brutally peaked at the coil: 1.38 × 1011 at x = 0, but only 658 008 at x = 30 nm.
  • Straightening is free in energy and ruinous in arrangements. That asymmetry is the entire spring.
Segments are distinguishable — each is labelled by its place along the chain — so the count is a plain binomial coefficient, and S is the Boltzmann entropy of the macrostate on screen.
02

How can counting arrangements be a force?

At fixed T the force you must apply is the slope of the free energy. Here ∂U/∂x is zero everywhere, so every piconewton of it comes from the entropy term.
  • Equilibrium minimises F = U − TS, not U. With U flat, the chain is pulled toward whichever length has the most arrangements.
  • Ω peaks at nR = N/2, which is x = 0. For x > 0 the entropy slope is negative, so the restoring force points back toward the coil.
  • The slope steepens without bound as x → Nℓ — the last arrangements are the expensive ones. That is why the isotherm diverges at r = 1 instead of ending at some yield point.
  • Temperature is the exchange rate: multiply the entropy slope by T and you get newtons. No stiffness constant is hiding anywhere.
The entropy hill, exact: S/kB = ln C(40, nR) at every length the chain can reach.
  • Finite N shows, and the sheet will not hide it. The tangent above is the exact thermodynamic slope, −f/kBT. Taking the slope discretely instead — one macrostate to the next, Δx = 2ℓ — gives about 3.9 pN rather than 4.00 pN at this working point. (our arithmetic from the sim's own Ω, not a number the sim prints; the gap closes as 1/N.)
03

How hard does it actually pull?

Fixed force, fixed T, fixed N — the Gibbs ensemble the sim runs. The segments are independent, so this is exact at every N, not a large-N limit.
f T N

These are the sim's own force, temperature and segment sliders — drag here and the chain behind you follows.

Below fℓ ≈ kBT the chain is an ordinary Hookean spring — but one whose stiffness is proportional to temperature.
  • Everything depends on the single group fℓ/kBT. Double f and double T together and the chain sits at exactly the same r.
  • Heat it and it stiffens. k = kBT/Nℓ² grows linearly in T, and it softens as the chain gets longer — more segments, more slack to take up.
04

Why does warming the loaded chain make it shorter?

The sim's isotherm — the exact inverse of the tanh law. T enters only through the prefactor kBT/2ℓ, so the live curve is the pinned 300 K curve scaled by exactly T/300 at every r.
Both curves computed from the sim's own isotherm on the sim's own axes; the dots are the 4 pN working points.
  • At fixed f, raising T raises the tension the chain supplies at every r. The weight can no longer hold it out that far, so the chain retreats.
  • Read it the other way: r = tanh(fℓ/kBT), so warming is arithmetically the same as weakening the pull.
  • The sim's own numbers: 4 pN at 300 K gives ⟨x⟩ = 29.87 nm; at 450 K, 22.70 nm. The chain contracts 7.17 nm and lifts the weight by about 18% of the contour.
  • The counter moves the opposite way — Ω climbs from 6.58 × 105 to 2.73 × 108. The chain retreats to where the arrangements are.
  • This is the Gough–Joule effect, known since 1805: hang a weight on a real rubber band, aim a hairdryer at it, and the weight rises. A heated metal rod does the opposite.
05

What has one jiggling 40-link chain to do with a real rubber band?

The width of the length distribution. At f = 0 it is √N ℓ = 6.32 nm — 16% of this chain's own contour.
  • ⟨x⟩ is an ensemble average. The chain on screen never sits at it: the "x now" arrowhead wanders by about σx while the ⟨x⟩ tick stays put.
  • The x̄ running average in the extension tile is a different object — a time average over one Metropolis run, which only settles onto ⟨x⟩ after many flips. Touch any slider and it starts over.
  • Relative fluctuations fall as 1/√N. Forty links wobble by 16% of their contour; a real band is a cross-linked network of order 1019 strands, so its length is the average. (order-of-magnitude estimate; the sim models one chain and never counts strands.)
  • The sim's units are the honest ones for this physics: kBT/ℓ = 4.14 pN at 300 K is the force at which a 1 nm segment starts to line up — piconewtons and nanometres, the scale of single-molecule pulling.
  • Two caveats worth carrying: this is the one-dimensional two-state chain, so the law is tanh; a three-dimensional freely-jointed chain replaces tanh by the Langevin function. And real rubber is mostly, not purely, entropic — a small energetic term survives.
06

The traps they'll set for you

  • "Heating always expands things, and a stretched band stores energy in its bonds." Neither holds here: every arrangement has identical energy, and a loaded chain contracts when heated.
  • "No energy moves, so nothing can happen." Equilibrium minimises F = U − TS. With U flat, the whole of the physics lives in the TS term.
  • "A longer chain has more arrangements." Ω = C(N, nR) is maximal at nR = N/2, the coil. Pushing nR toward N collapses it.
  • "Pulled hard means fully stretched, Ω = 1." Ω = 1 needs f → ∞. At 20 pN and 150 K the counter does read 1 — but only because the mean macrostate rounds to all-right; 1 − r is still 8 × 10−9, and ⟨x⟩ never touches the contour mark.
  • "The entropy of the chain is S = kB ln Ω, full stop." That is the entropy of one macrostate — the one on screen. The chain visits many, and the tile is reporting the count of the length you are looking at now.
07

Ninety-second self-test

Set f = 0. What is ⟨x⟩ — and is the chain therefore sitting still?

⟨x⟩ = Nℓ tanh(0) = 0, exactly. But the chain is not still: it jiggles with σx = √N ℓ = 6.32 nm at N = 40. Zero is the average length, not the length.

Double f and double T together. What happens to r, to ⟨x⟩ and to Ω?

Nothing at all — all three depend on f and T only through fℓ/kBT, which is unchanged. The tension the chain supplies at that same r has doubled, exactly as f(r) ∝ T requires.

A steel spring softens a little when you heat it; this chain stiffens. Why?

Steel's restoring force is stored bond energy, ∂U/∂x, which barely notices temperature. Here ∂U/∂x = 0 and the force is −T(∂S/∂x), so it scales with T: k = kBT/Nℓ² is proportional to temperature.
