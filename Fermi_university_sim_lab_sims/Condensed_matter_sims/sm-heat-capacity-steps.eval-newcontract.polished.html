Guided Inquiry

1 · The ladder and the budget

Every system in this large ensemble climbs the same ladder, rungs ε = 10 meV apart. Bars give the fraction of systems on each rung. The bracket beside them is the thermal budget kBT, drawn to the same energy scale. Compare bracket with first gap.

2 · Predict: cool to θ/10

Suppose you cool from 40 K to 11.6 K — one tenth of θ. What would this mode's heat capacity C/kB do?

3 · Cool it and watch

A new chart beside the ladder now plots C/kB against temperature, with a readout to match. Drag T down toward 12 K: watch the bars spill onto the ground rung, the kBT bracket shrink under the first gap, and the marker walk down the step.

4 · Frozen out, not gone

Check your prediction against the C/kB and P₀ readouts: almost every system now sits on the ground rung and C has collapsed far below kB. The mode has not vanished — it is frozen out, waiting for a budget that can buy a rung.

5 · Predict: double the spacing

Warm T until the C/kB readout reads 0.500 — the marker then sits exactly halfway up the step. Now suppose you double ε from 10 to 20 meV. What happens to the step?

6 · Drag the spacing

A second chart has appeared above C: average energy ⟨E⟩ per system versus T — its knee marks where the budget starts affording rungs. Now drag ε slowly from 10 to 20 meV. Watch the rungs spread against the unchanged bracket while the step below and the knee above slide together — and watch the parked marker get left behind.

7 · Same shape, new address

The half-step marker on the lower chart sits at T = 0.335 θ; read where it moved to. The parked marker fell off the step into freeze-out. Nothing about the shape changed — only where it stands, because C depends on T/θ alone.

8 · Why diamond broke Dulong–Petit

At 300 K the ruler reads kBT ≈ 25.8 meV, yet this widest ladder still delivers C/kB ≈ 0.82 — check the readout. Diamond's stiffest vibrations sit near 200 meV, five gaps beyond this slider. What does that do to C at room temperature?

The Physics

Heat capacity steps · a complete revision sheet · one Einstein mode in a heat bath

Heat capacity isn't a fixed property of the substance. It's a report on whether the thermal budget kBT can still afford the next rung.

01

What is actually on the ladder?

The same mode at three bath temperatures — rungs, occupation bars and the kBT bracket all on the sim's own 0–120 meV energy scale.
  • One mode, one ladder: evenly spaced levels En = nε, measured from the ground rung. This sim carries no zero-point term — E0 is exactly 0.
  • The bars are not one system jiggling. They are the fraction of a large ensemble of identical modes found on each rung, in equilibrium with a bath at T — the canonical ensemble. Nothing here evolves in time; every frame is an equilibrium state.
  • The amber bracket is the thermal budget kBT, drawn to the rungs' own scale. The whole sim asks one question: how does that bracket compare with one gap?
One partition function over the whole unbounded ladder; ⟨E⟩ and C follow by differentiation. Nothing else is assumed.
A geometric distribution. P0 = 1 − e−x is the ground-rung fraction — the sim's P₀ readout.
02

Why does cooling switch a mode off?

  • x = θ/T is the only variable. θ = ε/kB turns a spacing into a temperature at 11.60 K per meV; every curve the sim draws is a function of x alone.
  • kBT well above ε: rungs are cheap, many are occupied, and the mode stores heat like a classical one — C/kB climbs to 1.
  • kBT well below ε: the first rung costs more than the entire budget. P0 → 1, and warming the bath a little buys almost nothing. The mode is frozen out — not missing, just unaffordable.
  • The collapse is exponential in θ/T, which is why the rise looks like a cliff on a log-T axis.
At T = θ/10 this tail gives C/kB = 0.00454 — and the exact expression gives 0.00454 too.
The sim's lower chart, computed: ε = 10 and ε = 20 meV on the same fixed log-T axis, 5–1500 K.

How much heat can one mode hold?

These are the sim's real sliders — drag here and the ladder, the bracket and the marker follow. T moves on the sim's own log scale, 5–1500 K.

  • The plateau is exactly kB per mode — ½kB from kinetic energy, ½kB from potential. Dulong–Petit's 3kB is per atom: three modes each.
  • Half height always arrives at T½ = 0.3352 θ, whatever ε is — the amber marker on the sim's C chart.
04

If I change ε, does the curve change shape?

The scaling collapse. The sim's own invariant test checks (T, ε) → (2T, 2ε) to better than one part in 109.
  • C/kB depends on T and ε only through T/θ. Double ε and the step translates to double the temperature; on a log-T axis it slides rigidly, shape untouched.
  • That is why the sim's axes are fixed and never autoscale: if they rescaled, the slide would be invisible.
  • ⟨E⟩ does not collapse the same way. It is ε times a function of T/θ, so doubling both doubles the energy — the knee slides and the height doubles.
Because this ladder has no zero-point term, the classical limit is kBT − ε/2, not kBT. The sim draws its dashed classical line with that same offset.
05

Where does nature run this?

One master curve. The dots mark where T = 300 K falls for four real ladders.
  • Dulong–Petit, 1819. Almost every simple solid holds 3R ≈ 24.9 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ at room temperature — three modes per atom, each sitting on its kB plateau.
  • Diamond was the scandal. Stiff bonds mean a large ε. Einstein's 1907 one-frequency fit, θE ≈ 1320 K, gives C/kB ≈ 0.24 per mode at 300 K — a quarter of the classical value. Not a broken law: a frozen ladder.
  • The sim's own widest ladder. Set ε = 40 meV and T = 300 K: the readout reads C/kB = 0.822. Even 25.9 meV of budget is not quite enough to finish the climb.
  • Molecules too. The H2 stretch has θ ≈ 6330 K, so at 300 K its vibration is dead — which is why H2 has CV ≈ 5/2 R rather than 7/2 R. (θ from the measured 4401 cm⁻¹ stretch; this sim models a mode, not a molecule.)
06

The traps they'll set for you

  • "Heat capacity is a fixed property of the substance." The same ladder gives C/kB ≈ 0.005 at θ/10 and ≈ 1 at 10θ. It is a report on the current budget, not a label on the material.
  • "A frozen mode has gone away." The rungs are still there and still evenly spaced. Warm the bath and the full kB comes back.
  • "The plateau is 3kB." 3kB is per atom, because an atom in a solid has three vibrational modes. One mode plateaus at exactly kB.
  • "C dies off as a power of T." For one Einstein mode it dies exponentially, as (θ/T)²e−θ/T. Real solids do follow C ∝ T³ at low T — but only because acoustic modes have arbitrarily small ε, which a single fixed ladder cannot represent. That gap is exactly why Debye replaced Einstein.
07

Ninety-second self-test

Hold ε fixed and let T → 0. What do P0, ⟨E⟩ and C/kB do?

P0 → 1 (every system pinned to the ground rung), ⟨E⟩ → 0 and C/kB → 0. The approach is exponential, C/kB ≈ (θ/T)²e−θ/T, so it is already down to 0.0045 at T = θ/10.

Double ε and double T at the same time. What happens to C/kB, and what happens to ⟨E⟩?

C/kB is unchanged — it depends only on T/θ, and both doubled. ⟨E⟩ doubles: ⟨E⟩ = ε/(ex − 1) is ε times a function of T/θ, so the prefactor doubles while the bracket does not.

Why does this sim's plateau sit at kB and not at Dulong–Petit's 3kB?

Because it shows one mode. Dulong–Petit counts three vibrational modes per atom, each contributing kB (½ kinetic + ½ potential) once its ladder is affordable. Multiply this curve by 3N and you have Einstein's solid.
