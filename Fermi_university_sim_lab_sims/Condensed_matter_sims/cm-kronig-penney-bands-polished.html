Guided Inquiry

1 · A wire, atoms off

Left: a row of atoms with the periodic potential beneath. Right: energy E versus wavevector k. With the wells off (P = 0) the E–k curve is exactly the smooth free-electron parabola.

2 · Switch the wells on

Suppose you raise the well strength P from zero. What happens to the allowed electron energies?

3 · Drag P up

Drag the well-strength slider up from 0. Where do forbidden strips appear — and do they appear anywhere else? Watch the Gap-width panel as you go.

4 · Gaps at the zone boundaries

Gaps open only where k = ±nπ/a — the Bragg planes — and nowhere between. Deeper wells (larger P) widen them. Between the gaps, allowed bands remain, and the gap's lower edge stays pinned on the parabola.

5 · The two edges of one gap

The states just below and just above gap 1 are both standing waves of the SAME wavelength λ = 2a. Predict how the upper state earns its extra energy.

6 · Click both edges

Click the E–k curve just below gap 1, then just above. The left panel fills with each state's |ψ|². Compare where the density piles relative to the atom row.

7 · Same wave, different place

Lower edge: density sits ON the atoms, where the potential is low. Upper edge: density sits BETWEEN them, on the barriers, where it is high. Same λ, same k — the gap IS that potential-energy difference.

That is also why the lower edge stays pinned to the free-electron parabola at every P: with a node on every barrier, the sin wave never feels the wells.

8 · Why a gap can exist

The gap equals the cost of relocating the same standing wave from atoms to barriers — so it grows with P. No third placement exists, so no in-between energy is allowed.

The Physics

Kronig–Penney delta comb · a complete revision sheet · electron energies in a 1-D crystal

A band gap isn’t a wall the electron runs into. It’s the price of moving the same standing wave off the atoms and onto the barriers.

01

What does switching the atoms on do to a free electron?

The sim’s E–k panel, computed at a = 0.40 nm: wells off, and the P = 3 the guided inquiry stages. Energy axis cropped at 26 eV (the sim’s runs to 32.8 eV).
  • With P = 0 the condition below collapses to cos(ka) = cos(αa): every energy has a real k, and the E–k curve is the free-electron parabola the sim draws in slate.
  • Switch the wells on and the right-hand side is no longer capped at 1. Wherever |cos(αa) + P sin(αa)/(αa)| > 1 no real k exists, so the infinite crystal has no stationary state at all at that energy — the rose strips.
  • Nothing is taken away from the electron. The periodicity makes certain energies unpropagatable, not the electrons that would have carried them.
  • The model is one electron in an infinite 1-D chain of delta barriers, spacing a, strength P. No filling, no temperature, no electron–electron force.
The sim’s own master equation. P is the dimensionless barrier strength; α is the wavenumber inside a cell; k is the Bloch wavevector.

02

Why do gaps open only at k = ±nπ/a?

Zone boundary, reciprocal lattice, Bragg condition — three names for the same k.
  • The lattice can only change k by a reciprocal-lattice vector Gn = 2πn/a. At k = nπ/a — exactly half of Gn — the scattered wave lands on −nπ/a, degenerate with the one that came in.
  • Two degenerate travelling waves cannot stay travelling: they mix into two standing waves, one pushed down and one pushed up. That split is the gap.
  • In wavelength terms it is Bragg: λ = 2a/n means 2a = nλ, so the echo from every cell comes back in phase.
  • Anywhere else the echo is off resonance and the curve stays on the parabola — which is why the teal curve only peels away near the dashed zone lines.
  • Convention the sim uses: first Brillouin zone |k| ≤ π/a, and the plot is the extended zone — band n is drawn on (n−1)π/a < |k| < nπ/a, not folded back.
03

How wide is the gap?

The lower edge sits at αa = nπ for every P; only the upper edge moves. The sim finds it by bisection, exactly as written.
P a

These are the sim’s real sliders — drag here and the crystal follows. Energies come from the sim’s own solver.

  • Every energy in the model is ℏ²(αa)²/2ma², and the dimensionless roots αa depend on P alone — so at fixed P halving a multiplies every gap by exactly 4.
  • Raise P and the gaps widen while the bands narrow: at a = 0.40 nm band 1 runs 0–2.35 eV at P = 0, but only 1.64–2.35 eV at P = 10, heading for the isolated-box limit.
Weak-well limit. A delta comb has the same Fourier amplitude at every G, so at small P all three gaps open to nearly the same width — 0.094, 0.095, 0.095 eV at P = 0.1.
04

Two states, one wavelength — where does the extra energy come from?

The sim’s crystal panel at P = 3, computed from the same exact edge states it draws. Spikes are the delta barriers, circles the atoms.
The two states bracketing gap n. Both carry k = nπ/a, so both repeat over λ = 2a/n — that is the λ the Selected-state panel prints.
  • Lower edge: a pure sine with an exact node on every barrier. It never touches the potential, so its energy is exactly the free-electron value — that is why it stays pinned to the slate parabola at every P. Exactly ½ + 1/π = 81.8% of its probability sits in the half-cell centred on the atom, at any P.
  • Upper edge: an exact node on every atom instead, with its weight straddling the barriers — 74% in the barrier half-cell at P = 3. The delta cuts a notch in the top of that hump, so at large P the two peaks flank the barrier rather than sitting on it.
  • Same k, same repeat length, different address. The upper state pays ⟨V⟩ for standing where the potential is; the lower state pays nothing.
  • How much of the gap is that bill? Splitting the exact edge states into ⟨T⟩ + ⟨V⟩ at a = 0.40 nm gives 95% potential at P = 0.5, 74% at P = 3, only 42% at P = 10 — beyond weak wells the upper state also curves harder between barriers. (computed from the same wavefunctions the sim draws; the sim itself reports only the energies)
  • This exact pinning is a delta-comb property. Give the barriers real width and the sine state feels them too, and both edges move.
05

Where does nature run this?

Measured 300 K gaps of real 3-D crystals beside this model’s gap 1 at P = 3, a = 0.40 nm. The mechanism transfers; the number does not.
  • Ge 0.67, Si 1.12, GaAs 1.42, diamond 5.47 eV. Same origin: Bragg reflection off lattice planes splitting degenerate electron waves at the zone boundary.
  • Silicon’s 1.12 eV is a 1110 nm photon (hc/E) — near infrared. That is why a silicon wafer is opaque to visible light and transparent past 1.1 µm.
  • A real crystal is 3-D, so the zone boundary is a polyhedron and gaps open at different energies in different directions. A material is an insulator only if the forbidden intervals overlap in every direction — some strongly scattering solids are still metals.
  • What this sim deliberately leaves out: how many electrons there are. It draws the allowed energies, never a Fermi level or a filled band — so “semiconductor vs metal” is a question about filling, one layer beyond this screen.
06

The traps they’ll set for you

  • “More energy must mean a shorter wavelength.” The two states bracketing gap 1 both sit at k = π/a and both repeat over λ = 2a. The extra energy is where the density sits, not a shorter repeat.
  • “A gap is a stretch of the curve where the electrons are missing.” No electron is missing — for those energies the master equation has no real k, so no propagating state exists to be occupied.
  • “Squeeze the atoms closer and the gaps shrink.” The opposite: every energy scales as 1/a², so a = 0.40 → 0.20 nm multiplies gap 1 by exactly 4 (2.15 → 8.62 eV at P = 3).
  • “Higher gaps are always narrower.” True for a smooth potential whose Fourier components decay — not here. A delta comb feeds every G equally, so at P = 0.1 gaps 1, 2, 3 are 0.094, 0.095, 0.095 eV.
07

Ninety-second self-test

Set P = 0. Where are the gaps?

Nowhere. The condition becomes cos(ka) = cos(αa), which has a real k for every α, so every energy is allowed and E–k is exactly the free-electron parabola. The zone boundaries k = ±nπ/a still exist as geometry; their gap width is exactly zero.

Halve a from 0.40 to 0.20 nm at fixed P. What happens to gap 1?

It quadruples — 2.15 → 8.62 eV at P = 3. Every energy is ℏ²(αa)²/2ma² and the dimensionless root αa depends only on P, so the whole picture scales as 1/a². (In a real crystal squeezing the lattice would also change P; here the two sliders are independent.)

Both edges of gap 1 have λ = 2a. So where does the upper state’s extra energy come from?

From placement, not wavelength. The lower state is sin(πx/a): a node on every barrier, zero potential energy, and therefore exactly the free-electron energy at k = π/a. The upper state has its node on the atom and its density on the barriers, so it pays ⟨V⟩ — and at larger P it also curves more tightly between barriers, which is the rest of the gap.
