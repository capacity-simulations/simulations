Guided Inquiry

1 · The gallery of every way

Three labelled particles, three levels. Each tile is one complete arrangement drawn as a mini-ladder. Hover or click a tile to load it on the big ladder; the letters A, B, C are the labels.

2 · Peel the labels off

Suppose swapping two particles no longer makes a different arrangement. Would the gallery then hold more arrangements, fewer, or exactly the same number?

3 · Throw the switch

Press bosons on the statistics switch and watch the merge: tiles fly together, and each survivor gains a badge saying how many labelled tiles merged into it. What does the arrangements counter do?

4 · All three on one rung

Every arrangement in each gallery is equally likely. Compared with the labelled gallery, is finding all three particles on ONE level more likely for bosons, less likely, or exactly as likely?

5 · Read it off the badges

The all in one level counter is now on screen. Switch between distinguishable and bosons and compare it. The crowded tiles carry badge ×1, the one-per-rung tile ×6: the merge devoured the spread-out arrangements.

6 · Forbid sharing

Suppose no level may hold more than one of the three particles. How many of the ten boson arrangements would survive that rule?

7 · The exclusion wall

Press fermions and read both counters as tiles grey and fall. Then drag particles to 4, keeping 3 levels, and read what the gallery says.

8 · Statistics without forces

No force anywhere — only the rule for “different” changed. Use it: which rule you just threw explains why electrons stack into atomic shells instead of all falling to the lowest level?

The Physics

Counting with identical particles · a complete revision sheet · n particles on g levels

Bose bunching and Pauli exclusion are not forces. They are two different answers to one question: when do two of these pictures count as the same arrangement?

01

What is actually being counted?

  • One arrangement = one complete way to put n particles on g levels. The gallery draws every single one as a mini-ladder; nothing is sampled or estimated.
  • With the labels on, the letters A, B, C, D name the particles and each one picks a level independently — so the list has gn tiles.
  • The levels here carry no energy weighting: every arrangement in the current gallery is equally likely. That is the only probability assumption on this page.
The sim's arrangements readout in distinguishable mode. This is the raw labelled count — no 1/n! anywhere.
The default gallery, n = 3 on g = 3: the sim's own 27 labelled tiles and the 10 they merge into. Level 0 is the bottom rung; packing is tidied into rows.
02

Why does taking the labels off shrink the list?

  • Drop the labels and a tile is no longer "A here, B and C there" — it is only the occupancy vector (n0, n1, …, ng−1): how many sit on each rung.
  • Tiles that differed only by a swap now name the same arrangement, so they merge. Merging can only shrink a list — it never invents a new way to place particles.
  • Each survivor's ×m badge is the number of labelled tiles that landed on it, m = n!/∏ni!. The badges sum back to gn exactly: the boson tiles partition the labelled tiles.
The same occupancy vector, read under two rules. Levels are numbered 0 … g−1, as on the sim's big ladder.
Stars and bars: n identical stars into g bins is n + g − 1 symbols with n of them stars. This is the sim's boson count.
The badge on a tile, and the invariant the sim audits: the badges always total gn.

03

n g

These are the sim's real particles n and levels g sliders — drag here and the gallery rebuilds. The sim caps the gallery at gn ≤ 81, so n = 4 with g = 4 snaps back.

  • The two rules part company in n, not in g: at fixed g, WMB = gn grows exponentially with the particle number while WBE grows only like ng−1. At fixed n with g growing, they merely settle a factor n! apart.
04

If nothing attracts, why do bosons crowd?

  • Count the crowded tiles, not the gallery. All-on-one-level tiles merge one-to-one (m = 1), while spread-out tiles merge many-to-one (m = n!/∏ni!, reaching n! when no level holds two). The merge eats the spread-out arrangements preferentially.
  • So the same g crowded tiles end up as a bigger slice of a smaller list. At the defaults: 3 of 27 becomes 3 of 10.
  • This is not a small effect and not an accident: for every (n, g) this sim allows, C(n+g−1, n) < gn, so PBE > PMB strictly. The sim audits exactly that.
  • No interaction term exists anywhere in the code. The attraction is an artefact of the vocabulary — physicists call it an exchange effect, and it is pure counting.
05

Where does nature run this — and what is this sim not showing?

The two rules as nature uses them. A "level" here is one single-particle state, which is what exclusion actually forbids sharing.
  • Fermions — electrons, protons, neutrons. One per state, so electrons stack upward into shells instead of all dropping to the lowest orbital. A real energy level bundles several states — orbitals times two spins — and takes one electron each, not one electron in total.
  • Bosons — photons, He-4 atoms. Crowding is free, and the bunching is what a laser mode and a Bose–Einstein condensate live on.
  • Honest scale. This sim runs n ≤ 4 on g ≤ 4 and never exceeds 81 tiles, because it draws every one. A gas has n ~ 1023; the rule is the same, the gallery is not drawable.
  • What is missing. No energies, no temperature, no chemical potential — so this page never produces the Bose–Einstein or Fermi–Dirac distributions. Those need level energies and entropy maximisation on top of these counts.
Asymptotic, not exact: only when levels vastly outnumber particles do the three rules converge. At n = 3, g = 3 the approximation gives 27/3! = 4.5 against a true 10.
06

The traps they'll set for you

  • "Crowding needs attraction and avoidance needs repulsion." There is no interaction term anywhere here — the rule for what counts as different does all the work.
  • "The gallery shrank, so crowding got rarer." A smaller list is not a smaller share. The g crowded tiles survive intact; it is the spread-out tiles that merged away.
  • "Identical particles can never share a level." That is the fermion rule only. Bosons share freely, and the boson gallery keeps every all-on-one-level tile.
  • "WBE is just gn/n!." Only when g ≫ n. Dividing by n! is correct Boltzmann counting, an approximation; C(n+g−1, n) is the exact count and the two differ badly at small g.
07

Ninety-second self-test

Set n = 4 particles on g = 2 levels and press fermions. How many arrangements?

Zero. W_FD = C(2,4) = 0 — with at most one per level, two levels cannot hold four particles. The sim prints "0 arrangements", which is data, not an error.

At n = 2, go from g = 2 to g = 4. What happens to WMB, WBE, and the all-on-one-level odds?

W_MB = g² goes 4 → 16 (×4); W_BE = C(g+1,2) goes 3 → 10. The odds fall in both, P_MB from 1/2 to 1/4 and P_BE from 2/3 to 2/5 — but P_BE stays the larger of the two, as it does for every (n, g) this sim allows.

Nothing in this sim attracts or repels. So why do bosons crowd?

Because the set of equally likely things changed. Spread-out labelled tiles merged many-to-one and crowded ones merged one-to-one, so the crowded patterns take a larger share of a smaller gallery. It is exchange statistics — a counting rule, not a force.
