About: Particle Detector Headquarters

Concept
Layered detectors identify particles by where they leave energy. Trackers bend charged particles in a magnetic field (curvature gives momentum), the EM calorimeter absorbs electrons and photons, the hadronic calorimeter absorbs protons, neutrons, pions and kaons, and only muons cross everything. Neutrinos leave nothing and are inferred from missing momentum. At a fixed momentum, time of flight over 10 m and the Cherenkov threshold β > 1/n (water: β > 0.75188) split species by mass, because E² − p² = m² is invariant.
Why two calorimeters
Electromagnetic showers (e⁻, γ) are compact — bremsstrahlung and pair production stop them in a thin, high-Z layer. Hadronic showers (p, n, π, K) are longer and more irregular because they proceed by the strong force, so they need a much thicker absorber behind that layer. One calorimeter cannot do both jobs: a thin EM calorimeter lets hadrons punch through; a coarse hadronic calorimeter cannot measure e/γ energy well. Hence EM calorimeter in front, HCAL behind.
Serves
Introductory particle / modern physics; anyone learning to read an ATLAS-style event display.
Inquiry
Fire each species at the same momentum and compare signatures; then lower or raise p and watch the TOF race and Cherenkov cones separate — or fail to separate — π, K and p.
Misconception
"Heavier particles punch deeper." Penetration depends on how a particle interacts, not on its mass: the heavy proton showers and stops in the hadronic calorimeter, while the far lighter muon — which neither feels the strong force nor radiates much — sails through every layer. Real muons below a few GeV can range out in thick calorimeters; this sim shows the idealized textbook signatures.

The Physics

Particle detector headquarters · a complete revision sheet · how a detector names a particle

A detector never sees a particle. It sees where the energy stopped — and identification is nothing more than reading which layers lit up, in order.

01

How does the detector know what it caught?

Five events, one per row, drawn at the sim's own layer positions and stopping points.
  • Four layers, each asking one question. Tracker: are you charged? EM calorimeter: are you an electron or a photon? Hadronic calorimeter: do you feel the strong force? Muon spectrometer: are you still here?
  • A track is ionization, and ionization needs charge — so γ and n cross the tracker leaving nothing, even though both shower later.
  • The signature is the ordered list of layers that fired. Nothing in it is a direct measurement of identity; identity is inferred.
  • Only two species reach the outside: the μ⁻, which ionizes gently and never showers, and the ν, which does not interact at all.
The whole identification scheme, in one line.
02

What is the bent track worth?

The tracker's field is B = 2 T into the page; the sim labels it on the canvas.
  • Sign of the bend = sign of the charge. Every species here carries |q| = e, so the direction is the only charge information: q > 0 bends one way, q < 0 the other.
  • Curvature measures momentum. A stiffer track means a bigger radius, and r is proportional to p — which is exactly why the deflection dies off as 1/p.
  • At p = 5 GeV/c the track is nearly straight. That is the first hint that high momentum destroys information — a theme that returns in chapter 04.
  • Neutral species (γ, n, ν) produce no curvature at all, so the tracker gives them no momentum measurement.
Deflections integrated from the sim's own tracker model, drawn full length so the bend is visible; in the sim each track stops where it is absorbed.
03

Same momentum — what still tells them apart?

The mass shell. At fixed p, mass is the only thing left that can differ.
Time of flight over d = 10 m, measured against a light pulse on the same track.
p

These are the sim's real controls — pick a chip or drag p here and the detector follows.

  • π⁺, K⁺ and p share one signature: track → HCAL shower. The calorimeters cannot separate them; the clock can.
  • Heavier at fixed p means slower and more energetic at the same time — β = pc/E falls while E = √((pc)² + (mc²)²) rises.
04

Why does particle ID stop working?

Expand 1/β for pc ≫ mc²: every time-of-flight difference collapses as 1/p².
The Cherenkov threshold and cone the sim draws in the race strip, with n = 1.33 (water).
  • Every β climbs towards 1, so every arrival time climbs towards d/c = 33.36 ns. The p–π gap falls from 12.06 ns at 1 GeV/c to 0.57 ns at 5 GeV/c.
  • The Cherenkov threshold is a momentum threshold: pthr = 1.140 mc, so 0.16 GeV/c for the pion, 0.56 for the kaon, 1.07 for the proton. Below it the cone is simply absent.
  • That gives a working window, not a permanent tool: once every species is above threshold, all the cones saturate at the same 41.2° and the discrimination is gone again.
  • Real experiments answer this with several radiators of different n, transition radiation, and dE/dx — none of which this sim models.
05

Where does the real machine run this?

ATLAS and CMS in cross-section: the sim's stack wrapped around the beam, with the neutrino visible only as the red imbalance.
Momentum conservation transverse to the beam — the only handle a collider has on a neutrino.
  • The sim's flat stack is the real onion cut open: tracker innermost, then the EM calorimeter, the hadronic calorimeter, and the muon chambers outside everything.
  • Missing momentum is a subtraction, not a detection. Transverse to the beam the initial momentum is zero, so whatever the vector sum of the visible particles fails to cancel is attributed to what escaped.
  • That subtraction is how the neutrino was pinned down in β decay and how W → ℓν is found at a collider. It only works transversely; along the beam, momentum leaks down the pipe.
  • Honest limit: real muons below a few GeV can range out inside thick calorimeters, and real hadronic showers fluctuate badly. This sim draws the idealized textbook signature, as its own info panel says.
06

The traps they'll set for you

  • "Heavier particles punch deeper." No. The 0.938 GeV proton showers and stops in the HCAL; the nine-times-lighter 0.106 GeV muon crosses every layer. Depth is set by how you interact, not by mass.
  • "Neutral means invisible." Only in the tracker. The photon still deposits its full energy in the EM calorimeter and the neutron still showers in the HCAL — only the neutrino leaves nothing anywhere.
  • "The slowest one has the least energy." At fixed p the proton is both the slowest (β = 0.729) and the most energetic (E = 1.371 GeV against the pion's 1.010).
  • "More momentum, cleaner separation." The reverse: arrival-time gaps shrink as 1/p² and tracks straighten as 1/p. Nearly every handle in this detector gets worse as p rises.
07

Ninety-second self-test

Fire a photon at 0.2 GeV/c, then at 5 GeV/c. Why is the time of flight identical?

Because m = 0 makes E = pc exactly, so β = pc/E = 1 at every momentum and t = d/c = 33.36 ns always. For a massless particle, time of flight carries no momentum information at all.

Going from 1 to 5 GeV/c the p–π arrival gap falls from 12.06 ns to 0.57 ns — a factor of 21. Why isn't it 25?

The 1/p² law comes from expanding 1/β for pc ≫ mc², and at 1 GeV/c the proton is not ultra-relativistic (β = 0.729), so the expansion overshoots there — it predicts 14.36 ns instead of 12.06. At 5 GeV/c it is already accurate (0.574 vs 0.569).

π⁺, K⁺ and p all read "track → HCAL shower". What broke the tie in the sim, and what would break it in a real detector?

Here it is the 10 m time of flight, plus the Cherenkov threshold at pthr = 1.140 mc, which flips on for the proton only above ≈ 1.07 GeV/c. A real experiment adds radiators of several refractive indices, transition radiation and dE/dx — because time of flight alone dies above a few GeV/c.
