About: Wu Experiment — parity violation in ⁶⁰Co β decay

Concept
Cold, magnetically polarized ⁶⁰Co nuclei β-decay to ⁶⁰Ni. A single β counter above the source records the counting rate; flipping the field and repeating the run is the parity test — exactly Wu's 1957 protocol. Under a parity (mirror) transformation momenta flip but spins do not — so the mirror image of this apparatus is a different experiment unless the B-up and B-down runs give the same counting rate. Whether they do is exactly what you measure here.
Serves
Modern physics and particle physics units on symmetries and conservation laws; connects pseudovectors (spin, B) vs true vectors (momentum, current), β decay, and parity (P) symmetry and its violation.
Inquiry
Start warm (P = 0): the spins tumble even though the coil is energized. Cool the crystal (raise P) and run the source, then read the counting rate in both field orientations and their asymmetry 𝒜. Next hold up a mirror: the reflected apparatus has reversed current, reversed B and reversed spin, while the electrons' up-down motion — the direction the counter measures — is unchanged — compare what that image asserts with your own data.
Misconception
"Physics must look identical in a mirror, so flipping B cannot change the counting rate." It is encoded in the "Equal — a mirror image can't tell ↑ from ↓" prediction choice in the guided inquiry; the mirror panel then lets the student check that argument against the counts they collected themselves.

2 · Cool it down and count

Cooling to 10 mK locks most spins J onto B (↑), so P → 0.65. Relative to the warm rate, the counter above will read…

3 · Flip the field

Flipping B turns the apparatus into its own reflection. Parity conservation demands the flipped run repeat the same curve. The counter will read…

4 · Hold up a mirror

The two runs show electrons leaving opposite J. Reflect the whole apparatus — field, spins, tracks. Relative to the mirror-nuclei’s spin, which way do its electrons leave?

The Physics

The Wu experiment · a complete revision sheet · parity violation in ⁶⁰Co β decay

Parity didn't die in an equation. It died in a counting rate — one cobalt crystal, one β counter, read once with B up and once with B down.

01

Why does the cobalt have to be frozen first?

The channel the sim decays: ⁶⁰Co to an excited ⁶⁰Ni state, an electron, an antineutrino.
  • Every ⁶⁰Co nucleus already carries spin J. Warm, those axes point everywhere, so the ensemble has no direction — the sim draws net ⟨J⟩ as a ring, not an arrow, and labels it "axes disordered".
  • Cooling in the field lines them up. P is the polarization — the mean of cos θ over the spin axes — and in this sim it is set by temperature alone.
  • At the opening default T = 1 K, P = 0.008: effectively nothing. A recorded run starts at T = 10.3 mK, where P = 0.65 (the sidebar rounds the readout to 10 mK).
  • B and the coil current I never change — the sim draws them at constant size and says so. Only the temperature moves, so anything you see is P's doing.
The sim's polarization law — a hyperfine energy μBhf competing with kBT, with Tc = 8 mK.
The emission weight W(θ) at the sim's two reference temperatures, both computed from P = tanh(8 mK / T).
02

Which way does the electron leave?

θ is measured from B in the lab. The underbraced term is the only place a direction can enter.
  • A(⁶⁰Co) ≈ −1, so the weight is smallest along the spin and largest opposite it: the excess of electrons leaves away from J.
  • With B ↑ the single counter sits along B, so it reads below the warm rate. Flip to B ↓ and the favoured hemisphere now faces it. The canvas label switches between "β counter (along B)" and "(opposite B)".
  • Integrate W over each hemisphere and you get the counter's two readings, normalized to the warm (isotropic) rate.
  • Set P = 0 and W(θ) is flat: same rate either way. There is nothing to violate until the spins are aligned.
Hemisphere probabilities, and the same numbers as rate ratios against the warm run.

How big is the asymmetry, and how many counts buy it?

𝒜 is what the sidebar reports; σ is its binomial error from the two early windows.
T B

These are the sim's real controls — the crystal-temperature slider and the Flip B button. Move them here and the apparatus follows.

  • Halve P and 𝒜 halves; because significance is 𝒜/σ and σ falls only as 1/√N, the counts you need go up by four.
  • The (v/c) factor caps it: even at P = 1 this decay gives |𝒜| = 0.30, never 0.50.
04

Why is a mirror the whole argument?

Momentum is polar, spin and B are axial — no reflection can treat them alike.
  • In the sim's mirror panel the tracks keep their up/down sense while J, B and I all reverse. Its own subtitle says it: "reflection: tracks mirrored, axial J, B and I reverse".
  • So the reflected apparatus describes a nucleus that emits its excess along its spin. If parity were a symmetry of the weak interaction, that nucleus would have to exist.
  • The quantity that flips is ⟨J⟩·p, a pseudoscalar. Measuring it to be non-zero is the statement that parity is violated — no extra interpretation needed.
  • This is why one counter suffices. You never compare up against down; you compare the same counter against its own mirror-image run.
The sim's two panels, drawn from its own transformation rules.
05

Where does nature run this?

  • The v/c in W(θ) is the electron's own handedness. The V−A weak coupling emits β⁻ electrons with ⟨σ·p⟩ = −v/c, so at ⟨v/c⟩ = 0.60 four out of five are left-handed. The sim does not model helicity — but that is where its factor comes from.
  • Only the weak interaction does this. Strong and electromagnetic processes conserve parity exactly; no amount of cooling makes an electromagnetic decay pick a side.
  • Lee and Yang, 1956: nobody had ever actually tested parity in weak decays. Wu's group ran the cobalt over the turn of 1956–57; the Nobel followed the same year.
  • ⁶⁰Co (5⁺) to an excited ⁶⁰Ni state (4⁺) is a pure Gamow–Teller transition with a ~318 keV endpoint. That purity is why A is −1 — maximal, not marginal.
Why the weight carries v/c and not 1 — the two shares at the sim's mean beta.
06

The traps they'll set for you

  • "A mirror image can't tell ↑ from ↓, so flipping B can't change the rate." The mirror image of this apparatus is a different experiment — and your own two runs are the proof.
  • "The magnet is steering electrons into the counter." A fixed instrumental bias cannot reverse when only B flips, and the effect dies as the crystal warms with B still on. The sim draws the tracks straight — B does not bend them here.
  • "Maximal violation should give 𝒜 = 0.5." You measure P·(v/c)/2. The 0.60 mean beta caps it at 0.30 even at P = 1, and a real run's P is lower still.
  • "You need a second counter below the source." One counter plus a field flip is the entire measurement — that is what Wu did, and why the counts are labelled by field direction, not by hemisphere.
07

Ninety-second self-test

The crystal is warm, P ≈ 0.008. What does flipping B do to the counting rate?

Essentially nothing: 𝒜 = P·(v/c)/2 ≈ 0.002, far inside the error of any run you can collect. With the spin axes disordered there is no direction for the decay to correlate with.

You only manage P = 0.33 instead of 0.65. What happens to 𝒜 and to the counts you need?

𝒜 halves, to about 0.10. Significance is 𝒜/σ with σ = ½√(1/N↑ + 1/N↓), so the counts per field direction must rise by a factor of four — roughly N ≈ 2/𝒜² each for a 2σ claim.

The reflection reverses J and B but not the electrons' up/down motion. Why is that a statement about physical law and not about drawing?

Because ⟨J⟩·p is a pseudoscalar: J is axial, p is polar, so any reflection necessarily flips the sign of their product. A parity-symmetric law would require the reflected decay to be one nature also performs — and emission along the spin has never been observed.
