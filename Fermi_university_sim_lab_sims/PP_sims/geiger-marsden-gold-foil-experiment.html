About: Geiger–Marsden Gold Foil Experiment

Concept
Scintillation counts at scattering angle θ follow 1/sin⁴(θ/2), so they fall by about 2×10⁵ between 5° and 150° yet never vanish. Because roughly 1 in 26,000 incident α turns through more than 90° — impossible if the positive charge were spread through the whole atom — that charge must sit in a tiny, massive nucleus.
Sub-question
Nuclear size: the head-on closest approach is dmin = zZ·α_em·ħc/Eₖ = 45.5 fm at 5 MeV. Raising the beam momentum shrinks dmin; when the large-angle counts sag below the dashed point-Coulomb curve the α is grazing the nuclear surface at d_touch = R_Au + R_α ≈ 8.9 fm, so R_Au ≈ 7 fm (diameter ≈ 1.4×10⁻¹⁴ m).
Model notes
Plum pudding is modelled as ≈2.5×10³ random ≈0.02° kicks, so the random walk gives σ = √2500 × 0.02° ≈ 1° at 5 MeV and essentially no counts past a few degrees. The measured (green) points are always generated from the nuclear model — the switch only chooses which prediction curve is drawn over them. Single scattering only: sub-degree multiple scattering is not simulated. Foil: 0.40 µm gold, n·t = 2.4×10²² m⁻².

The Physics

Geiger–Marsden gold foil · a complete revision sheet · α on 0.40 µm gold

Rutherford never saw a nucleus. He counted flashes on a screen — and the fact that roughly one α in 26,000 came back past 90° is the entire argument.

01

What did Geiger and Marsden actually count?

  • An α source fires 5.0 MeV α at a 0.40 µm gold foil; a ZnS screen flashes wherever one lands. The only measurement is how many flashes at angle θ.
  • Thomson's plum pudding spreads +79e through the whole atom, so an α takes ≈2.5×10³ random kicks of ≈0.02°. Random kicks cancel: they add as √N, to about 1°.
  • Rutherford's nucleus puts all of that charge in a core ~10⁴× smaller. One close pass, one huge kick — and the counts never reach zero.
  • The two predictions do not overlap. At 90° they differ by over 1700 decades — which is why a single flash past 90° settles it.

02

Why 1/sin⁴(θ/2), and not something gentler?

The classical Coulomb orbit behind the formula. d(θ) is exactly what the sim uses to decide whether an α reaches the nuclear surface.
  • An α that would have missed by b is turned through θ. Halve b and the turn grows — but the ring of area 2πb·db you have to hit shrinks faster.
  • Squeezing that geometry gives a cross-section falling as 1/sin⁴(θ/2): a factor of 2×10⁵ between 5° and 150°, and never zero.
  • The sim samples θ straight from the cross-section — it does not integrate orbits. But the same orbit supplies d(θ), the closest approach it needs later.
Two exact Coulomb orbits at the sim's default 5.0 MeV: a 23 fm miss turns the α by 90°, a 6 fm miss by 150°.

How close does an α get, and how often does one come back?

The head-on closest approach — the magnitude law the panel below computes. Non-relativistic: even at 0.500 GeV/c, β ≈ 0.13.
p rate

Counts come from cross-section × areal density. For this foil n·t = 2.4×10²² m⁻² = 2.36×10⁻⁸ fm⁻².
  • Eₖ ∝ p² and dσ/dΩ ∝ 1/Eₖ², so the whole curve drops as 1/p⁴. Momentum buys depth, not counts.
  • Every factor is a ratio of distances: the odds past 90° are just n·t·πdmin²/4 — the chance of hitting a disc of radius dmin/2.
04

Does this prove the nucleus is a point?

Multiply every measured count by sin⁴(θ/2) and a point charge gives one flat line — the sim's own ×sin⁴ test.
  • At 5.0 MeV the α never gets nearer than 45.5 fm, about five times the touching distance. It only ever feels 1/r², so the flat line says "point" — at this resolution.
  • To see size you must get closer. Raise p to 0.500 GeV/c: Eₖ = 33.5 MeV, dmin = 6.8 fm, inside dtouch = RAu + Rα ≈ 8.9 fm.
  • Then grazing α are absorbed, the large-angle tail sags below the point-Coulomb curve, and the sag is the measurement of RAu ≈ 7 fm.
  • Honest caveat: the sim's absorption is a phenomenological cut-off — a logistic of 0.4 fm width on d(θ) — not a nuclear optical model.
05

Where else does nature run this experiment?

Every distance this experiment can speak about, on one logarithmic axis in femtometres.
  • 1911, Manchester. Rutherford quoted about 1 backscatter in 20,000; this model gives 1 in 26,000 at 5.0 MeV. Same order, same conclusion.
  • Nuclear radii follow R = r₀A1/3 with r₀ ≈ 1.2 fm, which for ¹⁹⁷Au gives 7.0 fm — the number the sim's absorption cut-off encodes.
  • The method never retired: fire something small and hard, look at the large-angle tail. Electrons on protons at SLAC found quarks exactly this way.
  • Scale honestly: the nucleus is ~10⁻⁴ of the atom across, so ~10⁻¹² of its volume. The atom is overwhelmingly empty, which is why most α sail through.
06

The traps they'll set for you

  • "Positive charge fills the whole atom, so α can only be nudged by fractions of a degree — never bounced back." True for that model, and that is exactly how it dies: ≈2.5×10³ random 0.02° kicks sum to ≈1°, never 90°.
  • "Backscattering means the α hit the nucleus." At 5.0 MeV it stops 45.5 fm out — more than six nuclear radii short. The reversal is pure Coulomb repulsion at a distance.
  • "A faster beam scatters more." Opposite: dσ/dΩ ∝ 1/Eₖ², so the whole curve drops as momentum rises. What improves is how close you get.
  • "The dashed curve is a second data set." No — the green points are always generated from the nuclear model. The dashed curve is only a prediction drawn over them.
07

Ninety-second self-test

The formula diverges as θ → 0. Why doesn't the sim blow up?

Because it only counts θ ≥ 1° — the 0°–1° cone is unmeasurable and is simply not simulated. In nature the divergence is cut off physically: the atom's electrons screen the nuclear charge at large impact parameters, so a bare 1/r² never acts out there.

Double the beam momentum. What happens to the counts at 150°?

Eₖ ∝ p², so Eₖ quadruples, and dσ/dΩ ∝ 1/Eₖ² drops by 16. dmin also falls by 4, from 45.5 fm to 11.4 fm — still outside 8.9 fm, so nothing is absorbed yet. Sixteen times fewer counts, same 1/sin⁴ shape.

One α in 26,000 turns past 90°. Why is that a statement about size, not about charge?

Both models carry the same +79e. Only the concentration differs. Spread through the atom it becomes ≈2.5×10³ random 0.02° kicks that sum to about 1°, so 90° is 90 standard deviations away — e⁻⁴⁰⁵⁰, zero for any experiment ever run. Concentrated, the same charge gives 1 in 26,000.
