About: Exploring the Standard Model

What this is
This simulation lays out the particle content of the Standard Model of particle physics — every quark, lepton, gauge boson and the Higgs, on one table. Recolour the tiles by Family, Charge or Mass to bring out a different pattern each time, and read the log-scale mass ladder beneath to get a feel for how enormously the masses differ. Click any tile for its mass, charge, spin and year of discovery, or switch the view to Lagrangian to see the single equation that encodes all their interactions.
Concept
The Standard Model: 17 fundamental particles — 6 quarks, 6 leptons, 4 gauge bosons and 1 Higgs — plus one compact Lagrangian encoding all their interactions. Every number shown (mass, charge, spin, discovery year) is a measured, experimentally verified value.
Units
Masses are quoted as energies through E = mc², so “0.511 MeV” is shorthand for 0.511 MeV/c². One electronvolt (eV) is the energy an electron picks up crossing a 1-volt drop, and the prefixes are just powers of ten: 1 MeV = 10⁶ eV, 1 GeV = 10⁹ eV, 1 TeV = 10¹² eV. The ladder therefore spans more than eleven decades, from neutrinos below 1 eV to the top quark at 172.6 GeV.
Serves
Summarize the Standard Model theory and explain what it means for a theory to be "experimentally well-tested": each tile carries a discovery year and precision-measured properties spanning 1897–2012, from cathode-ray tubes to the LHC.
Inquiry
Which particles are familiar and which are new? Recolor the same tiles by charge or by mass: electric charges repeat identically across the three generations, while masses grow enormously — and the log-scale mass ladder makes that pattern quantitative.
Misconception
"The Higgs, being the newest and most famous discovery, must be the heaviest particle." In fact the top quark (172.6 GeV) outweighs the Higgs (125.2 GeV); the predict question confronts this directly on the mass ladder.

The Physics

The Standard Model · a complete revision sheet · seventeen fields, one Lagrangian

The Standard Model isn't a list of seventeen particles. It's one Lagrangian with five terms — and every tile on the table is a field that one of those terms is written about.

01

What is actually on the table?

The sim's own layout: columns I–III are the three matter generations, then the gauge column, then the scalar column. Colours are the sim's Family palette.
  • 17 fields: 6 quarks, 6 leptons, 4 gauge bosons, 1 Higgs. That is the whole inventory — nothing on this table is built out of anything else on it.
  • Quarks and leptons are the matter fermions (spin ½ħ). The gauge column carries spin 1ħ and mediates the forces. The Higgs is the one spin 0 tile.
  • Every tile is a term the Lagrangian already names: the gauge column is the first term, the matter columns the second, the Higgs the last three.
The condensed form the sim's Lagrangian view expands chip by chip. Indices, gauge fixing and h.c. bookkeeping suppressed.
The covariant derivative is where matter meets force: quarks feel all three couplings, neutrinos only the weak one.
02

What repeats across the three generations?

The identity the sim prints under every particle card. Read the card's three rows and it always balances.
  • Go I → II → III and charge, spin and colour do not move. The up-type row is +⅔ three times; the down-type row −⅓; the charged leptons −1; the neutrinos 0.
  • Only mass changes — and it changes enormously: 2.16 MeV, 1.27 GeV, 172.6 GeV along the up-type row alone.
  • The L | R chip on the card is not decoration. Left-handed fermions sit in weak doublets (T = ½), right-handed ones are singlets (T = 0) — which is why T₃ and Y change when you click it while Q does not.
Recoloured by Charge: one colour per row, three generations wide.
03

Where does a particle's mass come from?

The Yukawa term. Once the Higgs field takes its value the coupling is the mass, up to v.
Particle Chirality Fermions Ladder

These are the sim's real controls — pick here and the table, the card and the ladder follow; click a tile in the sim and this panel follows back.

  • y is linear in the mass: the top sits at y ≈ 1, the electron at y ≈ 3 × 10⁻⁶. The eleven-decade spread on the ladder is a spread in coupling strength, nothing else.
  • The Standard Model does not predict a single one of these couplings. Each y is measured, then written back into the equation.
04

Why is the photon massless and the W is not?

The wrong-sign mass term tips the potential into a hat; the field settles off zero, at the 246 GeV the sim labels on the curve.
Feed that value back into |Dμφ|² and three of the four electroweak fields pick up mass.
  • From this table's own numbers: g = 2mW/v = 0.653, √(g² + g′²) = 2mZ/v = 0.741, and cos θW = mW/mZ = 0.881, so sin²θW = 0.223.
  • One combination of W³ and B never couples to the vacuum value and stays massless — that survivor is the photon. Click the photon or the Z and the card says so in as many words: isospin "0 (W³–B mix)".
  • The gluon is massless for a different reason: SU(3) colour is never broken, so no mass term for it is allowed at all.
  • The Higgs' own mass comes from the same potential: λ = mH²/2v² = 0.130 and μ = mH/√2 = 88.5 GeV.
05

How wide is the spread, and how do we know?

The sim's ladder, positions computed from the same masses: one decade per step, from the massless box to the top quark.
  • Eleven decades. Neutrinos below 1 eV, the electron at 0.511 MeV, the top at 172.6 GeV. A linear axis would put fifteen of the seventeen tiles on top of each other — hence the log scale.
  • Every number on a card is measured, with a discovery year attached: the electron in 1897, the τ neutrino in 2000, the Higgs in 2012. The table is a century of experiment, not a derivation.
  • The top quark was found in 1995, seventeen years before the Higgs, and still outweighs it — 172.6 GeV against 125.2 GeV. Newest is not heaviest.
  • Switch Fermions to Antimatter and 12 tiles flip: same mass and spin, opposite charge, T₃, hypercharge and colour. The four gauge tiles and the Higgs do not move — the photon, Z, gluon and Higgs are their own antiparticles, and the W's partner is just the W of the other sign.
06

The traps they'll set for you

  • "The Higgs was found last, so it's the heaviest." The top quark is 172.6 GeV, the Higgs 125.2 GeV — and the top was caught in 1995. Discovery order tracks how hard a particle is to make, not its mass alone.
  • "Later generations carry more charge." No. Each row holds one charge across I, II and III; only the mass grows. That is the single pattern the Charge recolour exists to show.
  • "The Higgs field gives everything its mass." It sets the masses on this table. A proton is uud — 2.16 + 2.16 + 4.70 MeV of Yukawa mass, about 9 MeV — and a proton weighs a hundred times that. The rest is strong-field energy, which the Higgs has nothing to do with.
  • "Neutrinos are massless — the table says 0." The card says "< 1 eV", an experimental upper limit. Click a neutrino and switch to R: the card answers "no νR in the SM". Without a right-handed partner the Yukawa term cannot be written, so their tiny mass comes from a mechanism outside this Lagrangian.
07

Ninety-second self-test

The ladder puts the photon, the gluon and all three neutrinos in the same left-hand box. Do they all have the same mass?

No — that box is "0, ν < 1 eV", a catch-all for everything below the axis. The photon and gluon are exactly massless, protected by unbroken U(1)em and SU(3)c. The neutrinos have a measured upper bound below 1 eV and no Yukawa term in this Lagrangian at all.

The top quark outweighs the electron by a factor of about 340 000. What does that do to their Yukawa couplings?

Exactly the same factor, because y = √2 m/v is linear in the mass: yt ≈ 0.99, ye ≈ 2.9 × 10⁻⁶. The mass hierarchy on the ladder is the coupling hierarchy — the Standard Model records it, it does not explain it.

Switch Fermions to Antimatter. Why do the four gauge tiles and the Higgs refuse to change?

Because they are their own antiparticles. The photon, Z, gluon and Higgs are neutral under every charge that would have to flip; the W is the one exception, and its partner is the W of the opposite sign, already sitting on the same tile as ±1. Only the 12 fermions have genuinely distinct antiparticles.
