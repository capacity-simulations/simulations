Guided Inquiry

1 · Dirac’s prediction

Let’s go back to 1928. Dirac’s equation for the electron insists on solutions with negative energy — and they can’t be discarded. His reading: they describe a real particle, an electron’s twin with opposite charge. This chamber is where that claim goes on trial.

2 · One track, no arrowheads

Here’s your first photograph, freshly developed: a single curved track at B = 1.50 T, and nothing else. No head, no tail, no clue which end came first. Do you think an e⁺ travelling one way and an e⁻ travelling the other could both have left this exact track?

3 · A stronger field

Now let’s turn up the field. This track was exposed at B = 1.50 T. The particle and its momentum stay the same, and the field is doubled to 3.00 T. What do you think the radius will do?

4 · Heavy versus light

Now let’s swap in something heavier. An e⁺ at 63 MeV leaves an arc of r ≈ 14 cm. A proton is given the same 63 MeV of kinetic energy. What do you think the proton’s arc will look like?

5 · A plate across the chamber

Now let’s add an obstacle. A 6 mm lead plate crosses the chamber (B = 0.70 T), and an e⁺ is about to cross it, losing ≈ 40 MeV inside. Which side of the plate do you think will carry the tighter arc?

6 · Anderson’s photograph, 1932

And now for the real thing. This is the signature Carl Anderson caught in 1932 — one track, a plate crossing, the tighter arc above. What do you think he could settle from a single photograph like this?

The Physics

Reading a cloud-chamber photograph · a complete revision sheet · curvature, momentum and the lead plate

A cloud-chamber track does not record a charge. It records a momentum — and the sign of the charge only appears once something tells you which way the particle was travelling.

01

Why does the track curve at all?

Same entry point, same 63 MeV, opposite charge — both arcs computed with the sim's own step-by-step integrator.
  • The chamber is a 32 cm × 20 cm box of supersaturated vapour with B out of the page. A charged particle ionises the vapour and droplets condense along the trail: the track is the ionisation.
  • The magnetic force is always perpendicular to the velocity, so it does no work. The speed never changes and the path is a circle.
  • Every particle in this sim carries |q| = 1 e, so the radius is reading one thing only: momentum. Not energy, not speed, not mass.
Perpendicular force, constant speed — what survives in the radius is p.
The practical form the sim evaluates: 0.299792458 is just c written as GeV per tesla-metre.
02

Can the curve alone tell you the sign?

Flip the charge and the direction of travel together and the force is unchanged — so the arc is unchanged.
  • A developed photograph has no arrowheads. The sim makes that literal: with the plate out the track is drawn as a static developing photograph — no glowing head, no chevron, no direction cue at all.
  • Reverse the direction of travel and the sign of the charge and q v × B comes out the same. An e⁺ going up and an e⁻ coming down leave one and the same curve.
  • The usual guess — "an e⁻ must bend the other way" — is the mirror image, a different curve entirely. It misses the photograph by centimetres.
The See-it below draws exactly this in the sim: the dashed mirror guess misses, the reversed e⁻ lands on the photograph point for point.
03

How much momentum is that arc worth?

The chain the Kinetic energy slider walks: energy in, momentum out, β on the side.

These two are the sim's real sliders — drag here and the chamber follows.

  • Double B and r halves; double p and r doubles. The species enters only through mc²: at the same 63 MeV a proton carries 350 MeV/c to a positron's 63.5 MeV/c, so its arc is 78 cm against 14 cm.
04

What does a lead plate add?

  • Six millimetres of lead across the middle of the chamber. Lead only ever takes energy — so p falls, and with it r = p/(|q|B).
  • That makes the exit side the tighter arc, always. Which fixes the direction of travel — and only then does the curl give the sign. This is the whole trick.
  • For e± the loss is dominated by bremsstrahlung, dE/dx ≈ E/X₀: proportional to E, so it bites hard and the arc tightens sharply. For μ, p and p̄ it is collision loss ∝ 1/β², which is why a 63 MeV proton stops inside the lead instead of emerging.
  • The sim does not model multiple scattering in the lead: the track leaves the plate along the direction it would have had anyway, only slower.
The sim's own loss law, integrated along the path actually travelled inside the lead. X₀,eff = 8 mm stands in for lead's true 5.6 mm so one typical track reproduces Anderson's 63 → 23 MeV.
The plate crossing at B = 0.70 T, computed with the sim's own integrator: the entry arc is 30.3 cm, the exit arc 10.7 cm, and the readout names the 41 MeV the lead took.
05

What did Anderson's single photograph prove?

The only inequality that matters: the exit arc is the tighter one, so the tighter side came second.
  • Carl Anderson photographed a cosmic-ray track crossing a 6 mm lead plate in a 1.5 T field: 63 MeV on one side, 23 MeV on the other.
  • The tighter arc fixed the direction — the particle went up. Its curl then gave the sign, and the sign came out positive.
  • A proton of that curvature would have been far slower and ionised far more heavily, stopping in a fraction of the observed path. The sim keeps that cue: droplet density scales as 1/β², so slow tracks are visibly fatter.
  • What was left was a positive electron — Dirac's antiparticle, the positron. The sim's defaults are his numbers: e⁺, 63 MeV, 1.5 T, 6 mm of lead. Here the track starts on the chamber floor, so at 1.50 T its 14 cm arc meets the lead about 43° off the normal, comes out on a tight 3 cm curl and falls back into the lead. At 0.70 T it meets the plate at about 19° and emerges: the crossing drawn in chapter 04.
06

The traps they'll set for you

  • "The way it curls tells you the charge." Only with the direction of travel already in hand. Same curl, opposite direction, opposite charge — identical photograph.
  • "A bigger radius means more energy." Radius tracks momentum. At the same 63 MeV of kinetic energy the proton's arc is five times wider than the positron's.
  • "The lead bends the track." The lead takes energy; B does all the bending. The arc tightens because p fell — and in this sim the lead deflects nothing at all.
  • "A stronger field gives you a bigger picture." B sits in the denominator: 1.50 T → 3.00 T takes r from 14.1 cm down to 7.1 cm.
07

Ninety-second self-test

You drop B to its minimum, 0.20 T, and fire a 63 MeV e⁺. What does the photograph look like?

Nearly a straight line. r = 0.06351/(0.29979 × 0.20) = 1.06 m — more than five times the chamber's 20 cm height — so the track climbs out of the top having drifted less than 2 cm sideways. Weak field, poor momentum measurement: that is why real chambers use strong magnets.

Double the kinetic energy from 63 to 126 MeV. Does the radius double?

For a positron, essentially yes: mc² = 0.511 MeV is negligible, so p ≈ E and r goes 14.1 → 28.1 cm. For a proton, no: p = √(E² − (mc²)²) grows only 350 → 502 MeV/c, a factor 1.44. Kinetic energy and momentum part company whenever the particle is not ultra-relativistic.

Plate out, one arc curling clockwise. What can you state, and what can't you?

You can state the momentum: p [GeV/c] = 0.29979 × B × r. You cannot state the sign of the charge — an e⁺ travelling one way along that arc and an e⁻ travelling the other are indistinguishable on the film. Slide the plate in, read which side is tighter, and the direction (then the sign) follows.
