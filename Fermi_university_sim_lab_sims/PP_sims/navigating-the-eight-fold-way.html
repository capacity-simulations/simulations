About: Navigating the Eightfold Way

Concept
Plotting baryons by isospin projection I₃ (x) and strangeness S (y) reveals the SU(3) "Eightfold Way" multiplets. Charge obeys the Gell-Mann–Nishijima rule Q = I₃ + (B+S)/2, and the one empty corner of the spin-3/2 decuplet predicted the Ω⁻ (1962) before its discovery at Brookhaven (1964) — with the equal-spacing mass ladder even predicting its mass (~1680 MeV vs. measured 1672 MeV).
Serves
Modern-physics and intro particle units: quark model, quantum numbers, and how symmetry patterns drive prediction (like Mendeleev's gaps in the periodic table).
Inquiry
Sort tiles onto the (I₃, S) chart; a symmetric pattern emerges; one corner stays empty. Students predict the missing particle's charge from the pattern, then click the gap to check against history.
Misconception
"I₃ = 0 means electrically neutral." Charge is constant along diagonals, not columns: the I₃ = 0, S = −3 corner demands Q = −1. This wrong intuition is one of the guided-inquiry choices, rebutted only when chosen.

The Physics

The Eightfold Way · a complete revision sheet · hadrons on the (I₃, S) chart

The Eightfold Way isn't a catalogue of particles. It's a map of two quantum numbers — and the one seat left empty on it was a particle nobody had ever seen.

01

Why do the baryons fall into shapes?

Both charts drawn from the sim's own tiles: across is I₃, down is S, colour is the S row. The decuplet's S = −3 corner is left as the sim's “?” gap — the one empty seat; chapter 05 fills it.
  • Every baryon tile is three quarks drawn from u, d, s. Count them and the two axes fall straight out — no extra input.
  • Colour tracks S, so a colour is "how many strange quarks". Blue S = 0, teal S = −1, purple S = −2, gold S = −3.
  • Spin picks the chart. The spin-½ combinations make the octet: a hexagon with a doubled centre. The spin-3/2 ones make the decuplet: a triangle whose corners are the one-flavour states uuu, ddd, sss — those exist only here.
The sim computes this for every baryon tile from its quark string.
The meson nonets instead: an antiquark flips the sign of S and I₃, and B is zero.
02

Where on this chart is the charge?

Hold Q fixed and the chart answers with a line — a diagonal, not a column.
  • A row shares strangeness (that is the colour). A column shares I₃. Neither shares charge.
  • Charge is constant along the dashed diagonals the sim draws: drop one unit of S and you must gain half a unit of I₃ to keep Q.
  • The decuplet carries four of them — Q = +2, +1, 0, −1; the octet three — +1, 0, −1. Follow Q = −1 down and it runs Δ⁻ → Σ*⁻ → Ξ*⁻ → straight into the empty corner.
03

04

Is the pattern real, or just a tidy drawing?

Three quark flavours; these are the only multiplets you can build from them.
  • The shapes are not chosen. Three flavours give 10 ⊕ 8 ⊕ 8 ⊕ 1 for qqq and 8 ⊕ 1 for q q̄ — the spin-0 and spin-1 nonets are that 8 ⊕ 1 twice.
  • In the lightest (L = 0) baryons colour is antisymmetric, so spin × flavour must be symmetric: only the 10 with spin 3/2 and one 8 with spin ½ survive — the sim's triangle and hexagon. The second 8 and the 1 appear only among excited baryons.
  • The doubled centre is the tell. Σ⁰ and Λ⁰ have the same quark content (uds), the same I₃ = 0 and the same S = −1, so the chart puts them on one point — yet they differ in total isospin (Σ⁰ in the I = 1 triplet, Λ⁰ the I = 0 singlet) and in mass by 77 MeV.
  • Two axes cannot show everything. Degeneracy on the chart is a hint, not a contradiction: the meson nonets stack three tiles on their centre, mixtures of uū, dd̄ and ss̄.
05

How did a hole on a chart become a particle?

The equal-spacing ladder — the second prediction, and the one that gave a number in MeV.
  • By 1962 nine of the ten decuplet seats were filled. The empty one sat at I₃ = 0, S = −3 — and the rule fixes its charge with no new input: Q = 0 + (1 − 3)/2 = −1.
  • Down the triangle the masses climb in near-equal steps: 1232 → 1385 (+153) → 1533 (+148). One more step lands at 1681 — the ≈1680 MeV the sim quotes.
  • Gell-Mann announced it in 1962; Brookhaven found the Ω⁻ in 1964 at 1672 MeV, three strange quarks and all. Both predictions landed.
  • This is Mendeleev's move: a symmetry you trust turns a blank square into a measurement. The gap is the claim; the bubble chamber is the test.
06

The traps they'll set for you

  • "I₃ = 0 means electrically neutral." No. Q = I₃ + (B+S)/2, so at I₃ = 0, S = −3, B = 1 the charge is −1. That corner is the Ω⁻.
  • "A row of one colour is a row of one charge." Colour is S. Charge runs along the diagonals that cut across the rows.
  • "The chart shows every property." It shows I₃ and S. Spin is which chart you opened; mass is in the particle card, not in the geometry.
  • "Σ⁰ and Λ⁰ are one particle drawn twice." Same quarks, same point, different total isospin — and 1193 vs 1116 MeV.
07

Ninety-second self-test

Could a baryon sit at I₃ = 0 with S = 0?

Not with a whole charge: Q = 0 + (1+0)/2 = +1/2. That is why the S = 0 rows sit at half-odd I₃ (±1/2, ±3/2) and the S = −1 rows at whole I₃ — the two halves of Q = I₃ + (B+S)/2 must add to an integer.

Step straight down one row, keeping I₃ fixed. What happens to Q?

It falls by ½, because Q = I₃ + (B+S)/2 and S dropped by one. No real tile can be there, which is exactly why the charge lines lean: the next particle of the same Q sits half a slot to the right.

The chart predicted the Ω⁻'s charge. Why could it not predict its spin?

The axes are I₃ and S only, and Q is a function of those two plus B. Spin is what selects the chart — you have to be looking at the spin-3/2 decuplet before the empty corner exists at all. The mass came from the separate equal-spacing rule, not from the geometry.
