Guided Inquiry

1 · One integral, all of perturbation theory

Spacetime shrunk to a point: this one integral is the whole theory. Expanding it in λ term by term is the diagram expansion. At λ = 0.01, press +1 term four times. What do the first dots do?

2 · Predict: does it keep improving?

Those first terms straddle the exact line and tighten. Suppose you kept pressing +1 term all the way to N = 25. Predict the zig-zag.

3 · Step on to N = 25

Press +1 term on to N = 25 (or drag N). Watch |SN − Z| in the sidebar, the dots, and the term-size bars. On the way, what did the distance to the line do?

4 · Why it turns

The bars fall to a valley, then climb without limit: |an| grows like 16ⁿn! while λⁿ only shrinks geometrically. The Closest-approach panel names the best N and 1/(16λ) beside it. Drag λ upward and watch that pair move together.

5 · Predict: flip the coupling

Back at λ = 0.01. Suppose the interaction were −λx⁴, with λ as small as you like. Predict the value of (1/√2π)∫e−x²/2+λx⁴dx.

6 · Flip it

Press ⚠ −λx⁴. Watch the tails swing up through the marker at x*, the shading flood outward, and the readouts. What does Exact Z(−λ) now read?

7 · Same series, nothing to converge to

The bars did not move — |anλⁿ| is sign-blind — yet the exact value is gone. The marker's height e−1/16λ is bitwise the same number as the floor your closest error rode: the closest error is a fixed fraction of that floor (the ratio in Closest approach). A series convergent at λ would have to work at −λ too.

8 · Asymptotic, not convergent

Radius of convergence zero: best truncation N* ≈ 1/(16λ), best error ≈ e−1/16λ. A ladder whose top rung is set by the coupling.

The Physics

Asymptotic perturbation theory · a complete revision sheet · zero-dimensional φ⁴

A perturbation series is not a slow road to the exact answer. It improves, stops improving, and then runs away — and the coupling alone decides where it stops.

01

How is one integral a whole field theory?

Spacetime shrunk to a point: the Gaussian is the propagator, e−λx⁴ is the interaction. No momenta left to integrate — only the combinatorics survives.
  • There is one field, called x, living at one point. Z(λ) is its whole partition function; the sim's shaded area ÷ √2π is Z.
  • Expand e−λx⁴ and integrate term by term: that expansion is the Feynman diagram expansion, with the Gaussian moments doing the Wick contractions.
  • (1/√2π)∫x4ne−x²/2dx = (4n−1)!! — the number of ways to pair up 4n fields. Divide by n! for the n identical vertices and you have an.
The series the sim sums. The ∼ is deliberate: it is an asymptotic expansion, not an equality.
Order λ: one quartic vertex, four fields, 3!! = 3 pairings — so a₁ = −3, exactly the first correction the sim adds.
02

Why do the corrections start growing?

The exact recurrence the sim uses everywhere. Integer ratios throughout — nothing here is floating-point noise.
  • Each coefficient is bigger than the last by a factor that grows with n: (4n+3)(4n+1)/(n+1) ≈ 16n. The number of diagrams is factorial.
  • The coupling only supplies a fixed factor λ per order. Factorial beats geometric, always — so |anλⁿ| falls, reaches a valley, then climbs without limit.
  • Consequence: the radius of convergence is zero. There is no λ ≠ 0 at which this series converges, no matter how small.
Term ratio crosses 1 near 16λn = 1 — the valley position. Everything past it makes the sum worse.

03

How close can it ever get?

Optimal truncation: stop at N* and you reach the non-perturbative floor. Take more terms and you leave it.
λ N

These are the sim's real controls — drag here and the panels follow.

  • Halve λ and you double the number of useful terms — and square the accuracy, because the floor e−1/16λ is squared.
  • The best error is never zero and never small in a polynomial sense: e−1/16λ has every λ-derivative equal to zero at λ = 0, so no power series can ever produce it.
04

Why could it never have converged?

Flip the interaction to −λx⁴ and the integrand turns around at x*, at exactly the floor height, then grows forever.
  • A convergent power series converges on a disc |λ| < R. That disc always contains negative λ — so Z(−λ) would have to exist.
  • It does not. For any λ > 0 the wrong-sign integrand beats the Gaussian past x* = 1/(2√λ): the area is infinite and the sim's readout says no value.
  • So R = 0 was forced from the start. This is Dyson's 1952 argument in miniature — the same reasoning he used against convergence in QED.
  • The bars do not move when you flip the sign: |anλⁿ| is sign-blind. Same series, nothing left to converge to.
Computed at λ = 0.01. Solid: the wrong-sign integrand, area shaded. Dashed: the right-sign one it tracks until the tails. The marked dot sits at height e−1/16λ = 1.93×10⁻³ — bitwise the same number as the error floor in chapter 03.
05

Where does nature run this?

  • QED. The same counting argument applies with α ≈ 1/137 in place of λ: the series is asymptotic, useful to roughly 1/α terms, and its ultimate accuracy is exponentially small in 1/α. Nobody has ever needed term 137 — g−2 is computed to order α⁵.
  • QCD at low energy. Here the coupling is not small, so N* is a handful of terms; confinement and the hadron masses are exactly the non-perturbative content no truncation can reach.
  • What the floor stands for. In real field theory e−const/λ is the instanton scale — the same shape, arrived at the same way. This sim does not compute instantons; it shows you the gap they have to fill.
  • The honest practice. Quote a perturbative result with its optimal-truncation error, and treat "add more loops" as a claim that needs checking, not a guarantee.
06

The traps they'll set for you

  • "If the sum runs away, take more terms — or blame the computer." The coefficients are exact integer ratios from the recurrence and the arithmetic is exact. Past N* every extra term is strictly worse.
  • "Small coupling means the series converges." Small λ moves N* further out and lowers the floor. It never makes the radius of convergence anything but zero.
  • "Asymptotic means useless." The opposite: at λ = 0.01 seven terms give seven-figure agreement. Asymptotic series are how precision physics is actually done.
  • "The wrong-sign integral is just a bit bigger." It has no value at all. The integrand is positive everywhere, so nothing cancels — the area is infinite for every λ > 0.
  • "The best N is where the terms are smallest." Close, but not identical: at λ = 0.01 the terms bottom out at n = 7 while the sums come closest at N = 6. The valley locates N*, it does not equal it.
07

Ninety-second self-test

Set λ = 0. What does the series do, and what is Z?

Every term past n = 0 carries a factor λⁿ, so the series collapses to SN = a₀ = 1 for all N, and Z(0) = (1/√2π)∫e−x²/2dx = 1. The degenerate case is the only one where the series is exact — and it is also where 1/(16λ) blows up, saying "you may keep every term".

Halve λ from 0.01 to 0.005. What happens to N* and to the best error?

N* ≈ 1/(16λ) doubles, 6.25 → 12.5, and the sim's closest approach moves from N = 6 to N = 12. The floor e−1/16λ is squared: 1.93×10⁻³ → 3.73×10⁻⁶. Twice the terms, roughly the square of the accuracy.

Why does flipping the sign of the coupling prove the series could never have converged?

Convergence of a power series on any disc |λ| < R would include −λ for small enough λ. But Z(−λ) has no value for any λ > 0, because e−x²/2+λx⁴ turns around at x* = 1/(2√λ) and grows without bound. So R = 0 — the expansion was asymptotic from the first term.
