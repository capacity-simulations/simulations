About the Simulation
Physics: This simulation follows the Lecture 6 construction: a free-particle wavefunction is built from A(k), evolved with the lecture's α(t), and displayed as Re[\sqrt{C}\psi], Im[\sqrt{C}\psi], and P(x,t).

Visualization: The upper plot shows the real and imaginary parts of \sqrt{C}\psi(x,t). The lower plot shows the lecture probability density P(x,t) = C|\psi|^2. For each slider choice, the vertical axes are fixed from the initial state rather than changing during the time evolution.

Controls: Adjust k₀, σ, and m. Here σ is the lecture's momentum-space width, so smaller σ produces a broader packet in position space.

Note: Here σ is the width in k-space, not the usual position-space Gaussian width. If the packet spreads beyond the visible x-window, the displayed norm drops below 1.

The Physics

The free Gaussian wavepacket · a complete revision sheet · drift, spreading and uncertainty

A free wavepacket doesn't spread because something pushes it. It spreads because it was never one momentum — it is many, and each one coasts at its own speed.

01

Why is a free particle a hump at all?

The same packet in its two descriptions, computed at the sim's defaults. Left: the weights A(k) the sim sums over. Right: the P(x,0) the lower plot draws. Dashed: what σ = 2 does to both.
  • A single plane wave eikx has one exact momentum ħk — and the same amplitude at every x. It is nowhere in particular.
  • To have a "where", the particle must mix momenta. The sim mixes them with Gaussian weights: A(k), centred on k₀, of width σ.
  • Doing that sum gives a hump times a carrier: ψ(x,0) ∝ e−σ²x²/2 · eik₀x. The blue and purple curves are the Re and Im of that product — at the default k₀ = 0 there is no carrier at all, Im vanishes, and the sim opens on one real hump.
  • σ is a width in momentum, not in position. Broader A(k) means a narrower packet: Δx(0) = 1/(σ√2). The sim's own note warns about exactly this inversion.
The whole simulation is this integral: every momentum gets its own phase, running at its own rate ħk²/2m.
Doing the integral gives the sim's α(t) and its closed form for ψ — and at t = 0, a hump and a carrier.
02

Where does it go, and what happens to its shape?

Exactly what the green lower plot draws. Only two numbers inside it ever move.
  • Read the plots apart: the lower one is P(x,t) = C|ψ|², a probability density. The upper one is Re[√Cψ] and Im[√Cψ] — a wavefunction, which dips below zero. No probability does.
  • P stays a Gaussian forever. Its centre is xpeak = ħk₀t/m and its width is the readout σ²|α|² — both sit in the sim's Live Metrics.
  • The centre obeys the classical rule exactly: v = ħk₀/m, which is p/m for p = ħk₀. Nothing quantum happens to the drift.
  • The width only grows: σ²|α|² = 1/σ² + σ²ħ²t²/m². k₀ appears nowhere in it — drift and spread are independent.
The sim's own P(x,t), computed at t = 0, 6 and 12 for k₀ = σ = m = 1: the centre marches in equal steps while the hump flattens.
Two independent laws, one for each of the sim's two readouts.
03

How fast does it spread?

One time scale governs everything: τ, the moment when σ²|α|² has doubled.
k₀ σ m

These are the sim's real sliders — drag here and the packet is rebuilt. Note the sim's own rule: touching any of the three rewinds the clock to t = 0.

  • Squeeze it and it escapes sooner. Double σ and the packet starts half as wide — but τ = m/σ²ħ falls by four. Narrow in x means broad in p, and a broad spread of speeds tears the packet apart.
  • Mass is the brake. τ ∝ m: the same spread of momenta divided by five times the mass is a fifth of the spread of velocities, so σ²|α|² climbs twenty-five times more slowly.
  • At t = 0 the packet sits exactly on the uncertainty floor, Δx·Δp = ħ/2. That is a property of a Gaussian, and it lasts one instant.
04

If its momentum never changes, what is changing?

Free evolution is one phase per momentum — and a phase cannot move a magnitude.
  • Free evolution multiplies each weight by e−iħk²t/2m. The magnitude |A(k)| never moves, so Δp is frozen at σħ/√2 for all time.
  • What changes is the relative phase between components. Position-space shape is built out of exactly that — which is why P widens while the momentum picture stands still.
  • So Δx·Δp = (ħ/2)√(1+(t/τ)²). The packet is minimum-uncertainty only at t = 0; ħ/2 is a floor a state can touch for an instant, not a value it must keep.
  • Differentiate the phase of the sim's own ψ and the sorting becomes visible: the local wavenumber inside the packet is kloc = (k₀/σ⁴ + ħtx/m) / |α|² — equal to k₀ everywhere at t = 0, and tilted across the packet ever after. It climbs steadily from left to right, so the fast right-movers have ended up on the right. At the sim's default k₀ = 0 that reads as long ripples at the centre and short ones at both edges.
05

Why doesn't a thrown ball do this?

The same law in SI units. Two levers only: the mass, and how tightly you localised it.
  • Write τ in terms of the starting width and the law is bare: τ = 2m(Δx₀)²/ħ. Heavier or looser means slower; lighter or tighter means faster.
  • An electron pinned to an atom's width, Δx₀ = 0.1 nm, has τ ≈ 0.17 fs. You cannot hold an electron still — which is why an atomic "orbit" is a cloud.
  • ⁸⁷Rb atoms released from a 1 µm trap have τ ≈ 2.7 ms. That is the millisecond time-of-flight expansion cold-atom labs photograph every day.
  • A 1 g bead localised to 1 µm has τ ≈ 6 × 10¹¹ years — some forty times the age of the universe. Same law, hopeless numbers. That is the whole reason the classical world looks classical.
Computed from τ = 2m(Δx₀)²/ħ. Thirty-five orders of magnitude separate the electron from the bead.
06

The traps they'll set for you

  • "σ is the width of the hump." It is the width of A(k), in momentum. The sim says so in its own notes. Larger σ gives a narrower packet: Δx(0) = 1/(σ√2).
  • "Free means nothing happens." Free means no force, so the centre coasts. The shape is not protected: σ²|α|² grows without limit, and the peak of P falls to match.
  • "Probability is leaking away." The Norm readout stays at 1.00 — the same total probability is only spread thinner. (It does dip below 1.00 once the packet outgrows the ±40 window the sim draws; that is the plot's edge, not physics.)
  • "The blue and purple curves show where the particle is." Those are Re[√Cψ] and Im[√Cψ], and they go negative. The chance of finding the particle is the green P(x,t) below them.
  • "Give it momentum and it will hold together." k₀ does not appear in the width law at all. Motion is no glue: at k₀ = 2 the packet spreads at exactly the rate it did at rest.
07

Ninety-second self-test

Set k₀ = 0. Is anything still happening?

Yes. k₀ only sets the drift, v = ħk₀/m, so xpeak stays at 0.00 and the v readout reads 0.00. The width law σ²|α|² = 1/σ² + σ²ħ²t²/m² contains no k₀ at all, so the packet spreads at exactly the rate it would have while moving.

You double σ and halve the mass. What happens to τ?

It falls by a factor of eight — four from σ² and two from m, since τ = m/σ²ħ. The packet also starts half as wide, Δx(0) = 1/(σ√2). Narrow and light is the fastest-spreading combination the sliders allow: σ = 2.00 with m = 0.5 gives τ = 0.125.

Δx grows while Δp never changes. Doesn't that break the uncertainty principle?

No — it only breaks the equality. Δx(t)·Δp = (ħ/2)√(1+(t/τ)²), which is exactly ħ/2 at t = 0 and strictly larger ever after. Δx·Δp ≥ ħ/2 is a floor a state may sit on for one instant, not a quantity that is conserved.
