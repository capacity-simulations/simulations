About the simulation

This simulation visualizes a single-qubit spin state on the Bloch sphere and shows how projective measurements along the X, Y, and Z axes collapse the state.

Use the Measure Spin buttons to perform axis measurements, Set Custom State to enter amplitudes or presets, and Reset in the header to return to the default state. The wavefunction panel and probability bars update after each action.

Guided Inquiry

1 · The Bloch sphere

Let’s get to know the picture. A spin-½ particle is drawn as the white arrow on the sphere: its tilt θ from the Z axis and its azimuth φ around that axis fix the state |ψ⟩ = cos(θ/2)|↑⟩ + eiφsin(θ/2)|↓⟩. The poles are |↑⟩ and |↓⟩, and the X and Y axes carry their own pairs of states. Measuring along an axis asks the particle which of that axis’s two states it’s in.

2 · The classical picture

Let’s begin the old-fashioned way. Before quantum mechanics, a spin is just a small arrow of fixed length ℏ/2 that can point in any direction. Tilt it 60° away from the Z axis and measure its component along Z. What do you think comes back?

3 · One measurement

Now for a real quantum measurement. The arrow lies on the equator along +X, an equal mix of |↑⟩ and |↓⟩. Measure Z just once. What do you think the history will record?

4 · How often up?

Let’s put some numbers on it. The state is now |ψ⟩ = (√3/2)|↑⟩ + (1/2)|↓⟩, the arrow tilted 60° from Z. If you prepare it afresh many times and measure along Z each time, what fraction of the runs do you think give Spin Up?

5 · Measuring twice

Now for a follow-up. The arrow is back on the equator, an even mix, and a Z measurement snaps it to one pole. What do you expect a second Z measurement on that same particle to return?

6 · An X measurement in between

One last twist. The particle sits at the |↑⟩ pole, the definite Z result of a first measurement. Now measure X, then measure Z again. How do you think this second Z result compares with the first?

The Physics

Spin measurement on the Bloch sphere · a complete revision sheet · one spin, three axes

A spin is not an arrow you read a number off. It is a direction on a sphere, and every measurement asks one yes-or-no question along an axis you choose — the tilt decides only how often the answer comes back yes.

01

Why does a tilted spin never give a tilted answer?

Left: the classical arrow, whose Z component sweeps continuously with the tilt. Right: the sim's own Bloch sphere, drawn in its default view and its axis colours, at the tilt the guided inquiry uses — θ = 60°, φ = 0.
  • The white arrow is the state: the tilt θ away from Z and the azimuth φ around it, printed live in the sim's Bloch Coordinates line. The α and β boxes carry the same information.
  • A classical spin of length ħ/2 tilted by θ has a Z component of (ħ/2)cos θ, and every value between +ħ/2 and −ħ/2 is on the table.
  • The operator has two eigenvalues and no others. Measure Z and the History line reads ↑ Spin Up or ↓ Spin Down — +ħ/2 or −ħ/2, nothing in between, whatever the tilt.
  • The tilt has not been thrown away. It survives as how often each of the two answers turns up.
One spin-½ state, written the way the sim's amplitude boxes hold it.
The measured quantity has two eigenvalues; the classical projection is not one of them.
02

Where does the arrow go the instant you measure?

The projection postulate — and the two angles the sim writes back.
  • A measurement projects the state onto the eigenstate that won. The sim does precisely this: after a Z measurement it sets θ = 0 or π with φ = 0, so the arrow ends on a pole.
  • After X it sets θ = 90° with φ = 0 or 180° (|+x⟩ or |−x⟩); after Y, θ = 90° with φ = ±90° (|+y⟩ or |−y⟩). The arrow always lands on the axis you asked about.
  • Ask the same axis again and the state is already an eigenstate: the same answer returns with probability 1 and the bars sit at 100 / 0. Repeatability is the whole content of "the state collapsed".
  • The swing you watch is the sim easing θ and φ over about two-thirds of a second. In the theory the projection is instantaneous — the sweep and the pulse rings are stage directions, not dynamics.

04

Did the spin have a value before you asked?

The classical-looking number is an average of ±ħ/2 readings, not a reading.
  • At θ = 60°, ⟨Ŝz⟩ = 0.5 × ħ/2 — and no single run ever returns it. It is what 75% at +ħ/2 and 25% at −ħ/2 average to.
  • Ŝx and Ŝz do not commute, so no state makes both sharp. Start at |↑⟩, where Z is certain; one X measurement leaves the arrow on the equator and the Z bars fall back to 50 / 50.
  • That is not clumsiness in the apparatus. Quantum mechanics assigns no joint value, and this sim implements that rule and nothing else: it stores one state, and draws each outcome against the Born probability.
  • Whether a pre-existing value could still be hiding is decided by Bell-type experiments on entangled pairs — outside this sim, which models a single spin.
Non-commuting observables, and the uncertainty relation that follows.
05

Where does nature run this?

The founding experiment: a beam of spin-½ atoms through a field gradient arrives as two spots, not the band a classical arrow predicts.
  • Stern–Gerlach, 1922. Silver atoms crossed a field with ∂Bz/∂z ≠ 0. A classical spread of tilts predicts a smear; the plate showed two spots — the same two answers this sim records.
  • NMR and MRI. The two proton spin states split in a magnetic field; the transition sits at f = (γ/2π)B = 42.58 MHz/T × B, so about 128 MHz in a 3 T scanner. (textbook constant — this sim models no magnetic field and no time evolution.)
  • Qubits. |ψ⟩ = cos(θ/2)|0⟩ + eiφsin(θ/2)|1⟩ is the line in chapter 01 with the labels changed. A gate rotates this arrow; a readout is exactly the Z measurement on screen.
  • Spin echo, electron spin resonance, atomic magnetometers, atomic clocks: all of them prepare a direction and then ask about a different one.
06

The traps they'll set for you

  • "Tilt it 60° and you measure (ħ/2)cos 60° = ħ/4." That is the classical projection, and it is never recorded. Every Z reading is +ħ/2 or −ħ/2; cos θ survives only in the average ⟨Ŝz⟩ over many runs.
  • "The amplitude is the probability." √3/2 = 0.87 is the amplitude α. Square it: cos²30° = 0.75, so the bars read 75% and 25%.
  • "The coloured sphere shows where the spin is." The shading is P(n̂), the chance a measurement along that direction returns "+". The state is the single white arrow; the colour is a map of questions, not of positions.
  • "Measure X in between and the old Z answer comes back." It does not. X leaves the arrow on the equator, where P(↑) = 1/2 — the sharp Z value is gone, not stored somewhere.
07

Ninety-second self-test

The arrow is on the |+x⟩ preset. What does an X measurement return, and what does a Z measurement return?

X returns Spin + every single time: P(+x) = ½(1 + sin θ cos φ) = ½(1 + 1) = 1, because the state is already an eigenstate of that axis. Z is a coin flip — cos²45° = 1/2 — and after it the arrow is on a pole, so |+x⟩ is gone.

Halve the tilt from 60° to 30°. Does P(↑) fall from 75% to 37.5%?

No. P(↑) = cos²(θ/2), not a straight line in θ: cos²15° = 0.933, so it rises to 93.3%. Halving the tilt moves the state closer to |↑⟩, and the probability tracks the squared cosine of the half-angle.

|+x⟩ and |+y⟩ both read P(↑) = 50%. What in the sim tells them apart?

The azimuth φ — 0° against 90° — and therefore where the arrow sits on the equator and how the sphere is coloured. Operationally: measure X. |+x⟩ gives Spin + with certainty, while |+y⟩ gives P(+x) = ½(1 + sin 90° cos 90°) = 1/2, an even split. The Z bars alone can never distinguish them.
