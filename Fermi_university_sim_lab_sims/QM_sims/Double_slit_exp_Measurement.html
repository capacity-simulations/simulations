The Physics

The double slit · a complete revision sheet · what a which-path detector does to the fringes

Interference is not light running into other light. It is one particle's two routes staying indistinguishable — and a detector that can tell them apart is all it takes to erase the fringes.

01

Why does one arrival at a time build a striped pattern?

The sim's own chart, computed at d = 1600 nm, a = 500 nm, λ = 480 nm, L = 4000 nm on the same 80-bin grid the chart uses. Above: the green curve, detectors off. Below: the orange curve the sim swaps in when the detectors go on. The sim opens at a = 100 nm: to match this figure, press Reset in the top bar in Free Exploration (the Enable Detectors button also switches to these values).
  • Each dot on the screen is one arrival. The sim never draws a wave hitting the screen — it draws individual hits, and the blue histogram is the pattern those hits make.
  • The rule is add the amplitudes, then square. The two routes each contribute a complex ψ; the square of the sum carries a cross term that the two separate probabilities do not.
  • That cross term is the fringes. Remove it and you are left with |ψ₁|² + |ψ₂|² — a sum of two humps, and nothing that alternates.
  • The green curve is a probability density, not the wave. ψ itself is never plotted in this sim; what you see is |ψ₁ + ψ₂|², and the blue bars are counts sampled from it.
Two routes, one amplitude — and the interference term that survives the squaring.

02

Which control moves which feature?

The sim's geometry in plan view: the two openings are d apart and a wide, the screen sits L behind them, and the extra distance one route travels to the point P is d sin θ.
Bright, dark, and where the single-slit envelope first falls to zero.
  • Slit separation d sets the fringe spacing: sin θm = mλ/d. Widen d and the fringes crowd together; narrow it and they spread.
  • Slit width a sets the envelope only — the broad (sin β / β)² hump that decides how bright each fringe is. It never moves a fringe.
  • Wavelength λ scales both at once, and it is the one control that also repaints the waves in the 3D view.
  • Screen distance L changes no angle at all. It converts angle into position: x = L tan θ. Push the screen back and the same fringes land further apart — so fewer of them fit on the sim's fixed ±4000 nm screen.
  • The ratio d/a is how many orders fit on each side inside the envelope's central lobe — when the lobe has an edge. At a = 500 nm (figure 1) d/a = 3.2, so the lobe could hold up to m = ±3, but only m ≤ 2 land on the ±4000 nm screen: sin θ₃ = 3λ/d = 0.90 > 0.707. At the sim's opening a = 100 nm, λ > a, so there is no lobe edge at all (d/a = 16) and every fringe sits under one hump.

04

What exactly does the detector change?

The which-path record does not touch ψ₁ or ψ₂ — it multiplies the cross term by the overlap of the two detector states.
  • Turning the detectors on entangles each route with a different detector state. The two probabilities survive untouched; only the interference term is multiplied by ⟨D₁|D₂⟩.
  • If the record is perfectly conclusive, ⟨D₁|D₂⟩ = 0 and the term vanishes. This sim models exactly that limit — its detector button is on or off, with nothing in between.
  • What the sim then draws is its own display model of "each particle went through one definite opening": two Gaussians centred on the slit positions ±d/2, each of width σ = 0.025 Lλ/a. Read them as two images of the slits, not as a computed single-slit diffraction pattern.
  • Note the sim's own housekeeping: switching the detectors on restores its detector-default geometry, and switching them off puts your earlier values back.
Fringe contrast against path knowledge. The inequality is the general statement; this sim implements only its two endpoints.
  • 𝒟 is path knowledge: how reliably the detectors tell the two routes apart — 0 = no idea, 1 = certain. For two equally likely routes and pure detector states, 𝒱 = |⟨D₁|D₂⟩| and 𝒟 = √(1 − |⟨D₁|D₂⟩|²), so the bound is met exactly.
05

Has this really been done with matter?

λ = h/p, computed for each object; the published runs are quoted as round-figure estimates. The shorter the wavelength, the finer the slits an experiment needs.
  • Single photons first (G. I. Taylor, 1909): a beam so faint that arrivals are essentially one at a time still builds the fringes over a long exposure.
  • Electrons (Jönsson, 1961; Tonomura, 1989): at 50 keV the de Broglie wavelength is about 5 pm, so the slits have to be micrometres apart and the fringes are microradians wide.
  • Neutrons and whole C₆₀ molecules (Arndt et al., 1999) do it too. C₆₀ at 200 m/s has λ ≈ 2.8 pm — smaller than the molecule itself.
  • Nothing forbids it for a 1 g bead: λ ≈ 7×10⁻²⁸ m. The fringes are there in principle and no apparatus could ever resolve them.
The three numbers above, from λ = h/p. Estimates for the quoted speeds, not measured values.
06

The traps they'll set for you

  • "The detector knocks the particle, and that smears the fringes." A momentum kick is not the mechanism. The fringes die because the cross term is multiplied by ⟨D₁|D₂⟩ — it goes to zero when the path becomes recorded, whether or not anything was jostled.
  • "The particle goes through both slits." The formalism never says so. ψ₁ + ψ₂ is one amplitude with two contributions; "which slit" simply has no value until something records it.
  • "The fringes are two particles interfering." The sim adds one hit at a time, and so did Taylor and Tonomura. The pattern is a property of the ensemble, built from single arrivals.
  • "Detectors on means classical." The measured outcome is still quantum mechanics — the same arrivals with the coherence between the routes gone. The label on the sim's chart changes from |ψ₁+ψ₂|² to P₁′ + P₂′, not from quantum to classical.
  • "Wider slits, wider fringes." Slit width a only stretches or shrinks the envelope. Fringe spacing is set by the separation d, and by λ and L.
07

Ninety-second self-test

Push λ above d — say λ = 700 nm with d = 400 nm. Where do the fringes go?

There are none to find. Bright fringes need sin θm = mλ/d ≤ 1, and with λ > d even m = 1 is impossible. Across the whole screen cos²(πd sin θ/λ) never completes a half period, so the chart shows a single broad central maximum tapering towards the edges — no crests and troughs at all.

Scale d and λ by the same factor — d 800 → 1400 nm with λ 380 → 665 nm (both ×1.75). What changes?

Not the fringe angles: sin θm = mλ/d = 0.475m either way, so the cos² maxima stay at x = ±2159 nm and the screen still holds 3 bright fringes. The envelope does change — β = πa sin θ/λ falls by 1.75, so it widens and the side fringes brighten: from 28% to 66% of the centre at a = 500 nm, but only from 95% to 98% at the sim's opening a = 100 nm, where the envelope is already broad.

The detectors record which slit, but nobody ever reads the record. Do the fringes come back?

No. The interference term is multiplied by ⟨D₁|D₂⟩, the overlap of the two detector states — a property of the apparatus, not of anyone's knowledge. Once the record is conclusive the term is zero whether or not it is read. (Erasing the record afterwards is a different experiment; this sim does not model a quantum eraser.)
