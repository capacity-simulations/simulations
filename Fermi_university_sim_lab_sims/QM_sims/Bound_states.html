About the Simulation

This simulation visualizes bound states in a finite quantum potential well, demonstrating how discrete energy levels emerge from mathematical constraints. The potential matches the lecture notes: V = 0 outside the well and V = −V₀ for |x| < a; bound-state energies E are quoted with zero at infinity (so E < 0).

The constant ℏ²/(2m) is set to a pedagogical value (not the SI free-electron value) so that typical well depths and widths produce a clear number of bound states in this demo.

Bound-state energies are fixed by the intersection of a transcendental curve with a circle in the (k, η) plane:

Here k is the wave number inside the well, η is related to decay outside the well, and a is the half-width. Each intersection corresponds to an allowed energy.

N is the number of bound states for the currently selected parity (even or odd). Adjust V₀ to see states appear/disappear as the circle expands/contracts. Finite wells support only a finite number of bound states.

Guided Inquiry

1 · The finite well

Here’s the setup. A particle moves in a well of depth V₀ and half-width a: the potential is 0 outside and −V₀ for |x| < a. A bound state is one with −V₀ < E < 0 — it’s trapped, unable to reach infinity.

2 · The classical particle

Let’s start with the classical picture. Imagine a classical particle trapped in this well. Which energies between −V₀ and 0 do you think it can have?

3 · A shallower well

Right now, three even-parity levels fit this well. Let’s make it shallower: the depth is about to be cut to its smallest value, V₀ = 1 eV, with the half-width unchanged. How many even-parity bound states do you think will be left?

4 · Squeezing the well

Now push it as far as it goes. The half-width is about to be cut to a = 0.1 nm as well — the weakest well this apparatus can make. What do you think becomes of that last even state?

5 · Filling the well

One last experiment — let’s fill the well back up. The half-width returns to a = 0.5 nm, and V₀ is then raised from 1 eV upward. How do you think the collection of bound states will grow?

The Physics

The finite square well · a complete revision sheet · bound states, counted

A finite well does not pick a few energies out of many. At every other energy the wave grows without limit outside the walls — the well keeps only the energies whose tails die away.

01

Why are almost all energies thrown away?

The sim's own two regions: V = 0 outside, −V₀ for |x| < a, with E quoted from zero at infinity.
  • Inside the well E > −V₀, so the wave oscillates: B cos kx for the even family, B sin kx for the odd one. That is the Region II line in the sim's legend.
  • Outside, E < 0. The equation allows a decaying exponential and a growing one; only the decaying one can be normalised. A bound state is one whose wave dies away at infinity.
  • At x = ±a, ψ and its slope must join. Choose an arbitrary E and that join drags the growing exponential back in — the right-hand panel below. It solves the equation; it is simply not a state.
  • Demanding that ψ stay finite is the whole of quantisation here. Nothing discrete was imposed by hand.
Both curves computed at the sim's defaults (V₀ = 10 eV, a = 0.5 nm) with its own ψ construction. Left: E₂ = −2.86 eV, one of the three even states the sim draws. Right: E = −3.50 eV, an energy the well rejects — same cosine inside, exploding tails outside. The ring marks the join at x = +a.
Kill the growing exponential and one condition is left — the curve the sim plots in its η-versus-k panel.
02

What are the curve and the circle in the η-versus-k panel?

  • The curve is the matching condition: η = k tan(ka) for the even family, η = −k cot(ka) for the odd one. It breaks into branches at the dashed asymptotes, one branch per π/2 of ka.
  • k and η are not independent. Square both definitions and add — E cancels — and you are left with k² + η² = 2mV₀/ħ². That is the blue circle, fixed by the well alone.
  • In the dimensionless pair ka and ηa the circle has radius z₀ = a√(2mV₀)/ħ. It is the one number that decides everything about the spectrum.
  • Every intersection is one bound state. The sim marks them 0, 1, 2 … and prints the matching E and δ in the Bound States panel.
The circle the sim draws in blue — its radius knows about V₀ and a, nothing else.
The sim's η-versus-k panel, computed at the defaults: orange η = k tan(ka) branches, the blue circle, and the three crossings the sim labels 0, 1, 2. Axis values are physical k and η in nm⁻¹, as the sim ticks them, so the circle's radius here is √(2mV₀)/ħ = 16.2 nm⁻¹ = z₀/a; z₀ = 8.10 is the same radius measured in units of ka.
03

How many states does this well hold, and where are they?

What the Bound States panel prints for each crossing: the energy, and the tail length δ.
V₀ a

These are the sim's own two sliders — drag either one here and the well follows.

  • Each crossing hands you k, and the energy follows: E = ħ²k²/2m − V₀. The sim sets zero at infinity, so every bound energy is negative and −V₀ is the floor, not the ground state.
  • The same crossing hands you η, and δ = 1/η is the number printed beside each energy — how far the wave reaches past the wall.
  • The panel's N counts one parity at a time. The line above counts both, because the well does not care which button is pressed.
04

What is the wave doing outside the walls?

The classically forbidden region — and the length the wave reaches into it.
  • Past the wall ψ is small but not zero, so |ψ|² is non-zero in a region where a classical particle would need negative kinetic energy and could never go.
  • The reach is δ = 1/η = ħ/√(2m|E|). Deeply bound states are held tight; a state sitting just below E = 0 spills far outside. At the sim's first-load settings the lowest even state has δ = 0.06 nm and the highest odd state δ = 0.41 nm — both printed in the panel.
  • That borrowed room is why a finite well is not an infinite one. The wave gets a longer wavelength than the walls alone would allow, so k is smaller and every level sits lower above the well floor than the matching infinite-well level of the same width.
  • Squeeze until z₀ ≪ 1 and the last state does not disappear — it becomes weakly bound, with a tail far longer than the well is wide. At the sim's weakest well (V₀ = 1 eV, a = 0.1 nm) it reports E = −0.196 eV with δ = 0.44 nm, more than four half-widths.
The shallow limit. A first-order estimate: it gives 0.26 eV where the sim's solver finds 0.196 eV at z₀ = 0.51, which is not yet small.
05

How many states can a well hold — and where does nature build them?

One new branch every π/2 in z₀: even branches at multiples of π, odd branches halfway between.
  • The even curve leaves the origin, so a circle of any radius must cut it. A one-dimensional finite well always binds at least one state, however shallow or narrow. (Three dimensions is different — there a well really can be too weak to bind anything.)
  • The families alternate, one state at a time. They are not created in even–odd pairs: at V₀ = 2 eV and a = 0.5 nm the well holds two even states and one odd.
  • z₀ ∝ a√V₀, so the count grows slowly: quadruple the depth to double the number of states. Across the sim's entire V₀ slider at a = 0.5 nm the total only runs from 2 to 10.
  • Nature's version: a few-nanometre GaAs layer between AlGaAs barriers is this exact problem, and its confinement energies set the colour of a quantum-well laser. The sim's ħ²/2m = 0.0381 eV·nm² is the free-electron value, so its energies are real electron energies; inside GaAs the effective mass is about 0.067 mₑ, which makes ħ²/2m about 15× larger — the same layer holds fewer, more widely spaced levels.
  • The extreme case is the deuteron — the neutron–proton well holds exactly one bound state, about 2.2 MeV below threshold, with a tail reaching well past the range of the force. (outside this sim's scale: MeV and femtometres, not eV and nanometres)
The count across the sim's whole depth slider at a = 0.5 nm, from its own counting condition. Steps are single, never paired; the marked point is the first-load well.
06

The traps they'll set for you

  • "N = 3, so the well has three states." The Bound States panel lists the currently selected parity. Press Odd and you get that family's list instead; at the defaults there are three of each, six in all.
  • "Make it shallow enough and nothing stays bound." In one dimension that never happens. The even branch starts at the origin, so however small the circle, it still crosses — the sim's weakest well still holds one state at E = −0.196 eV.
  • "States arrive in even–odd pairs." They arrive singly, π/2 apart in z₀. Watch the two staircases in chapter 05 step at different depths.
  • "E < 0 means the particle is below the bottom of the well." Zero is set at infinity, not at the floor. −V₀ < E < 0 is precisely the bound range; E = −V₀ would be the floor and E > 0 would be a free particle.
07

Ninety-second self-test

Set the sim to its weakest well — V₀ = 1 eV, a = 0.1 nm. How many bound states are left, and what does the survivor look like?

Exactly one, and it is even. z₀ = 0.51, which is below π/2, so no odd branch has appeared yet. The sim reports E = −0.196 eV in a well 1 eV deep and δ = 0.44 nm — a tail more than four times the half-width. The state cannot be lost: the even curve leaves the origin, so a circle of any radius still meets it.

Double the half-width and quarter the depth. What happens to the number of bound states?

Nothing. z₀ = a√(2mV₀)/ħ, so doubling a and quartering V₀ multiplies z₀ by 2 × ½ = 1. The circle is the same size, it crosses the same branches, and the count is unchanged — though the energies themselves all move, because E = ħ²k²/2m − V₀ and both k and V₀ have changed.

Why is the probability density not zero just outside the walls, and what sets how far it reaches?

The wall is finite, so the Schrödinger equation outside has real solutions; the physical one falls off as e−η(|x|−a) rather than stopping. The length scale is δ = 1/η = ħ/√(2m|E|) — set by how far the state sits below E = 0, not by the depth of the well. A state near the top of the well reaches furthest, which is why the sim's least-bound level always shows the largest δ.
