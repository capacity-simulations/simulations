Guided Inquiry

1 · The bound electron

An electron held by a proton settles into standing waves ψnlm(r, θ, φ), labelled by three integers n, l and m. Every dot is one possible position, scattered with probability |ψ|² — blue where ψ is positive, pink where it is negative. Surfaces carrying no dots at all are where ψ vanishes.

2 · The planetary picture

In the Bohr model the electron circles the proton on an orbit of one fixed radius. For the ground state, where do repeated position measurements actually land?

3 · Counting the gaps

At n = 2, l = 0 the cloud separates into an inner ball and an outer shell, divided by one spherical surface with no dots. For a state (n, l), how many such spherical surfaces are there?

4 · At the proton itself

The s states put their densest dots right at the proton. Which orbitals have any electron density there at all?

5 · Turning the orbital

The dumbbell on screen is the n = 2, l = 1, m = 0 state, aligned with z. Holding n and l fixed, m is changed to +1. What happens to the energy?

About this simulation

The Physics

Hydrogen atom wavefunctions · a complete revision sheet · ψnlm(r, θ, φ)

An orbital is not a path. It is the one standing wave that a single 1/r pull leaves behind — and every dot in the cloud is a place a measurement could put the electron, not a place it passes through.

01

Why does one electron around one proton get only certain shapes?

One particle, one Coulomb pull — the whole atom is this line.
  • The pull depends only on r, so the equation splits: a radial factor that says how far out, and an angular factor that says which way.
  • Quantisation is not assumed, it is left over. Demanding a solution that stays finite at r = 0, dies away as r → ∞, and comes back to itself after a full turn in φ leaves only integer-labelled solutions.
  • That is why the sim's dropdowns are nested: picking n rebuilds the l list (0 … n − 1), and picking l rebuilds m (−l … +l). Nothing else solves the equation.
  • ψ itself is signed, and here it is a real number at every point. The cloud you see is built from |ψ|² — the sign survives only as the blue/pink colour.

02

What are the empty surfaces inside the cloud?

Exactly the three factors the sim's radialExplicit multiplies together.
  • The Laguerre factor has n − l − 1 zeros. Each one is a whole sphere where ψ changes sign — the sim's "Radial nodes" readout, and the blue-to-pink flip in the figure above.
  • The angular factor supplies l more nodal surfaces — planes and cones through the proton. That is the "Angular nodes" readout.
  • They always add to n − 1. In the point cloud a node is a gap with no dots; in Radial Slice it is a dark line, because brightness there tracks |ψ|.
  • Near the origin R ∝ rl, so every l > 0 orbital is hollow at the proton — angular momentum keeps the electron off the centre.

03

What do the Orbital-properties readouts actually say?

The four laws behind the tiles — energy, angular momentum, mean radius, shell degeneracy.
n l m

These drive the sim's own n / l / m dropdowns — drag here and the orbital changes on screen. They obey the same nesting: lowering n pulls l down with it, and l pulls m.

  • E depends on n alone. That is special to a pure 1/r pull: 2s and 2p sit on the same rung, and the sim's energy tile does not move when you change l or m.
  • |L| = √(l(l+1)) ℏ is never lℏ. Even the stretched state has more total angular momentum than it can put on any one axis — 1.414 ℏ for l = 1, never 1.000 ℏ.
  • ⟨r⟩ grows like n². That is why the sim resizes its view box to (2.5n² + 4)a₀ when you climb the shells — the orbital genuinely gets bigger, not just redrawn.
04

Is the cloud the electron?

What one dot is drawn from — and why the sim writes ⟨Lz⟩ = 0 whenever m ≠ 0.
  • No — it is a sample. The sim keeps a random point with probability ∝ ψ²r², which is exactly |ψ|² weighted by the volume element. 50 000 points are 50 000 independent draws of one electron's position, not 50 000 electrons.
  • Colour is the sign of ψ, not the density. Density is in how closely packed the dots are. |ψ|² throws the sign away; it matters only when two orbitals overlap and add — which is where chemical bonds come from.
  • This sim draws real orbitals. Its angular factor is the real combination, so an m ≠ 0 state is a 2px/2py-type lobe pair: definite |L|, but ⟨Lz⟩ = 0. The State line says so. The complex Ylm, with Lz = mℏ, is the other basis for the same shell.
  • Nothing here evolves. ψnlm is a stationary state and the sim computes no time dependence at all — |ψ|² is frozen. The only thing moving on screen is the camera.
05

Where does nature run this?

The ladder behind the sim's energy tile, computed from En = −13.6 eV / n². Arrow labels are photon energies, not sim readouts.
  • Every line in the hydrogen spectrum is a gap on this ladder. 3 → 2 releases 1.89 eV — the red 656 nm line that made this ladder believable in 1885.
  • 13.6 eV ionises it. That is the whole depth of the n = 1 rung, and the number the sim's tile shows at the default state.
  • Push n to 7 and ⟨r⟩ reaches ~74 a₀ — about 4 nm. Real Rydberg atoms are run far higher than this sim allows, and get large enough to see under a microscope.
  • The l shapes are chemistry's s, p and d. Their degeneracy is a hydrogen-only accident: add a second electron and the l levels split, which this sim does not model.
λ = hc/ΔE is the standard conversion — the sim prints the levels, never the photons.
06

The traps they'll set for you

  • "The electron orbits at a fixed radius." Look at the 1s cloud: the dots are densest right at the proton and thin out smoothly. No radius is singled out, and r²R² peaks at 1 a₀ only because there is more volume out there — not because there is an orbit.
  • "n − 1 spherical shells." Only n − l − 1 of the nodes are spheres. At n = 3, l = 1 there is one sphere and one plane, and the sim's two tiles read 1 and 1.
  • "Blue and pink are two different things — charge, or two electrons." They are the sign of one real ψ. Probability is |ψ|², identical in both colours.
  • "Changing l or m changes the energy." Not in hydrogen. Pick any of the eight n = 2 states and the tile still reads −3.400 eV; the degeneracy g = 2n² counts them.
07

Ninety-second self-test

What happens to the cloud when l = 0?

|L| = √(0·1) ℏ = 0, so there is no angular node and no orientation — the cloud is spherically symmetric for every m (and m can only be 0). R ∝ r⁰ stays finite at the origin, so an s orbital is the only kind that is dense at the proton rather than hollow there.

Go from 1s to 3s. What do the four tiles do?

Energy climbs from −13.600 eV to −13.6/9 = −1.511 eV, so the electron is nine times less tightly held. ⟨r⟩ = (3n² − l(l+1))/2 goes 1.5 a₀ → 13.5 a₀, a factor of nine. |L| stays 0. Radial nodes go 0 → 2, total nodes 0 → 2, and the degeneracy tile goes 2 → 18.

You pick m = +1 and the sim writes ⟨Lz⟩ = 0, not Lz = ℏ. Why?

Because this sim builds real angular functions — for l = 1 those are px, py, pz, and px is an equal mix of the m = +1 and m = −1 complex states. A mix of ±ℏ in equal parts has an average of zero and no definite Lz at all. |L| = √2 ℏ is still sharp, because both pieces share the same l.
