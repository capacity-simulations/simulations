About the simulation

Physics. This uses the lecture convention for the finite well: V(x) = -V0 for |x| < a and V(x) = 0 outside, so the well has width 2a. Bound states have negative energy, oscillate inside the well, and decay exponentially in the classically forbidden region |x| > a.

Display. The upper panel shows the potential and the selected bound-state energy. The lower panel shows the actual wavefunction and probability density with their own labeled vertical scales, so no hidden peak-rescaling is used.

Assumptions. Numerical values are shown using a fixed particle mass equal to one electron mass. Keeping the mass fixed narrows the model compared with the symbolic lecture notes, but makes the plotted energies and decay lengths concrete.

Controls. V0 changes the well depth and 2a changes the well width. Animation speed only changes the phase rotation of Ψ. Choose n with ◀ ▶, the energy list, or ↑/↓. Reset restores defaults; Pause or space stops time.

The Physics

The finite potential well · a complete revision sheet · the wave that reaches past the wall

A wall of finite height is not where the wave stops. It is where the wave stops oscillating and starts dying away — slowly enough that the particle is still, measurably, out there.

01

Why doesn't the wave stop at the wall?

The sim's two panels, computed at the default well (V₀ = 20 eV, 2a = 0.50 nm). Above: the potential and the four levels it holds — the sim draws the selected one as the yellow dashed line and lists the rest in the sidebar. Below: ψ₁(x) on the right-hand scale and |ψ₁|² on the left, with |x| > a shaded.
  • This sim uses the lecture convention: V = −V₀ inside, V = 0 outside, width 2a. A bound state therefore has a negative energy, which is the number the sidebar prints.
  • Inside, E lies above V, so ψ″ = −k²ψ and the wave oscillates. Outside, E lies below V, the sign of the curvature flips, and the only solution that stays finite is a decaying exponential.
  • Decaying is not vanishing: e−κ(|x|−a) never reaches zero, so a real share of the probability sits beyond the walls. That is what Probability outside well counts.
  • Nothing is lost at the wall. A stationary state has one energy everywhere; what changes at x = a is the sign of E − V, not the value of E.
The well, and the energy window a bound state must sit in.
One equation, two regimes — the whole finite well is in the sign of E − V.
02

How far past the wall does it actually get?

The tail's reach, and the exact share of the probability that lies outside.
  • The tail falls off over the decay length 1/κ, and κ = √(2m|E|)/ħ. In this convention the rim is at zero, so |E| is exactly how far the level sits below the rim.
  • Loosely bound reaches further. A level close to the rim has a small κ and a long tail; the most deeply bound state has the stubbiest one.
  • At the default well that is a factor of 28 in the leak: 0.83% outside at n = 1 against 23.3% at n = 4 — the readout the sim prints for each state.
  • For this symmetric well the outside share is exactly |ψ(a)|²/κ: the height the wave still has at the wall, spread over the length it takes to die.
The tail beyond the wall, computed for the default well: both curves are pure exponentials, and the loosely bound n = 4 reaches more than twice as far as n = 1.
03

How many levels does a given well hold?

Matching ψ and ψ′ at the wall is the whole quantisation condition — these are the two equations the sim's root finder solves.
Each root zn is one level; the well's total is set by z₀ alone.
The root finder, drawn: every crossing of a tan/cot branch with the circle of radius z₀ is a bound state. At the default well z₀ = 5.73 gives four.
V₀ 2a

These are the sim's own two sliders — drag here and the well follows. The well relaxes into its new shape over a few frames, so the level list settles a moment after you let go.

  • Depth and width enter only through z₀. Double a and quarter V₀ and z₀ is unchanged — the same number of levels, each at the same fraction of the depth: every En is quartered along with V₀.
  • A new level appears every time z₀ passes a multiple of π/2, alternating even, odd, even, odd — which is why the parities in the list never repeat.
  • However shallow the well, N ≥ 1: a symmetric one-dimensional well always holds at least one state. Wind V₀ down to 5 eV at 2a = 0.20 nm and the four levels become one.
04

If the state is stationary, what is spinning on screen?

All the time dependence is one phase factor — and it cancels in |Ψ|².
  • The lower panel carries two curves on two scales. On the right-hand Ψ scale: the state-coloured curve is Re Ψ = ψ(x)cos(phase) and the purple one is Im Ψ = −ψ(x)sin(phase). They trade places as the phase turns.
  • On the left-hand scale, green, is |ψ(x)|² — and it never moves, because the phase multiplies by its own conjugate and cancels. That is what "stationary state" means.
  • The shape of the tail, the zeros, the energy and the outside share are all properties of ψ, not of the phase: pausing the animation changes none of them.
  • Animation speed is a display rate. The sim turns the phase at a rate proportional to |E| (with a floor, so the loosest state still moves) scaled by that slider. It is not E/ħ in seconds: at the default ground state that would be 18.9 eV/ħ ≈ 2.9×1016 rad s−1. Using |E| also fixes the sense of the rotation on screen — that direction is a display choice, not something to read physics from.
05

Where does nature build a wall of finite height?

The sim's constants and its default well, as a worked case.
  • Semiconductor quantum wells. A thin GaAs layer between AlGaAs is a well a few nm wide with a barrier of a few tenths of an eV. The electron's tail reaches into the barrier — which is exactly how two neighbouring wells couple into a superlattice band.
  • The same κ runs a scanning tunnelling microscope. An electron bound in the tip has a tail of order 0.1 nm reaching into the vacuum; the tunnelling current tracks e−2κd, so each extra 0.1 nm of gap cuts it nearly tenfold, and one atomic step (0.2–0.3 nm) changes it by about two orders of magnitude.
  • The deuteron is a finite well with a single level. Its s-wave radial problem is the odd sector of this well. Take V₀ ≈ 35 MeV and a ≈ 2 fm and that state comes out bound by ≈ 1.7 MeV against the measured 2.22 MeV, with roughly two-thirds of its probability outside the range of the force — a square-well estimate, but the right physics: the deuteron is mostly tail.
  • That is the finite well's signature everywhere: weakly bound means spatially huge, because 1/κ = ħ/√(2m|E|) blows up as the level approaches the rim.
06

The traps they'll set for you

  • "Below the rim, the particle is trapped between the walls." Classically yes — outside would need negative kinetic energy, so a marble turns around exactly at x = a. The quantum state does not: it carries real, normalised probability beyond the wall, and the sim prints the percentage.
  • "So it tunnels out and escapes." A bound state is stationary and normalised: the tail is standing, not leaking. Nothing flows outward, and the total probability stays one. Escape needs a second region on the far side of a barrier, which this well does not have.
  • "The most deeply bound state must reach out furthest." The opposite. κ = √(2m|E|)/ħ grows with binding, so n = 1 has the shortest tail (0.83% outside at the default well) and the top state the longest (23.3%).
  • "A finite well of width 2a gives the same levels as a box of width 2a." Every level sits lower. The wave continues past the wall, so its half-wave is stretched beyond 2a, k is smaller and E follows: at the default well the ground state sits 1.09 eV above the floor against 1.50 eV for an impenetrable box of the same width.
07

Ninety-second self-test

Take the well as shallow and as narrow as the sliders allow. What is left?

V₀ = 5 eV with 2a = 0.20 nm gives z₀ = a√(V₀/(ħ²/2m)) = 0.10 × √(5/0.0381) = 1.15, and ⌈2z₀/π⌉ = 1. One even state survives, at E = −2.57 eV — and with so little binding left its tail is long: about 27% of the probability lies outside the well. A symmetric well in one dimension never empties completely.

Double the width and quarter the depth. How does the level count change?

Not at all. z₀ = a√(2mV₀)/ħ, so doubling a multiplies z₀ by 2 and quartering V₀ divides it by 2 — z₀ is unchanged, and so is N = ⌈2z₀/π⌉. The energies do change, all by the same factor. The rim stays at 0 and every zn is unchanged, so each En = (ħ²/2m)(zn/a)² − V₀ is exactly a quarter of what it was (En/V₀ is fixed): from the default well to V₀ = 5 eV, 2a = 1.00 nm the ground state goes from −18.91 to −4.73 eV.

n = 1 and n = 4 turn at different rates. If the sim showed (ψ₁ + ψ₄)/√2, would the green curve still stand still?

No. Each part keeps its own phase, e−iE₁t/ħ and e−iE₄t/ħ, so |Ψ|² = ½[ψ₁² + ψ₄² + 2ψ₁ψ₄ cos((E₄ − E₁)t/ħ)] — the cross term does not cancel. Because ψ₁ψ₄ is odd, the density sloshes from one side of the well to the other at (E₄ − E₁)/ħ: at the default well E₄ − E₁ = 15.15 eV, a period h/ΔE ≈ 0.27 fs. Only a single-energy state is stationary.
