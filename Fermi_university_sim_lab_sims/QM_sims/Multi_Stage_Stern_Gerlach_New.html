About this simulation

Physics: The Stern-Gerlach experiment measures spin along a chosen axis. Z-magnets split atoms into Z+ and Z−, while X-magnets split into X+ and X−. Switching measurement axis changes the state basis, so prior spin information along a different axis is no longer definite.

Visuals: The 3D view shows the atom source, magnet stages, optional blockers, beam branching, and detector hits. Beam direction and color encode the current spin channel, and the legend maps colors to unpolarized, Z, and X outcomes.

Controls: Build Stage 2/3 by dragging magnets or blockers, set Stage 1 orientation/blocker, tune emission rate and field strength, and use play/pause, reset, and stats controls. Turn on Explore to see hover explanations on apparatus parts.

Guided Inquiry

1 · Sequential stages

In this experiment we consider sequential Stern–Gerlach stages, instead of just a single stage. A Z magnet measures the spin component along z and an X magnet measures it along x; each deflects a beam according to the value it measures. A blocker placed after a magnet stops one of that magnet’s two output beams.

2 · A second magnet

An X magnet now stands after the first Z magnet. How many regions of the detector screen are lit up?

3 · One output blocked

The spin-down (↓) output of the first Z magnet is blocked, and a Z magnet occupies the second slot. How many regions of the detector screen are lit up?

4 · A third stage

The ↓ output of the first Z magnet is still blocked. An X magnet follows, with its ← output blocked. A Z magnet fills the third slot. How many regions of the detector screen are lit up?

The Physics

Multi-stage Stern–Gerlach · a complete revision sheet · measuring spin, twice

A Stern–Gerlach magnet does not read a spin the atom was already carrying. It writes one — and writing σx erases whatever σz said.

01

Why does one magnet split the beam in two — and only two?

The sim's default setup, computed from its own geometry: one Z stage at B = 0.30 T, the parabola inside the 3.5-unit gap, then free drift to the screen. Colours are the sim's legend — lime unpolarized, rose Z+, blue Z−.
  • A magnet whose field varies across the gap pulls on a magnetic moment. The force depends on μz, the component along the field — not on the atom's speed or charge.
  • A silver atom's moment comes from one unpaired electron. Measuring it along z can only return μz = ±μB, so there are exactly two forces and two beams.
  • The sim's Z magnets deflect vertically (↑↓) and its X magnets horizontally (←→) — the same physics, a different chosen axis.
  • Before the first magnet the beam is unpolarized (lime): each atom's first measurement is a fresh 50/50 draw.
The force that does the sorting, and the only two values it can see.
02

Why doesn't a second magnet on the same axis split it again?

A measurement leaves the atom in the state it just reported. Ask the same question again and the answer is certain.
  • Block the ↓ output of a Z stage and everything downstream is in the pure σz = +ℎ/2 state. A second Z magnet measures the same observable, gets +ℎ/2 with probability 1, and has nothing left to separate.
  • The spot does not stay put, though: the second magnet still pushes that one beam further from the centre. One spot, further out — not two.
  • This is repeatability, and it is what makes the word "measurement" mean anything: repeat it immediately and you get the same number.
  • In the sim's code this is literal — when the stage axis matches the atom's recorded axis, its spin value is left untouched.
03

How many spots, and how many atoms actually reach the screen?

A σz eigenstate is an equal superposition in the x basis — so an X magnet splits it exactly down the middle.
rate field

These two are the sim's real sliders — drag here and the apparatus follows. The chain itself is read live from Stage 1–3, so build a setup on the left and every number below follows it. Note the sim's own rules: moving Field Strength rebuilds the apparatus and zeroes the counters, while moving Emission Rate only clears the spot pattern.

  • Every branch is an independent ½. Multiply the ½s along a surviving path and you have the fraction of emitted atoms that land in that spot — exactly what the Emitted / Detected / Blocked counters measure.
  • Field strength changes no probability. It only scales how far apart the spots sit; the counts are identical at 0.05 T and 0.50 T.
How this sim turns the field slider into a spot position: a fixed displacement inside the gap, then free drift. It is a rendering rule with the right shape, not a calculation with real atomic constants.
04

If the ↓ beam was blocked, how does spin-down come back?

The deck's third arrangement, computed: Z with ↓ blocked, X with ← blocked, then Z. One eighth of the emitted atoms reach each final spot, and the screen panel shows where they land at B = 0.40 T.
Three independent halves. The last one is spin-down — the value that was blocked two stages earlier.
  • Nothing was "let through". The X magnet measured a different observable, and its output is a σx eigenstate — a state with no σz value at all.
  • Ask that state for σz and it answers ↑ or ↓ with probability ½ each. The third magnet creates the split; it does not uncover a hidden label.
  • Ordering matters, and that is the formal content: σx and σz do not commute, so no apparatus can pin both down at once.
  • Remove the X magnet and the regeneration stops dead — Z then Z gives one spot. The X stage is the eraser.
Why no clever ordering of magnets can rescue the blocked value.
05

What did this look like in 1922?

Order-of-magnitude estimate for a real apparatus, from the inputs shown. The sim itself never computes this — it renders a deflection proportional to its field slider.
  • Stern and Gerlach fired a silver beam through an inhomogeneous field and collected it on a plate. They expected a smear; they got two lines.
  • The gap between those lines is tiny — a tenth of a millimetre for the numbers above. Gerlach reportedly developed the plate with cigar smoke, whose sulphur darkened the silver enough to see.
  • The experiment was designed to test orbital space quantization; what it actually found was electron spin, two years before spin was proposed.
  • Cascaded Stern–Gerlach stages are still the standard picture of projective measurement — and the same physics runs in atomic-beam magnetometers and in spin-state preparation for atomic clocks.
Left: a classical moment would take every value of μz = μcosθ, giving a continuous band. Right: what the plate showed.
06

The traps they'll set for you

  • "The X magnet only hides the atom's z-label." After σx is measured there is no z-label to hide. The atom is in a σx eigenstate, and its σz value does not exist until a Z magnet makes it exist.
  • "Blocking ↓ removes spin-down atoms for good." It removes them at that stage. Put an X magnet after it and half of what comes out of the next Z magnet is spin-down again.
  • "Two magnets, four peaks — so each magnet measures two things." Each magnet splits whatever enters it in two. Two beams into an X magnet gives four out; one beam gives two.
  • "The spin points somewhere in between, and the magnet rounds it off." Then μz would take a continuum of values and the plate would show a band. It shows two spots.
  • "A stronger field gives a cleaner result." The field only spreads the spots apart. Every probability and ratio is unchanged — the counters do restart from zero, but only because moving that slider rebuilds the apparatus.
07

Ninety-second self-test

Set Field Strength to 0.00 T with a blocker in place. What do the counters do?

They restart from zero — touching Field Strength rebuilds the apparatus — and then climb at exactly the same rates as before. The deflection is proportional to B, so at 0.00 T every beam lands on top of the others at the centre of the screen; but each magnet still measures and the blocker still removes its half, so the Detected, Blocked and Final +/− rates are identical to 0.30 T. Geometry and statistics are separate things here.

Z with ↓ blocked, then X with ← blocked, then Z. Out of 800 emitted atoms, how many land in each spot?

One eighth each, so about 100 and 100, with 600 stopped by the two blockers. The chain is ½ (survive stage 1) × ½ (survive stage 2) × ½ (each outcome of stage 3) = 1/8. Counting noise is about √100 = 10 per spot.

Why does the third magnet split the beam when the second one, in the Z→Z arrangement, did not?

Because of what stands in front of it. In Z→Z the second magnet receives a σz eigenstate and repeats its own answer with probability 1. In Z→X→Z the third magnet receives a σx eigenstate, which is an equal superposition of |+z⟩ and |−z⟩ — so it splits 50/50. Same magnet, different incoming state.
