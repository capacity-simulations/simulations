About this simulation

A quantum particle on a ring (\(R=1\); natural units \(\hbar=1\), \(2m=1\)) in periodic eigenstates labeled by the integer \(n\).

Physics

Energy \(E_n \propto n^2\), with lecture notation \(\psi_n(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi R}}e^{inx/R}\). The plot around the ring uses the angular coordinate \(\phi=x/R\). For \(n\neq 0\), \(\pm n\) are degenerate in energy.

\[E_n = \frac{n^2\hbar^2}{2mR^2}\]
\[\psi_n(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi R}} e^{inx/R}\]
\[P(x)=|\psi_n(x)|^2=\frac{1}{2\pi R}\]
Visuals

Energy levels: ladder of states vs energy; the chosen \(n\) is highlighted.
Wavefunction: real (blue) and imaginary (green) parts vs \(\phi\), or the same data wrapped on the ring; optional \(| \psi |^2\) band (yellow). Panels can be maximized.

Controls

Quantum Number: slider for \(n\); degeneracy note below it. Display Mode: ring vs \(\phi\) plot toggle; Show Re part / Show Im part; Show Probability Density \(|\psi|^2\); Animate Time Evolution. Guided Inquiry

1 · The ring

Here’s our playground. A particle moves freely on a circle of radius R — no walls, no forces, but the wave must join up with itself after one full turn. The energy ladder shows you which energies are allowed; beside it, the real (blue) and imaginary (green) parts of ψ are drawn around the ring. The integer n counts the whole wavelengths that fit into one circumference.

2 · The classical bead

Let’s start before quantum mechanics. A bead slides on a frictionless circular wire, with nothing to push it or slow it. Which energies do you think the bead can have?

3 · Whole numbers only

Now back to the quantum ring. Here the label n is restricted to integers. What do you think imposes that restriction?

4 · The lowest rung

Here’s something the box never allowed. A particle in a box can never have zero energy: there ψ would vanish everywhere. But the ring’s ladder has a rung at E = 0. What do you think that state looks like?

5 · Reversing the sign

Now let’s play with the sign. n may be negative as well as positive. Put side by side, how do you think the states n = +2 and n = −2 compare?

6 · Where is the particle?

One last look, and it’s a subtle one. The yellow band is |ψ|², the probability of finding the particle at each point. Take |n| from 2 up to 5. What’s your guess — how does that band change?

The Physics

The particle on a circle · a complete revision sheet · no walls, and still a ladder

Nothing on a ring traps the particle — there is no wall anywhere to reflect it. The energies are a ladder all the same, because a wave that runs the whole way round has to come back to exactly the value it started with.

01

Why is there a ladder at all, when there are no walls?

Computed: the ring with its radius displaced by Re ψ, the sim's welcome-hero picture. At n = 3 the wave meets itself at the seam; at n = 2.5 it returns as −ψ and the red gap is the contradiction. (The sim's 3-D ring view displaces ψ vertically instead — the closure argument is the same.)
  • V = 0 the whole way round and there is nothing to reflect off. A classical bead on this wire could circle at any speed at all, with any energy down to zero.
  • The quantisation comes from single-valuedness: x and x + 2πR are the same point of the ring, so ψ must return to its own value — not merely to zero, as a wall would demand.
  • That closes the wave only when a whole number of wavelengths fits the circumference. The integer n counts them, and it is free to be negative or zero.
  • In numbers: at n = 3 the sim's Spatial Wavelength reads 2πR/3 ≈ 2.09 (R = 1), and three of those are exactly the 6.28 R circumference. A wave with λ = 2.51 R would fit 2.5 times and arrive back half a cycle out, as −ψ — the red gap in the figure.
  • The bottom rung is E = 0, and it is not a zero-point energy. At n = 0 the wave is the constant 1/√(2πR): non-zero, normalised, and closed onto itself trivially. The badge under the slider reads Non-Degenerate (n=0).
The whole problem is one boundary condition — and it is periodicity, not a wall.
Schrödinger's equation on the ring, and the only solutions periodicity permits. The plot's horizontal axis is x; with R = 1 the arc length and the angle φ are the same number.
02

Why do n and −n land on the same rung?

Fitting |n| waves fixes the wavelength; de Broglie turns it into momentum, and on a ring that momentum is an angular momentum.
  • |n| whole waves in one circumference means λn = 2πR/|n| — the Spatial Wavelength the sim prints in Current State.
  • Shorter wave, bigger momentum: pn = nħ/R, so Lz = nħ. The sign of n is the sense of circulation, nothing else.
  • Energy is p²/2m, which squares the sign away: E−n = E+n. Every rung except n = 0 carries two distinct states — the sim's Doubly Degenerate (±n) badge.
  • At t = 0, or with Animate Time Evolution off, the difference is still visible: Re ψ is identical for ±n while the green Im ψ flips sign. Let the phase run and the whole pattern travels the opposite way.
The sim's energy ladder, computed, at |n| = 5 — the state the guided inquiry ends on. Rung height goes as n², and the top rung is drawn in pink exactly as the sim draws the current level.

The magnitude law and the degeneracy in one line; the gap to the next rung grows as 2|n| + 1.
n speed

These are the sim's own Quantum Number and Time Speed sliders — drag here and the ring follows.

  • Double |n| → four times the energy, half the wavelength, twice the angular momentum. R is fixed at 1 here; it is a readout, not a slider.

04

If the wave is travelling, in what sense is the state stationary?

The whole time dependence is one rotating phase — and in |Ψ|² it multiplies by its own conjugate and cancels.
  • The sim draws two real curves: Re Ψ in blue, Im Ψ in green. They are one complex number seen twice, a quarter of a wavelength apart.
  • With Animate Time Evolution on, the whole pattern slides round the ring — towards +φ (rightwards on the flat plot) for n > 0, the other way for n < 0. That sliding is the factor e−iEnt/ħ.
  • Nothing measurable moves. |Ψn|² = (Re Ψ)² + (Im Ψ)² = (cos² + sin²)/2πR = 1/2πR — no x in it, no t in it. That is what "stationary state" means, and it is why the yellow band never twitches.
  • Time Speed is a viewing rate, not physics. It scales the clock the sim feeds into the phase; the energy readout, the ladder and the band are untouched by it.
Computed from the sim's own plotting code at t = 0, using its scale factor exactly: the band height is 1/√(2π) of the curve amplitude. At n = 5 the curves wiggle five times faster and the amber band is at precisely the same height.
05

Where does nature put an electron on a ring?

  • Benzene. Six π electrons delocalised over a carbon ring of radius ≈ 0.139 nm. They fill n = 0 with two and n = ±1 with four, so the first empty rung is n = ±2.
  • That gap is ΔE ≈ 5.9 eV, a photon near 210 nm. Benzene really does absorb hard in the deep ultraviolet, with bands around 180–200 nm — not bad for a first-order estimate containing no chemistry at all.
  • The same ladder gives aromatic rings their ring current: in a magnetic field the delocalised electrons circulate, and the field they produce is what shifts the NMR signals of nearby protons.
  • Scale it up and the ladder collapses. The same free electron on a ring of radius 50 nm has a first rung near 15 µeV — about 0.2 K. Mesoscopic metal rings at millikelvin temperatures are exactly this problem.
  • Thread a magnetic flux through such a ring and it carries a persistent current that does not decay. The flux splits the ±n pair of ch 02, so one circulation wins; with no flux the +n and −n currents cancel.
Computed from En = n²ħ²/2mR² with R = 0.139 nm: the six π electrons, the filled rungs, and the first allowed jump.
06

The traps they'll set for you

  • "A ring is just a box bent round." A box forces ψ to vanish at two walls; a ring forces ψ to equal itself after one turn. That one difference removes the zero-point energy, admits n = 0 and negative n, and wipes out every node.
  • "n = −3 sits lower than n = +3." The energy goes as n², which is blind to the sign, so both label the same rung. What the sign changes is the direction of travel — and with it the angular momentum Lz = nħ.
  • "Crank |n| up and the yellow band grows peaks." |ψn|² = 1/2πR at every n and every point. The blue and green curves wiggle faster; their squares still add to a constant, because cos² + sin² = 1.
  • "The wave is moving, so it can't be a stationary state." "Stationary" is a statement about |Ψ|², not about Re Ψ. The travelling pattern is pure phase — nothing you could measure changes with time.
07

Ninety-second self-test

What does the n = 0 state look like — and why is it allowed here but not in a box?

ψ₀ = 1/√(2πR), a non-zero constant all the way round, with E = 0, Lz = 0 and no wavelength to speak of (the sim prints "∞ (uniform)"). It closes onto itself trivially, so periodicity has no objection. A box instead forces ψ = 0 at both walls, where a constant is not an option, so its lowest state costs π²ħ²/2mL². A ring has no zero-point energy — and n = 0 is the one rung with a single state.

Double the radius and double n. What happens to the energy and the wavelength?

Neither changes. En = n²ħ²/2mR² depends on n²/R², and λn = 2πR/|n| on R/|n| — doubling both leaves each alone. This sim fixes R = 1, so you can only test the n half of it: doubling n on its own quadruples the energy and halves the wavelength.

The blue and green curves travel round the ring, yet the yellow band never twitches. Which one tells you the state is stationary?

The band. Ψn(x,t) = ψn(x) e−iEnt/ħ: the phase rotates, which slides Re Ψ and Im Ψ round the ring at angular speed ωn/n. In |Ψ|² that phase meets its own conjugate and cancels, leaving 1/2πR with no x and no t. Only |Ψ|² is measurable, so nothing observable evolves — the travelling curves are bookkeeping.
