About Simulation

This simulation models a 1D Quantum Harmonic Oscillator in units where \(\hbar = 1\), with user-controlled mass \(m\) and angular frequency \(\omega\).

  • Harmonic Potential: Displays the parabolic potential \(V(x)=\tfrac{1}{2}m\omega^2x^2\) and the corresponding energy levels.
  • Wavefunction: Visualizes the real and imaginary parts of the time-dependent wavefunction ψ(x,t).
  • Probability Density: Shows |ψ(x,t)|², representing the particle's position probability distribution.

Use the right sidebar to adjust the quantum state n, mass m, and angular frequency \(\omega\) to observe how the eigenstate width and energy spacing change. The animation-speed control only changes how quickly the plotted phase advances on screen.

The Physics

The quantum harmonic oscillator · a complete revision sheet · one particle in a parabolic well

A quantum oscillator isn't a mass swinging on a spring. It is a standing wave in a parabolic bowl — which is why its energies climb in perfectly equal steps, and why the lowest step is never zero.

01

Why is the energy ladder perfectly evenly spaced?

The sim's own Equations box: a bowl that keeps rising, not a box with walls.
  • There is no fixed width here. A box fixes L and the wave must fit inside it; a parabola lets each state pick the width that suits its own energy, out to the turning points the sim draws as blue arrows.
  • Count the orbit in phase space: a classical orbit of energy E encloses an area 2πE/ω, which is proportional to E. Each extra rung adds one quantum h of that area, so equal area steps mean equal energy steps. (Semiclassical counting — for the parabola alone it happens to be exact.)
  • So En+1 − En = ħω on every rung. The box spreads as n²; an atom's levels crowd together; only the parabola gives a uniform ladder.
  • This sim's n slider starts at 0, not 1. The bottom rung is E₀ = ½ħω — the zero-point energy, which chapter 03 shows is the smallest energy the uncertainty principle allows.
The result the sim's Energy readout prints: one quantum per rung, starting half a quantum above the floor.
The sim's top panel, computed at its defaults (m = ω = ħ = 1): the parabola V = ½mω²x², all eleven rungs the n slider can reach, and the n = 3 level with the turning-point arrows the sim draws in blue.
02

What shape is each rung, and why do the tails leak out?

Exactly what the sim's psiN() evaluates: one Gaussian, one Hermite polynomial.
  • Every state is the same Gaussian envelope e−x²/2x₀² multiplied by the Hermite polynomial Hn. The Gaussian sets the size; the polynomial puts in the wiggles.
  • ψn crosses zero exactly n times, so |ψn|² shows n + 1 peaks. At n = 0 there are no crossings at all — one Gaussian hump centred on x = 0. Even n is symmetric, odd n is antisymmetric, and either way ⟨x⟩ = 0.
  • There is only one length in the problem: x₀ = √(ħ/mω), the sim's Osc. length readout. Raise m or ω and every curve on screen narrows by the same factor.
  • The tails run past the turning-point arrows, into the region where E < V and a classical mass could never go. They fall off like a Gaussian but never reach zero at any finite x.
The sim's ψ and |ψ|² panels, computed at m = ω = 1: the ground state on the left, n = 10 on the right. The amber dashed curve is the classical time-average density for the same energy — added here for comparison, and clipped where it diverges at the turning points. The sim itself never draws it.
03

What do the numbers actually say?

The three lengths the sim reports, plus the uncertainty product they imply.
n m ω

These are the sim's own Physics-controls sliders — drag here and the three panels follow. The sim works in ħ = 1 units, so every number is dimensionless.

Why the ladder cannot start at zero — and why the ground state sits exactly on the uncertainty bound.
  • Δx Δp = (n + ½)ħ exactly, for every state. Only n = 0 reaches the floor ħ/2; every higher rung is further from it, by the odd factor 2n + 1.
  • Raise ω and E goes up linearly while x₀ shrinks as 1/√ω — the bowl steepens and squeezes the wave. Raise m and E is unchanged but x₀ shrinks the same way, which is why the m and ω sliders look different in the top panel and identical in the bottom two.
04

If the state is stationary, what is moving on screen?

The whole time dependence is one phase factor — and it cancels in |Ψ|².
  • The middle panel plots Re Ψ (blue) and Im Ψ (pink), not |Ψ|. Both share the same envelope ψn(x); the phase factor just pours amplitude out of one and into the other.
  • The bottom panel is |ψn(x)|² and never moves: the phase multiplies by its own conjugate and cancels. That is precisely what "stationary state" means — and it is why the sim's probability panel has no t in it at all.
  • The rotation rate is En/ħ, so higher rungs turn faster: at the same ω, n = 10 rotates 21× faster than n = 0, because E₁₀/E₀ = 10.5/0.5.
  • Animation speed is a viewing rate, not physics. It multiplies the on-screen phase only; the Energy readout, the turning points and |ψ|² are untouched. (Motion — a wave packet that actually sloshes — needs a superposition of rungs, which this sim does not build: it shows one eigenstate at a time.)
05

Where does nature actually run this?

Taylor-expand any smooth minimum and the first surviving term is a parabola. That is why this one problem is everywhere.
  • A C–O bond is this sim with real numbers: k ≈ 1.9×10³ N m⁻¹ and reduced mass 6.86 u give ħω ≈ 0.27 eV. The stretch absorbs infrared near 2143 cm⁻¹ — the band that makes CO visible to a spectrometer.
  • Its zero-point energy is ≈ 0.13 eV and its zero-point spread is x₀/√2 ≈ 3 pm on a 113 pm bond. At room temperature kT ≈ 0.026 eV is ten times smaller than ħω, so roughly one molecule in 30 000 is excited — the vibration is frozen in n = 0. (Boltzmann estimate; the sim models neither temperature nor the bond's anharmonicity.)
  • Zero-point energy is not bookkeeping. It is why liquid helium never freezes under its own vapour pressure: about 25 atmospheres are needed to solidify it even at absolute zero.
  • The same ladder counts phonons in a crystal and photons in a cavity mode. Every one of them keeps a ½ħω it can never spend — the same ½ you read at n = 0 here.
Carbon monoxide's bond, computed from its measured constants (ωe = 2170 cm⁻¹, re = 1.128 Å, De = 11.1 eV): near the minimum the real Morse curve and the parabola are the same curve, and the harmonic rungs sit ħω = 0.269 eV apart with the n = 0 floor at 0.134 eV. Real CO levels creep closer together higher up; the sim models the parabola only.
06

The traps they'll set for you

  • "The lowest state has zero energy — the quantum spring can sit still." Exact rest would fix x = 0 and p = 0 at once, and Δx Δp ≥ ħ/2 forbids it. The floor is E₀ = ½ħω, and the sim's Energy readout never drops below it.
  • "Higher levels spread apart, like the box." That is the box, where E ∝ n². Here E is linear in n and the gap is ħω on every rung — measure the drawn ladder and the spacing never changes.
  • "The bottom panel is the particle oscillating." In a single eigenstate |ψn|² is completely time-independent; the green curve is frozen by construction. Only Re Ψ and Im Ψ move, and they are not probabilities.
  • "The particle is most often found near the ends, like a classical mass." True classically, and true again at large n — but |ψ₀|² peaks at the centre, the exact opposite. The classical picture only returns as n grows.
07

Ninety-second self-test

Set n = 0. What is the energy, and why can't it be zero?

E₀ = ½ħω — at the sim's defaults, 0.50. Zero energy would need both ⟨x²⟩ = 0 and ⟨p²⟩ = 0, i.e. Δx = Δp = 0, which violates Δx Δp ≥ ħ/2. Trading the two terms off gives a minimum of exactly ½ħω at Δx = √(ħ/2mω), and the Gaussian ground state sits precisely there.

Double ω and halve m. What happens to E₃, to x₀, and to the level spacing?

E₃ = 3.5ħω doubles, and so does the spacing ħω. But x₀ = √(ħ/mω) depends on the product mω, which is unchanged — so every curve keeps exactly the same width while the energies double. (The sim's sliders only span 0.7–1.2, so you can verify the trend, not the full factor of two.)

The middle panel is alive and the bottom one is frozen. What is the bottom panel plotting, and why doesn't it move?

It plots |ψn(x)|², the probability density. The full state is Ψn(x,t) = ψn(x) e−iEnt/ħ; taking the modulus squared multiplies the phase by its conjugate and cancels it, leaving no t. The blue and pink curves are Re Ψ and Im Ψ, which do carry the phase — so they rotate into one another at En/ħ while the density stays put.
