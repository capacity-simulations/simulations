About this simulation

This simulation compares a classical harmonic oscillator with a quantum coherent-state wavepacket in the same harmonic potential V(x) = 1/2 mω²x². The left column follows Newton's equation of motion; the right column follows the Schrödinger equation for the same m, ω, and amplitude A.

Use the shared sliders to change m, ω, and A. The coherent-state center satisfies ⟨x⟩(t) = A cos(ωt), so it follows the same trajectory as the classical particle. The nonzero tails beyond ±A show probability in a classically forbidden region, not escape from the trap.

The Physics

The harmonic trap · a complete revision sheet · a mass on a spring beside a quantum wavepacket

The wavepacket on the right is not a blurred-out ball being pushed around. It is a coherent state: its centre obeys Newton's equation exactly, and its width was fixed once by m and ω and never changes again.

01

Why does the packet keep pace with the mass?

Computed from the sim's defaults m = ω = 1, A = 2: the shared trap above, |ψ(x,t)|² below at t = 0 (centred on +A) and a quarter period later (centred on x = 0). The blue dots are the classical mass at those two instants.
  • Both columns share one potential, V(x) = ½mω²x², and one set of sliders. Only the description differs.
  • On the left, the restoring force is linear in x, which is what makes x(t) = A cos ωt a pure cosine.
  • On the right, Ehrenfest's result says m d²⟨x⟩/dt² = −m ω²⟨x⟩ exactly when the force is linear — so the mean position obeys the classical equation with no approximation.
  • Same m, ω and A on both sides means the same initial condition, so ⟨x⟩(t) = A cos ωt: the packet's peak and the mass leave +A together and cross x = 0 together.
The left column: force, equation of motion, solution.
The right column: the Schrödinger equation, and the mean it forces to move classically.
02

What decides how wide the packet is?

The density the sim plots, and its standard deviation — the Δx = σ readout.
  • The right-hand canvas draws |ψ(x,t)|², a probability density — not ψ itself, and not a particle.
  • Its width is σ = √(ħ/2mω). Only the product mω appears: a heavier particle or a stiffer trap both narrow it, by the same rule.
  • A is missing from σ. Amplitude moves the packet; it does not squeeze it. That is the point the sim's third inquiry card tests.
  • Nothing here depends on t either. A coherent state keeps its shape as it swings — unlike a free packet, which spreads without limit.
Computed: |ψ|² at mω = 1 and mω = 4. Both enclose probability 1, so the narrower one is taller.

04

If both columns agree, what is quantum about the right one?

The mean energy of the packet — the classical energy plus a floor that never goes away.
Probability past the turning points, and the width–momentum trade the packet sits exactly on.
  • Three things differ, and only three. The packet has a width, it has tails beyond ±A, and its mean energy sits ½ħω above the classical one.
  • Zero-point energy is not the energy at t = 0. ⟨H⟩ = Ecl + ½ħω holds at every instant and at every amplitude; shrink A and ⟨H⟩ falls towards ½ħω, never towards 0.
  • The orange tails are ordinary probability in a region a classical particle of energy Ecl could not reach. At the moment the packet is centred on x = 0 that fraction is erfc(A√(mω/ħ)); at the moment it reaches +A, exactly half of it lies past the +A marker — a Gaussian is symmetric about its own centre.
  • A coherent state is the minimum-uncertainty state: Δx = √(ħ/2mω) and Δp = √(mωħ/2), so ΔxΔp = ħ/2 exactly — the smallest the relation allows. (Standard result for this state; the sim shows Δx only.)
05

Where does nature run this?

  • The honest measure of "how classical" an oscillator is: n̄ = Ecl/ħω, the number of quanta the swing is worth. The zero-point share is 1/(2n̄).
  • A CO molecule at room temperature carries n̄ ≈ 3×10⁻⁵ quanta on average — a thermal occupation, not a swing — so it sits in its ground state and effectively all of its ⟨H⟩ is zero-point energy.
  • A 1 g pendulum swinging 1 cm once a second has n̄ ≈ 3×10²⁷. The ½ħω is still there; it is one part in 10²⁸ of the energy, which is why nobody trips over it.
  • This sim runs at n̄ = 2 on its defaults — deliberately deep in the regime where the quantum correction is a quarter of the classical energy and you can see it.
Computed: n̄ = Ecl/ħω on a log scale, from a molecule to a car suspension. For the two thermal entries n̄ is the mean thermal occupation at 300 K; the cooled-ion figure is a typical experimental value.
06

The traps they'll set for you

  • "The orange tails mean the particle is leaking out of the trap." Nothing escapes: the state is bound and |ψ|² stays normalised for ever. The shading only marks where a classical particle with energy Ecl could not go.
  • "The packet spreads as it travels." A free packet does. This one is a coherent state in a harmonic trap, so σ = √(ħ/2mω) at every instant — the sim's Δx readout never moves while the packet does.
  • "Make the amplitude small and the physics becomes classical." Backwards. Small A means small n̄ = mωA²/2ħ, and the zero-point share 1/(2n̄) grows. Classical behaviour is the large-n̄ limit.
  • "⟨x⟩ is where the particle is." ⟨x⟩ is the mean of a distribution of width σ. A single position measurement returns one value of x, typically σ away from the centre — the sim draws the distribution, not the outcome.
07

Ninety-second self-test

Let A go to zero. What is left on each side?

On the left, nothing: the mass sits at x = 0 with Ecl = 0. On the right, the ground state — the packet stays centred at x = 0, still σ = √(ħ/2mω) wide, with ⟨H⟩ = ½ħω. The sim's amplitude slider stops at A = 0.5, so you can approach this but not reach it.

Double m and halve ω. What happens to σ, Ecl and ⟨H⟩?

σ is unchanged: it depends on the product mω, and 2m × ω/2 leaves it alone. Ecl = ½mω²A² halves, because ω² falls by 4 while m doubles. ⟨H⟩ = Ecl + ½ħω halves twice over — both terms are down by a factor 2 — so the zero-point share of the total is unchanged too.

The two columns never disagree about position. So why is the right one not just the left one?

Ehrenfest's theorem is exact here only for the mean: ⟨x⟩ follows A cos ωt because the force is linear in x. Everything else is different — the state has a width σ, it carries probability beyond the classical turning points, and its mean energy is ½ħω above the classical value. Agreement about ⟨x⟩ is a special property of the quadratic potential, not a statement that the particle is classical.
