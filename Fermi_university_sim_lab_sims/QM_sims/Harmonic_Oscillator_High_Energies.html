The Physics

The quantum harmonic oscillator · a complete revision sheet · from the ground state to the classical limit

The classical oscillator is not what is left when quantum mechanics switches off. It is what the quantum answer looks like once the rungs are too close together, and the ripples too fine, to resolve.

01

Where does the ladder come from, and why are its rungs evenly spaced?

The sim's top panel, computed: V(x) = ½x² with the allowed energies drawn across it, one rung selected in gold and its turning points dashed.
  • The well is a parabola, V(x) = ½mω²x² — in the sim's natural units ħ = m = ω = 1, so V(x) = ½x². Every horizontal rung on that panel is an allowed energy; the gold one is the state you have selected.
  • Solve Schrödinger's equation inside it and only a discrete set survives: Eₙ = (n + ½)ħω, with n running 0 to 60 on the Energy level slider.
  • The gap between neighbours is ħω at every n — the Quantum Numbers readout prints 1.000 whatever you do. A parabola widens with energy at exactly the rate that keeps the gaps equal.
  • The ladder does not start at zero. E₀ = ½ħω is the zero-point energy: a confined oscillator cannot be brought to rest at the bottom of the well.
  • A rung ends where it meets the parabola, at the classical turning points x = ±√(2n+1) — the sim's ξtp readout, and the dashed verticals on all three panels.
The potential the sim draws, and the equation every ψₙ has to satisfy inside it.
The ladder, and the eigenfunctions — generated by the same stable recurrence the sim's code runs.
02

Where does a classical mass actually spend its time?

Time in a strip is its width over the speed there — and the spring is slowest at the ends.
  • A mass on a spring is fastest crossing the centre and comes to rest at each end of its swing, so the time it spends in a strip of width dx goes as 1/|v|.
  • That is the orange Pclassical(x) curve on the sim's probability panel: a shallow minimum at x = 0, rising towards both turning points.
  • The divergence at ±xtp is integrable — the total probability is still exactly 1 — but it is infinite on paper, which is why the sim clips the drawing near the ends rather than letting it run off the panel.
  • Pclassical(x) contains no ħ. It is the answer Newton would give, laid over the quantum one for comparison.
Computed: the mass photographed at thirteen equally spaced times, with its speed to scale — the strobe bunches up exactly where the orange curve peaks.
03

What do the numbers say at rung n?

The absolute gap never changes; the gap measured against the energy is what collapses.
n speed

These are the sim's own two sliders — drag here and the sim follows. Animation speed is a viewing control: it sets how fast the phase is played back and changes no number on this page.

  • Climb the ladder and the absolute step never moves: Eₙ₊₁ − Eₙ = ħω at n = 0 and at n = 60 alike.
  • What collapses is the relative step, ΔE/Eₙ = 1/(n + ½) — the sim's Classical Limit readout. It is 2 at the ground state and 0.0165 sixty rungs up.
  • The state spreads as √(2n+1) while the node count grows as n, so the ripples get finer twice over: more of them, packed into a range that grows only as a square root.
04

Sixty rungs up: how does the comb become the curve?

The sim's probability panel, computed at both ends of its slider: green |ψₙ(x)|², orange Pclassical(x), each panel on the sim's own axes.
  • At n = 0 the two pictures disagree completely: |ψ₀(x)|² is a single Gaussian hump, tallest exactly where the classical mass is found least often.
  • By n = 60 the green curve is a comb of 61 ripples filling the orange envelope, swinging between nearly zero and about twice Pclassical — and its local average is the orange curve. That is the correspondence principle.
  • Nothing became classical on the way up. The ripples are still there; they are simply finer than any measurement can resolve, and averaging over one ripple is what a real detector does.
  • A CO molecule at room temperature sits on rung n = 0: ħω ≈ 0.27 eV is ten times kBT, so its ladder shows up plainly in an infrared spectrum.
  • A seconds pendulum, 1 kg swinging through 1 cm, sits at n ≈ 1.5×10³⁰. Its rungs are still ħω apart — one rung is 7×10⁻³¹ of its energy, which is why its energy looks continuous.
The WKB form away from the turning points: a rapid cos², riding on exactly the classical distribution.

05

If |ψₙ(x)|² never moves, what is moving on screen?

One phase factor is the whole time dependence — and it cancels in the probability density.
  • The middle panel draws Re Ψₙ in blue and Im Ψₙ in pink: the same fixed shape ψₙ(x), multiplied by cos and by −sin of Eₙt/ħ. The amplitude breathes and the two curves trade places; the shape never changes. The faint gold envelope is ±|ψₙ(x)|.
  • The bottom panel draws |ψₙ(x)|² and is completely still, because the phase factor cancels against its own conjugate. That is what stationary state means — and why the sim's ∫|Ψ|²dx readout sits at 1.0000.
  • One full turn of the phase takes Tphase = 2π/Eₙ, and it gets shorter as you climb: 0.155 at n = 40, 0.104 at n = 60. The Animation speed slider only sets how fast that clock is played back.
  • Tcl = 2π/ω = 6.283 is a different thing entirely, and it never changes with n or with amplitude. The classical frequency comes back not from Tphase but from the difference of two neighbouring phases: (Eₙ₊₁ − Eₙ)/ħ = ω.
06

The traps they'll set for you

  • "The probability density sloshes back and forth like the classical mass." For an energy eigenstate |ψₙ(x)|² has no t in it at all. The blue and pink curves swap; the green curve never moves.
  • "At high n the rungs merge into a continuum, and quantum mechanics switches off." The spacing is exactly ħω at every n, pendulum included. Only the ratio ΔE/Eₙ = 1/(n + ½) shrinks — the rungs become unresolvable, not absent.
  • "Eₙ = nħω." The ½ is not a rounding convention. It is the zero-point energy, and dropping it makes E₀ = 0 — a mass sitting perfectly still at the bottom of the well, which the uncertainty principle forbids.
  • "The particle can never be found beyond the turning points." The green curve is small but non-zero outside ±√(2n+1). At n = 0 about 16% of the probability lies in the classically forbidden region.
07

Ninety-second self-test

Set n = 0. Can the oscillator sit still at the bottom of the well?

No. E₀ = ½ħω is the lowest rung there is, and |ψ₀(x)|² is a Gaussian of finite width. Average position and average momentum are both zero, but ⟨x²⟩ and ⟨p²⟩ are not — pinning the mass at the bottom would need both to vanish at once, which the uncertainty principle forbids. The zero point is the cheapest compromise between the two.

Go from n = 30 to n = 60. What happens to Eₙ, to the gap, to ΔE/Eₙ and to the turning points?

Eₙ goes from 30.5 to 60.5 ħω — just under double. The gap to the next rung stays exactly ħω. ΔE/Eₙ halves, 0.0328 to 0.0165. The turning points move from ±√61 = ±7.81 to ±√121 = ±11.00, a factor of 1.41, because they grow only as √(2n+1).

At n = 60 the green curve drops to zero sixty times; the orange one never does. Do they contradict each other?

No — the correspondence principle is about local averages, not about pointwise values. The ripples at n = 60 are about 0.36 apart, far finer than any real position measurement, and averaging the green curve over one ripple gives back the orange one exactly. The nodes survive; they just stop being resolvable.
