Guided Inquiry

1 · The bound electron

A single electron is bound to a proton. Unlike a particle in a box it is held by the Coulomb attraction, so every allowed energy is negative: En = −13.6/n² eV for n = 1, 2, 3, …, with E = 0 the ionisation limit. Moving between two levels, the electron emits or absorbs one photon carrying exactly the energy difference, and that photon’s wavelength is recorded on the strip.

2 · The classical atom

In classical physics the orbiting electron radiates, spiralling inward through every orbit size on the way. What light would a gas of such atoms give off?

3 · The three series

A series is the set of transitions ending on one fixed lower level. Lyman ends on n = 1, and every one of its lines is ultraviolet. Which series lands in the visible band?

4 · Above the seventh rung

The diagram draws levels up to n = 7. How many bound levels lie between n = 7 and the ionisation limit E = 0?

5 · Light through cold gas

White light passes through cold hydrogen gas, whose electrons all sit in n = 1. What does the transmitted spectrum show?

The Physics

The hydrogen spectrum · a complete revision sheet · one electron, one ladder, one photon per step

A spectral line is not a colour hydrogen owns. It is the exact size of one step on a ladder the electron is allowed to stand on — and nothing else in the atom is allowed to change.

01

Why sharp lines instead of a rainbow?

The sim's energy ladder, computed: En = −13.6/n² on a linear energy axis, with the top 1.6 eV magnified. Each arrow is drawn in the colour the sim gives that line on its strip.
  • A classical electron on a shrinking orbit would radiate on the way in at every frequency — a smooth continuum. Hydrogen does not do that.
  • Only the energies En = −13.6/n² eV are allowed. Every one is negative: the electron is bound, and E = 0 is the escape line the sim draws in red.
  • A jump between two rungs releases one photon carrying the whole gap. There are no half-steps, so there are no in-between colours.
  • The ladder has no top rung. The sim draws seven because seven fits; the rungs keep coming and pile up at E = 0.
The whole spectrum comes out of this one formula.
02

Why do the lines fall into families?

One gap, one photon: the energy difference fixes the wavelength completely.
  • A series is every jump that lands on the same lower level — exactly what the sim's three series buttons run.
  • Lyman (→ n = 1): the gap is at least 10.20 eV, so λ ≤ 121.6 nm. Every line is ultraviolet.
  • Balmer (→ n = 2): 1.89 to 3.12 eV across the sim's rungs, i.e. 656.5 down to 397.1 nm — the visible lines.
  • Paschen (→ n = 3): at most 1.51 eV, i.e. 1876 down to 1005 nm — all infrared.
  • Within a series the lines crowd towards the series limit, the wavelength of a jump from n → ∞.
The Rydberg form, and where each family ends.

03

What does one jump actually cost?

The sim's own constants — the readouts below are computed from these.
From n To n

These are the sim's own From n and To n selectors — drag here and the sim follows. It re-validates the pair on every change, so an impossible choice is snapped back: in emission the starting level must be the higher one.

  • Only the two rung heights matter. Every jump between the same pair gives the same photon, whatever route you imagine.
  • Energy and wavelength run opposite ways: double ΔE, halve λ. The biggest drop the sim can make is 7 → 1 at 13.32 eV, or 93.1 nm.
04

Emission or absorption — is it different physics?

One ladder, read in either direction.
  • Drop a rung and a photon leaves; climb the same rung and a photon of exactly the same energy is taken in. The wavelength is identical either way.
  • That is why the sim's two strips line up: bright lines on a dark strip for emission, dark bars cut out of a continuum for absorption, at the same positions.
  • Cold hydrogen sits in n = 1, so it can only absorb the Lyman gaps. A cold cloud in front of a star prints the Lyman set into the starlight.
  • What the sim does not model: how likely each jump is. It records one line per level pair and fades them all up to the same brightness, whereas a real lamp gives Hα far more strongly than Hδ.
05

Where does nature run this?

Computed from the same four Balmer wavelengths the sim fires: 656.5, 486.3, 434.2 and 410.3 nm, now missing from a continuum. This is a star's spectrum in one picture.
  • Hα at 656 nm is why hydrogen gas glows pinkish-red: emission nebulae are lit almost entirely by this one Balmer line.
  • Lyman-α at 121.6 nm is the strongest line in the ultraviolet universe — and invisible from the ground, because the atmosphere absorbs it. Space telescopes were built partly for it.
  • Take 13.6 eV from a ground-state atom and the electron is gone: any photon with λ ≤ 91.2 nm ionises it. That wavelength is the edge of an ionised hydrogen region around a hot star.
  • The atom is small because a₀ is small — but an n = 7 atom is already 49 times wider than a ground-state one, which is why highly excited atoms are so fragile.

06

The traps they'll set for you

  • "The ladder stops at n = 7." Seven is how many rungs the diagram draws. −13.6/n² is negative for every n, so every n is a bound level; they never run out, they just crowd towards E = 0.
  • "The electron is a ball going round one of those rings." The rings are bookkeeping for n, not a map. Two honest warnings: the true Bohr radii go as n²a₀ while the sim spaces its seven rings evenly, and the real atom has no orbit at all — only a cloud whose energy is the En above.
  • "Higher up means a bigger jump." The opposite: the gaps shrink as n grows. 2 → 1 releases 10.20 eV; 7 → 6 releases 0.100 eV, over a hundred times less.
  • "Every line in the strip is equally bright." The sim ramps each recorded line to the same brightness so you can see it. Real intensities depend on how many atoms sit in each level and how likely each jump is — physics this sim does not model.
07

Ninety-second self-test

Push nhi to infinity with nlo = 2. What wavelength comes out, and what does it mean?

ΔE → 13.6/4 = 3.40 eV, so λ → 1240/3.40 = 364.7 nm — the Balmer series limit, in the near ultraviolet. It is the photon released when a free electron with no kinetic energy is captured straight into n = 2, and every Balmer line crowds up to it from the red side.

3 → 2 gives 656 nm. Without a calculator, is 4 → 2 longer or shorter, and roughly by how much?

Shorter. The gap grows from 13.6(1/4 − 1/9) = 1.89 eV to 13.6(1/4 − 1/16) = 2.55 eV, a factor of 1.35, and λ goes as 1/ΔE — so 656/1.35 ≈ 486 nm. That is Hβ, the blue-green line the sim fires next in the Balmer run.

Cold hydrogen sits between you and a star. Why are the dark lines at exactly the emission wavelengths?

Because absorption and emission use the same rungs. A photon is absorbed only if its energy matches a gap, so the gas removes precisely the energies it would have emitted. Same ΔE, same λ — only the direction of the jump, and therefore bright line versus dark gap, changes.
