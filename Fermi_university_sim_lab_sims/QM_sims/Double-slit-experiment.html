Guided Inquiry

1 · The apparatus

Here’s the setup. A source emits monochromatic light of wavelength λ towards a barrier with two slits, separated by d and each of width a. A detector screen stands a distance L behind the barrier, and every particle that gets through is recorded right where it lands.

2 · Bullets through two slits

Let’s start with the classical picture. Think of the incoming particles as tiny bullets that travel in straight lines, each passing through one slit or the other. After many have landed, what do you think the screen will show?

3 · The real source

Now let’s swap in the real thing. The bullets are replaced by the real source: monochromatic light of wavelength λ, both slits open. After many arrivals, what do you think the screen shows now?

4 · One dot at a time

Look closely at how the pattern builds. Each arrival is a single localised dot, and the bands only emerge after thousands of dots have landed. So what do you think keeps the dots out of the dark regions?

5 · Halving the separation

One last experiment. Both slits stay open and λ, a and L are unchanged. The slit separation d is about to be halved, from 1600 nm to 800 nm. What do you think happens to the spacing between neighbouring bright bands?

The Physics

The double-slit experiment · a complete revision sheet · amplitudes, not beams

The bands are not two beams of light overlapping. They are one particle's amplitude arriving by two routes — added first, and only then squared.

01

Why do two slits give bands where two bullets give mounds?

The sim's own detection chart, computed at its defaults (d = 1600 nm, a = 490 nm, λ = 525 nm, L = 4000 nm). Dashed lines mark the five band centres.
  • Bullets go through one slit or the other. The screen just adds the two one-slit distributions, P₁ + P₂ — the orange dashed curve the Classical Pattern button draws, modelled here as two smooth lobes from a broad spray. Nothing anywhere cancels.
  • The real source has an amplitude at every point. ψ₁ and ψ₂ both reach the same x, they add as complex numbers, and only the sum gets squared.
  • Squaring the sum leaves a cross term that changes sign across the screen. That term is the entire difference between the two curves above.
  • The cross term adds nothing to the total count: it moves arrivals out of the dark places and into the bright ones.
Add probabilities, or add amplitudes and then square — the whole experiment is that choice.
02

Where do the bright bands fall?

The sim's geometry at its defaults, drawn to scale. The amber stub is the extra distance Δ that the light from the far slit must cover; at this point it is exactly one λ.
  • Everything hangs on the path difference Δ = r₂ − r₁, exactly the quantity the sim calls pathDiff. Where |Δ| is a whole number of wavelengths the two amplitudes arrive in step; where it is a half-integer they cancel.
  • This sim computes r₁ and r₂ exactly, from Pythagoras — it never assumes the screen is far away.
  • The textbook shortcuts d sin θ = mλ and spacing ≈ λL/d come from letting L ≫ d, x. At the sim's defaults L/d is only 2.5, so they are estimates, not answers.
  • At the defaults, |Δ| = λ exactly at x = 1414 nm; d sin θ = λ predicts 1389 nm and λL/d predicts 1313 nm. The visible peak sits a little further in, at 1362 nm, because the one-slit envelope is already falling there.
  • Halve d and the bands spread. Δ grows more slowly with x, so you must go further out to collect each whole wavelength.
The sim's own path difference, and the condition for a bright or a dark band.
The far-field shortcuts — useful, and only as good as L ≫ d, x.
03

What sets the height of each band?

Exactly what the sim's green theory curve is: interference times a one-slit envelope.
d a λ L

These are the sim's real sliders — drag here and the apparatus follows. The sim keeps d > a, and λ is locked while Classical Pattern is on, because bullets have no wavelength.

  • d sets the spacing, a sets the envelope. Two independent knobs: d says where the bands are, a says how far out they are still bright.
  • The envelope has zeros only when a > λ. At the defaults a/λ = 0.93, so there are no zeros at all — the envelope just sags, which is why the outer bands are dim rather than missing.
  • Drop λ until a/λ > 2 and the sinc² zeros appear; when d/a is a whole number, a zero lands on a maximum and that order goes missing.
  • The expanding rings in the 3D view are a Huygens picture, not a time-dependent solution: each time a wavefront reaches the screen the sim draws 150 trial positions and keeps the ones the curve above accepts.
Where the envelope vanishes, and when it swallows an interference order.
04

If every arrival is one dot, what is interfering?

Close S2 and the second amplitude — and the whole cross term — is gone.
  • Each detection is one whole dot in one place. Nothing splits, and nothing arrives smeared out. The bands are a statement about where dots are likely, built up over thousands of them.
  • What passes through both slits is the amplitude. |ψ₁ + ψ₂|² is one particle's own probability, so a single particle interferes with itself — the arrivals are independent, and slowing the source down does not wash the bands out.
  • Closing a slit is not shading the screen. It deletes an amplitude, so the cross term disappears and what is left is the single-slit envelope centred on the open slit.
  • That is why some places get fewer dots with both slits open: at the first dark fringe the two-slit curve is essentially zero, where S1 alone would deliver a substantial fraction of the central brightness.
  • The sim has no which-path detector. Closing a slit is the only way it can remove a route — it does not model watching a particle go by.
05

Where does nature run this?

  • This sim runs light: λ is in nanometres and the beam colour follows it, greying out below 380 nm. Matter does the same thing with the de Broglie wavelength λ = h/p.
  • A 50 eV electron has λ ≈ 0.17 nm and a room-temperature neutron about the same — atomic spacing. That is why crystals, not machined slits, are the usual gratings for them.
  • C₆₀ molecules at 200 m/s have λ ≈ 2.8 pm and still produce bands through a nanofabricated grating: 60 carbon atoms travelling as one amplitude.
  • A 1 mg grain drifting at 1 mm/s has λ ≈ 7 × 10⁻¹⁶ nm. The physics has not switched off; the band spacing is simply far smaller than an atomic nucleus.
Computed from λ = h/p. Eighteen orders of magnitude separate this sim's light from an everyday object.
06

The traps they'll set for you

  • "Opening the second slit can only add light." Watch the dark fringes: places that were well lit with one slit receive almost nothing with two. Adding a route can remove arrivals — that is what a negative cross term means.
  • "The particle splits and goes through both slits." Half a particle is never detected. The amplitude takes both routes; the particle arrives once, whole, somewhere the amplitude allowed.
  • "The bands come from particles bumping into each other." The dots land independently and one at a time, and the band positions depend only on d, a, λ and L — the geometry of the two routes, not the traffic.
  • "The spacing is λL/d." Only for small angles. Here L/d = 2.5 and the bands reach 40° off axis, so that formula is off by a quarter at the second order.
07

Ninety-second self-test

Close S2. What is left on the screen, and why?

Only the single-slit envelope of S1: I(x) = (sin β₁/β₁)² centred on that slit. With ψ₂ = 0 there is no cross term, so there are no bands. At the default a/λ = 0.93 that envelope has no zeros either, so what you see is one broad mound — exactly the classical-looking result, from a purely quantum calculation.

Double λ and double d together. What happens to the bands?

Almost nothing. The pattern depends on Δ/λ, and in the far field Δ ≈ d sin θ, so doubling both leaves sin θ_m = mλ/d unchanged. Two things do change: the envelope, because a/λ has halved, so the outer bands get relatively brighter, and the exact positions shift a little because this sim solves r₂ − r₁ without the far-field approximation.

Ten thousand dots have landed. Which slit did any one of them go through?

The question has no answer inside this model. The pattern requires both amplitudes; assigning a slit to each dot would give P₁ + P₂, which is the orange curve, not the green one. Only a measurement that actually records the route can make "which slit" meaningful — and this sim does not implement one.
