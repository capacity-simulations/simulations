Guided Inquiry

1 · The packet

A single quantum particle is described by a real Gaussian centred at X₁: ψ₁(x) = e−a(x−X₁)² / √N. The prefactor 1/√N normalises it, so the area under |ψ₁|² is always 1. The number a sets how tightly the packet is localised; the plot draws either |ψ₁(x)|² or ψ₁(x) itself.

2 · Squeezing the packet

The localisation a is increased, so the packet occupies a narrower stretch of x. What happens to the peak of |ψ₁|²?

3 · The wave and its square

The same packet can be drawn as ψ₁ or as |ψ₁|², on the same axes and at the same a. How do the two widths compare?

4 · How the peak scales

Starting from a = 0.40, the localisation is quadrupled to a = 1.60. By what factor does the peak of |ψ₁|² grow?

The Physics

The single Gaussian packet · a complete revision sheet · normalisation, width and the uncertainty floor

A Gaussian packet is not a tiny ball parked at X₁. It is a probability spread over x — and one number, a, decides how much of x the particle is allowed to occupy.

01

What is this curve — and what is it not?

The sim's own plot at first load (X₁ = −2, a slider 589 → a = 0.812), computed: both curves the View toggle can draw, on the sim's shared vertical scale. σ = 0.555.
  • One state, drawn two ways. The View toggle picks |ψ₁(x)|² (first load) or Re(ψ₁(x)); the dashed vertical line is the centre X₁.
  • This ψ₁ is real and never negative — the sim puts no phase eikx in it — so the "Re(ψ₁)" view is the whole wavefunction, not a slice of it.
  • Only |ψ₁|² is a probability density. The 1/√N prefactor pins its area at 1. ψ₁ is an amplitude: its height means nothing until you square it.
  • X₁ only slides the packet. Shape, σ, peak and area never notice — which is why nothing in the readouts except X₁ changes when you drag it.

02

Why does squeezing the packet make it taller?

Square the amplitude and you get the density the sim normalises to 1.
  • a is a localisation, not a width. The width is σ = 1/(2√a) — the readout under the slider. Turn a up and the packet narrows, but only as 1/√a.
  • The area is pinned at 1, so a narrower packet must be a taller one: peak = √(2a/π), which grows as √a.
  • Quadruple a — the guided inquiry's 0.40 → 1.60 — and σ halves while the peak only doubles, 0.505 → 1.010.
  • Squaring doubles the exponent coefficient, so |ψ₁|² is √2 narrower than ψ₁ on the same axes. σ belongs to the density, not to the amplitude.
  • The two peaks are related by a square root: peak(ψ₁) = √peak(|ψ₁|²). They swap places at a = π/2 ≈ 1.571 — below it ψ₁ is the taller curve, above it the density is.

03

What do the numbers actually say?

X₁ a

These are the sim's real controls — drag here and the plot follows. The a slider is logarithmic: a = 0.1 × 35v/1000, so v = 0…1000 sweeps a = 0.10…3.50.

  • Every number above is fixed by a alone. X₁ appears only as the place the packet sits.
  • The sim uses a bare axis — x carries no unit, so a is a number, not a value in nm−2. What transfers to real systems is the scaling, not the size.
  • The sim normalises by integrating over the plotted window x ∈ [−6, 6], not over all of x. Push X₁ to ±5 at small a and a real tail falls off the screen, so the drawn peak sits above √(2a/π) — the panel reports that capture fraction.
04

Squeeze it in x — what happens to p?

The same packet, read in momentum. The sim never draws this — it follows from the ψ₁ it does draw.
  • Fourier-transform a Gaussian and you get a Gaussian: Δp = ħ√a. Tighten a and Δx falls as 1/√a while Δp rises as √a.
  • The product is stuck at ħ/2 for every setting the slider can reach — a Gaussian is a minimum-uncertainty packet, the tightest position–momentum trade nature allows.
  • ⟨p⟩ = 0. Any real ψ has zero average momentum, so this packet is sitting still rather than drifting — give the sim a clock and the centre would stay on X₁.
  • But it has no clock: the sim draws one instant. No Schrödinger time step, no Play button, nothing to wait for.
Δp against Δx for the a slider's whole range, computed. Every reachable setting lands on the same line; the shaded region is what no state may enter.
05

Where does nature make a packet this shape?

  • The ground state of a harmonic trap is exactly this Gaussian, with a = mω/2ħ. It is the one place a real, node-free packet is also an energy eigenstate — so it genuinely never changes.
  • A laser-cooled ⁴⁰Ca⁺ ion in a 1 MHz trap sits in a Gaussian of σ ≈ 11 nm — the residual zero-point motion that single-ion optical clocks have to design around.
  • Read this sim's axis as nanometres and the first-load packet (a = 0.812, σ = 0.555 nm) implies Δp = ħ√a, which for an electron is a speed spread of ≈ 104 km/s. (Our estimate — the sim carries no units and never states this.)
  • A free Gaussian does spread as time runs, Δx(t) = σ√(1 + (2ħat/m)²). That is real physics, and it is not in this sim: there is no time here.
06

The traps they'll set for you

  • "a is the width of the packet." a is the localisation: the width is σ = 1/(2√a), so bigger a means a narrower packet — and quadrupling a only halves the width.
  • "Quadruple a and the peak quadruples." The peak is √(2a/π) ∝ √a, so it merely doubles: 0.505 → 1.010. Height and width are locked together by the fixed area, not by a directly.
  • "The Re(ψ₁) curve is the probability." It is the amplitude. Probability is its square, and squaring makes the curve √2 narrower and changes its height — the sim redraws and relabels when you toggle the view.
  • "Leave it running and it will spread out." A free packet would, but this sim has no time in it: it draws ψ₁ at one instant. Nothing here evolves, and there is no Play control to wait on.
07

Ninety-second self-test

What would happen if a could go to zero?

σ = 1/(2√a) would run to infinity and the peak √(2a/π) to zero: the packet flattens out over all x and the particle is nowhere in particular. Momentum goes the other way — Δp = ħ√a → 0, a perfectly definite momentum. The slider stops at a = 0.10 (σ = 1.581), and even there the sim has to normalise over the window it can draw.

You want to halve σ. What must a do, and what happens to the peak?

σ ∝ 1/√a, so halving σ needs four times the a. The peak √(2a/π) then rises by √4 = two, exactly conserving the area of 1. Δp = ħ√a also doubles, so ΔxΔp stays at ħ/2.

On screen |ψ₁|² is narrower than ψ₁. Is the particle more localised in the |ψ|² view?

No — it is one state, drawn two ways. Only the density answers "where is the particle", and its spread is σ = 1/(2√a). The ψ₁ curve looks √2 wider because it carries half the exponent coefficient (a, not 2a); that width is a property of the amplitude, not a second, looser localisation.
