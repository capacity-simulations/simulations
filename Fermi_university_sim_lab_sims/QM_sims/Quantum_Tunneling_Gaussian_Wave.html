About this simulation

Physics

A localized Gaussian wavepacket propagates toward a symmetric rectangular barrier with V(x) = U for |x| < a. The model is one-dimensional, non-relativistic, spinless quantum mechanics (free-electron mass). Depending on energy and barrier geometry, the packet partially reflects and partially transmits; for E < U, the transmitted component is quantum tunneling.

Simulation visuals

The top panel shows the barrier profile and carrier-energy reference. The lower panel shows Re(ψ), Im(ψ), and |ψ|² versus position, including packet spreading and barrier interaction. Absorbing layers at both boundaries suppress wrap-around reflections from the FFT domain and support cleaner reflection/transmission estimates.

Controls

Use sliders to tune U, a, carrier E, packet width σ, initial center x₀, and playback speed. Toggle Re(ψ), Im(ψ), and |ψ|² traces; the regime badge compares carrier E to U, while Wavepacket Info reports broader packet-energy context.

The Physics

Quantum tunneling · a complete revision sheet · a Gaussian wavepacket against a rectangular barrier

Tunneling is not the particle borrowing energy to climb over the barrier. Inside a barrier a wave does not stop — it decays. Whatever amplitude is still standing at the far face walks out at the energy it came in with.

01

What is actually launched at the barrier?

The sim's two panels at t = 0, computed from its defaults: the Barrier Diagram above (V(x) in red, the dashed line is the carrier energy E), and Re ψ with the shaded |ψ|² below. Im ψ is the same envelope, shifted a quarter wavelength. The sim's window runs to +18 nm; this is a crop.
  • The barrier is V(x) = U for |x| ≤ a and zero elsewhere — so the slider sets the half-width, and the wall the packet meets is 2a wide.
  • What travels is a Gaussian wavepacket, not a particle with a position: a bell-shaped envelope of width σ carrying a carrier wave of wavelength λ = 2π/k₀.
  • E is the carrier energy, ħ²k₀²/2m. The Physics panel calls the same number k₁; Wavepacket Info calls it k₀. They are one wave vector.
  • The packet is not monoenergetic. A finite σ means a spread of k, and that spread is what makes the measured outcome differ from the textbook number — chapter 05.

02

Why doesn't the wave just stop at the wall?

With E < U the curvature flips sign: sines become real exponentials.
  • Outside the barrier ψ curves back on itself and oscillates. Inside, E − U < 0, so ψ curves away from the axis — it decays rather than turning around.
  • The decay rate is κ = √(2m(U−E))/ħ, and 1/κ is the distance over which the amplitude falls by e. The sim prints it as Decay length 1/κ whenever E < U.
  • Decaying is not vanishing. At the far face ψ is small but non-zero, and a non-zero ψ there means an outgoing wave beyond it.
  • Nothing here is "inside the barrier with negative kinetic energy" — the evanescent region is not a classical trajectory. The sim counts the probability still inside the barrier separately: it is neither reflected nor transmitted until it leaves.
The exact stationary scattering solution at the sim's defaults (E = 0.20 eV, U = 0.30 eV, a = 0.50 nm), computed here: standing-wave ripple on the left where incident and reflected waves overlap, exponential decay across the barrier, a flat transmitted wave of amplitude |t| = 0.362 on the right.
03

How much of it gets through?

The exact plane-wave transmission for E < U — the formula the sim's Physics panel evaluates.
E U a

These are the sim's real sliders — drag here and the barrier changes on screen. Every change relaunches the packet, exactly as it does in the sidebar.

Once the barrier is more than a decay length or two thick, one exponential carries everything.
  • T falls exponentially in the width, not linearly: the controlling factor is e−4κa, with 4κa built from the full width 2a.
  • At the defaults 2κa = 1.62, so the asymptotic form already lands within about 6% of the exact value (13.9% against 13.10%) — close, but the sim always prints the exact expression.
  • T is just as sensitive to U − E, through κ = √(2m(U−E))/ħ. Halving U − E by raising E from 0.20 to 0.25 eV, at the same U and a, lifts T from 13.1% to 21.8%.
04

What does the transmitted packet lose?

Above the barrier there is still reflection — and at the resonances, none at all.
  • Amplitude, not energy. Outside the barrier V = 0 on both sides, so the transmitted packet keeps k₀, keeps λ, and keeps the group velocity vg = ħk₀/m. Fewer electrons cross; none arrive slowed. (The barrier does favour the faster components of the packet, so the filtering nudges the transmitted mean energy up a little — nothing is ever drained.)
  • Raising E above U does not end reflection. At E = 0.60 eV against U = 0.30 eV the plane-wave T is 98.66%, so about 1.3% still reflects — waves scatter off any sharp step.
  • Reflection does vanish at the resonances 2k₂a = nπ, where the two barrier faces cancel each other. At E = 0.60 eV, U = 0.30 eV that first happens at a = 0.56 nm: T = 1 exactly.
  • The sim's regime badge names which branch you are in — tunneling, threshold, or above-barrier — and the Physics panel switches formula with it.
05

Why does the sim's estimate never match the formula exactly?

The packet carries a spread of energies, and T is steep in E — so the outcome is an average, not a value.
  • At the default σ = 1.50 nm the packet's energy spread is ΔE ≈ 58 meV against a 100 meV gap to the barrier top. Components with more energy tunnel far better, so the weighted average leans above the single-E value: at the defaults the sim settles near a 16% transmission estimate against the 13.10% plane-wave number.
  • The readouts are labelled estimates for a second reason: the FFT grid is periodic, so absorbing layers at both edges swallow the outgoing packets, and the sim adds that absorbed weight back into R and T.
  • Narrowing σ sharpens the packet in space but widens ΔE — the sim prints k₀σ and marks it "(low)" once it falls below 3, because by then "the carrier energy" barely names one energy.
  • Read the two numbers as a pair: the Physics panel gives the monoenergetic law; the bar under the plots gives this packet's outcome.
The sim's own T formula, plotted against half-width a: the sim's barrier (U − E = 0.10 eV, marks at a = 0.50 nm → T = 13.1% and a = 1.00 nm → T = 0.54%) and a metal–vacuum gap (U − E = 4 eV). Steepness is why a scanning tunneling microscope resolves single atoms: widening that gap by 0.1 nm (a → a + 0.05 nm) costs the 4 eV barrier a factor of about 8.
06

The traps they'll set for you

  • "With E < U the particle simply cannot cross." That is the classical answer, and it is the first thing this sim breaks: ψ decays through the barrier instead of stopping, and a small transmitted packet leaves the far side.
  • "It borrows energy to get over the top." Nothing in the Hamiltonian changes E. The packet never goes over; its wave goes through, and the transmitted piece has the same carrier energy it started with.
  • "The transmitted packet comes out slower." V = 0 on both sides, so equal energy means equal speed. Only the amplitude shrank — the sim shows both packets covering equal distance in equal time.
  • "Double the width, halve the transmission." T ∝ e−4κa. At the defaults, doubling a from 0.50 to 1.00 nm divides T by 24, from 13.1% to 0.54%.
  • "Above the barrier everything crosses." A wave reflects off any abrupt step. The reflection estimate stays a few percent above zero at E > U, and only hits zero at the resonances.
07

Ninety-second self-test

Set E exactly equal to U. Does everything get through?

No. κ → 0, the sinh form becomes ill-conditioned, and the limit is T = [1 + k₁²a²]⁻¹ — at U = E = 0.30 eV and a = 0.50 nm that is 33.7%, so two thirds still reflects. The sim detects this branch itself and prints the threshold formula with an "E ≈ U (threshold)" badge.

Halve U − E at fixed width. Does T double?

No — the dependence is exponential in κ ∝ √(U−E), so halving U − E only shrinks the exponent by √2. Raising E from 0.20 to 0.25 eV at a = 0.50 nm takes T from 13.10% to 21.8%: a factor of 1.66, not 2.

The Physics panel says T = 13.10%, but the Transmission estimate under the plots reads something else. Which one is wrong?

Neither. The panel evaluates the plane-wave formula at a single energy E; the bar reports what this packet did, and the packet carries ΔE ≈ 58 meV of spread through a law that varies steeply with E, so its outcome is a flux-weighted average of T(E) over that spread. The estimate also accumulates probability caught by the absorbing layers, which is why it is called an estimate.
