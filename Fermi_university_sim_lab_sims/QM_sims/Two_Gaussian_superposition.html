Guided Inquiry

1 · Two packets, one particle

Two Gaussian packets sit on one axis: ψ₁ centred at X₁, ψ₂ centred at X₂, each localized by a. The particle's state is the superposition Ψ = (αψ₁ + βψ₂)/√N′, with α² + β² = 1. The lower panel draws |Ψ(x)|², and over it a dashed curve — the two packet densities added with those same weights.

2 · Two chances

Classically a particle that is partly at X₁ and partly at X₂ simply has two chances of turning up. On that reckoning, what is its probability density at any point on the axis?

3 · Bringing them together

The second centre X₂ is brought in to 0, so the packets now overlap appreciably. Midway between the two centres, how does |Ψ(x)|² compare with the dashed curve?

4 · Paying for the rise

Both curves are normalized: each encloses a total probability of exactly 1. The solid curve now stands well above the dashed one between the centres. What must follow elsewhere?

5 · Reading the amplitude

Read the same state as Re(ψ) instead of |ψ|². The lower panel then draws αψ₁ and βψ₂ faint and Ψ solid. Midway between the centres, where does the solid curve sit?

6 · Narrowing the packets

Back in |ψ|², a is raised so both packets become narrower, with X₁ and X₂ left where they are. What happens to the gap between the solid and dashed curves?

The Physics

Two-Gaussian superposition · a complete revision sheet · amplitudes add, probabilities don't

A superposition is not a coin toss between X₁ and X₂. It is one wave that reaches both places at once — and wherever the two reaches meet, they add before anything is squared.

01

What is the sim actually adding?

The sim's three panels, computed at X₁ = −2, X₂ = 0, a = 0.812, α = β = 0.707. Top two: each component alone. Bottom: |Ψ|² (solid) against the "Classical (normalized)" curve (dashed). Shown over x = −5 … 3; the sim's own axis runs −6 … 6.
  • Each component is one normalised Gaussian, parked at X₁ or X₂. The parameter a is localization: bigger a, narrower packet.
  • The top two panels are unweighted. Sliding α or β never changes their shape — the sim's own model note says so, because α and β weight only the combined Ψ.
  • The state is the sum of the amplitudes, not of the humps: Ψ = (αψ₁ + βψ₂)/√N′. N′ is whatever it takes to keep ∫|Ψ|² dx = 1.
  • α and β are tied: moving one retunes the other so α² + β² = 1. The sim prints that check under the sliders.
One packet: its width comes from a alone, nothing else.

02

Why isn't |Ψ|² just the two humps added?

Squaring a sum is not summing the squares — the difference is the cross term.
  • Two classical alternatives contribute their probabilities: α²|ψ₁|² + β²|ψ₂|². That is exactly the sim's dashed "Classical (normalized)" curve.
  • Quantum mechanics adds amplitudes first. Squaring αψ₁ + βψ₂ leaves the extra term 2αβ ψ₁ψ₂, which lives only where both packets are non-zero.
  • In this sim the two packets are held in phase (the code fixes cos φ = 1, sin φ = 0), and α, β are both ≥ 0 — so ψ₁ψ₂ is positive everywhere and the overlap region always fills up.
  • Watch the sign of the shading, not just its presence: green where |Ψ|² beats the dashed curve, pink where it falls short.
The gap between the two plotted curves, in closed form — and why it changes sign.
03

How much interference, exactly?

One number controls the whole effect: the overlap of the two packets.
S against packet separation, computed at the sim's default a and at the value card 6 drives it to. The three marked points are states this sim actually visits.
X₁ X₂ a α

These are the sim's real sliders — drag here and all three panels follow. β is not listed because the sim ties it to α through α² + β² = 1; it moves on its own.

  • S is the entire interference budget. It depends on a and on the separation only through the combination a(X₁ − X₂)² — halve the separation or quarter a and you land on the same S.
  • S falls to ½ once the centres are one packet FWHM apart, and it is Gaussian in the separation after that: by 4 units at the default a it is already 0.0015.
  • A big ratio is not a big effect. At the exact midpoint |Ψ|² always runs (α + β)²/N′ times the dashed curve — which tends to 2 at equal weights as the packets separate, never to 1. What dies with S is the height there, not the ratio: at first load the midpoint is still 2× the classical value and only 0.6% as tall as the peaks, which is why you see nothing.
04

If the middle goes up, what goes down?

The area under |Ψ|² is pinned at 1 — so interference can only move probability around.
  • Both plotted curves enclose exactly 1: the sim normalises |Ψ|² by N′ and the classical curve by α² + β². The comparison is fair, so the green area and the pink area must balance.
  • The pink slivers sit on the two peaks. Piling probability into the overlap is paid for by thinning the packets where they were tallest — the sim's legend names only the green, so the pink is easy to miss.
  • That is the same 1/√N′ that shrinks every peak: dividing by a bigger N′ is what takes the height off X₁ and X₂.
  • Interference therefore narrows the state: Δx of Ψ comes out below the classical Δx at the same settings. The live worked run above prints both.
05

Where does nature run this algebra?

Two 1s-like Gaussians on two nuclei, computed. The bonding combination is this sim at α = β; the antibonding one needs β < 0, which these sliders cannot reach.
The same normalisation, written the way a chemist writes it.
  • A chemical bond is this figure. The H₂⁺ bonding orbital is (φA + φB)/√(2(1 + S)) — which is exactly (αφA + βφB)/√N′ at α = β = 1/√2, with the same peak-flattening and the same build-up in between.
  • That build-up of electron density between the nuclei is what holds them together: H₂⁺ binds by about 2.6 eV at a separation of roughly 0.11 nm. (quoted from the H₂⁺ literature; this sim computes neither — it has no potential and no Hamiltonian.)
  • Real quantum-chemistry codes genuinely do this with Gaussians: Gaussian basis sets exist because overlap integrals like S have the closed form on this sheet.
  • Flip the sign — β < 0 — and the cross term reverses: a node appears between the centres and the bond becomes an antibond. This sim's sliders run 0 … 1, so that half of the story is off its range.
06

The traps they'll set for you

  • "The particle is in two places at the same time." The sim's About box quotes that line, and it is a slogan about the amplitude, not a report of two particles. Every position measurement still returns one x. The honest operational content of "both at once" is the cross term — no "it is really at one of them" story can produce it.
  • "A superposition just means we don't know which packet it's in." That is precisely the dashed curve. The sim draws both so you can see they differ; if ignorance were the whole story, the solid curve would sit on the dashed one at every x.
  • "Raising α makes the first packet taller." The top two panels never move when you drag α or β. They are the unweighted components; α and β weight only the combined Ψ in the bottom panel.
  • "More separation means more interference." Backwards. The cross term carries S = exp(−a(X₁ − X₂)²/2); pulling the centres apart, or narrowing the packets at fixed separation, drives it to zero and |Ψ|² settles back onto the dashed curve.
07

Ninety-second self-test

Set X₁ = X₂. What happens to the interference shading?

It vanishes. With zero separation S = 1, and Ψ = (α + β)ψ/√((α + β)²) = ψ — one single packet. |Ψ|² lands exactly on the dashed curve everywhere, because the two "alternatives" are no longer distinguishable places at all. Two terms in the algebra are not enough; interference needs two separated reaches that still overlap.

Double a at fixed separation. What happens to S?

It squares. S = exp(−a d²/2), so doubling a replaces S by S². At the sim's defaults with the centres 2 apart, S = 0.197 becomes 0.039 — which is why card 6's push on the localization slider flattens the bump so fast. The same rule works through d: doubling the separation takes S to S⁴.

In Re(ψ) view the bottom curve is exactly the sum of the two above it. In |ψ|² view it is not. Why?

Because Ψ is linear in its components and |Ψ|² is not. Re(Ψ) = αψ₁/√N′ + βψ₂/√N′ adds pointwise — and here it is all of Ψ, since the sim fixes the relative phase at zero so Im(Ψ) = 0. Squaring that sum produces the extra 2αβψ₁ψ₂, so the probability curve is the two humps plus a term that belongs to neither.
