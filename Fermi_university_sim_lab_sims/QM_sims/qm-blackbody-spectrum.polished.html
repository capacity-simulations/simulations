The Physics

Black-body radiation · a complete revision sheet · the spectrum of a hot cavity

Planck's law is not a better curve fitted to the classical one. It counts exactly the same standing waves — and then refuses to give a high-frequency mode its share, because energy of frequency f only comes in whole units of hf.

01

Where does a spectrum come from at all?

The sim's own fixed frame, computed point by point from its engine at T = 6000 K. The dashed classical curve leaves the top of the plot at 1.61×10¹⁴ Hz — before the real peak has even arrived.
  • Every cavity spectrum is the same product: how many standing waves sit in that slice of frequency, times how much energy each one holds.
  • The count is not in dispute. Both laws use 8πf²/c³ modes per m³ per Hz — it grows as f², and nothing quantum touches it.
  • The whole argument of 1900 is about the second factor. Change ⟨E(f)⟩ and you change the curve; everything else is shared.
Count times energy. The two laws differ in the right-hand bracket only.
02

Why does one curve climb forever and the other turn over?

Rayleigh–Jeans: classical equipartition hands every mode the same kT, so u just tracks the mode count.
Planck: the same count, but a mode's mean energy collapses once hf passes kT.
The entire difference between the two laws, computed from the sim's own η = hf/kT: the mean energy of one mode, in units of kT.
  • Classically every mode is worth kT no matter how fast it oscillates, so u ∝ f² with no turnover. That is the ultraviolet catastrophe, and it is a statement about the energy, not the count.
  • Quantised, a mode of frequency f can hold 0, hf, 2hf, … With hf ≫ kT it is almost always in the zero state, and its average energy dies as e−η.
  • So the rising f² and the falling exponential fight, and the winner changes at η ≈ 1. That crossover is the peak.
03

Where is the peak, and how much energy is inside?

Wien's law in the frequency form the sim plots — η at the peak is a pure number, so fmax is strictly proportional to T.
Stefan's fourth power. The classical integral has no value at all — this is where "diverges" stops being a figure of speech.
T

This is the sim's own temperature slider — drag it here and the plot follows.

  • Both scalings come from the same substitution η = hf/kT: it pulls a kT/h out of the peak position and a (kT)⁴ out of the integral.
  • Double T and the peak doubles, the peak height goes up eightfold (∝T³), and the total goes up sixteenfold. Three different powers, one substitution.
04

Then is the classical law simply wrong?

Two limits of one bracket. On the left the agreement is exact, not approximate; on the right there is nothing left to approximate.
  • Rayleigh–Jeans is exact as η → 0. It is not a rough fit that degrades gently — it is the true low-frequency limit of Planck's law, and the two curves are indistinguishable there.
  • What fails is the extrapolation. The single control on the failure is η = hf/kT, nothing else: not f alone, not T alone.
  • That is why the sim's inset, plotted against η, never moves. In η the two laws read η³/(eη−1) and η², and neither contains T — one picture serves every temperature.
  • Planck himself called the quantisation "an act of desperation" — a bookkeeping trick to get the fit. It took Einstein's 1905 paper to read it as physics.
05

Where does nature run this?

One straight line, fmax = 5.879×10¹⁰ T, computed from the sim's own peak formula. The shaded strip is everything the sim's slider can reach.
  • The Sun, 5772 K. Its per-frequency peak lands at 3.39×10¹⁴ Hz — the near infrared, not the green you may have been told. The famous 500 nm figure is the peak of the per-wavelength curve, which is a different maximum of a different function.
  • A tungsten filament, 2800 K. Peak at 1.65×10¹⁴ Hz, so most of its output is heat, not light. An incandescent bulb is a space heater with a visible leak.
  • The cosmic microwave background, 2.725 K. Peak at 1.60×10¹¹ Hz. COBE measured it in 1990 and it is the most perfect black body ever recorded — the residuals to Planck's law are under one part in 10⁴.
  • You, 310 K. Peak near 1.8×10¹³ Hz — that is what a thermal camera sees. By Stefan's law your skin radiates close to 900 W; you stay warm only because the room radiates almost as much straight back.
06

The traps they'll set for you

  • "The classical curve is a rough approximation that gets steadily worse." It is exact at low frequency and then fails without limit. There is no regime where it is "roughly right" — it is either perfect or unboundedly wrong.
  • "There are fewer high-frequency modes, so the curve falls." Backwards. The count 8πf²/c³ keeps growing; the fall is entirely the mean energy of each mode dying.
  • "The classical curve flattens off eventually." Nothing pulls it back. u ∝ f² forever, which is why the sim draws it straight out of the top of the frame and gives it no total.
  • "Hotter means more energy at every frequency, so the peak stays put." More energy at every frequency, yes — but the increase is far bigger high up, so the peak slides: fmax ∝ T.
  • "The peak is at hf = kT." Close, but the peak is where the product turns over, not where the exponential does: ηp = 2.8214, the root of η = 3(1−e−η).
07

Ninety-second self-test

Let h → 0 with T fixed. What happens to Planck's curve?

It becomes Rayleigh–Jeans everywhere. With h → 0 you have η = hf/kT → 0 at every frequency, and hf/(eη−1) → kT. The catastrophe is exactly what the classical world looks like: quantisation switched off.

You triple T. By what factors do fmax, the peak height and the total energy density change?

×3, ×27 and ×81. fmax ∝ T, the height at the peak ∝ T³ (it is 8πh fmax³/c³ divided by a fixed number), and the total ∝ T⁴. All three follow from the single substitution η = hf/kT.

Why does the sim refuse to print a total for the dashed curve?

Because there isn't one. ∫(8πf²/c³)kT df diverges — the classical law assigns infinite energy to any cavity at any temperature above absolute zero. Printing a number would be inventing one.
