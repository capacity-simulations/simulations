Guided Inquiry

1 · The apparatus

Here’s the apparatus. A particle source emits particles with a random magnetic moment μ, and the beam passes through an inhomogeneous magnetic field aligned along the z-axis, as you can see. The force on each particle is proportional to the projection of the magnetic moment along z, which we’ll call μz. Quantum mechanically, the magnetic moment is proportional to the particle’s spin.

2 · The classical prediction

Let’s start with the classical picture. Classically, what do you think the distribution of particles on the detector screen would be?

3 · The split

Here’s the quantum version. The source now emits spin j = 1/2 particles, and the beam splits in two. Why do you think that happens?

4 · Counting the bands

Let’s push that further. For particles of spin j, how many bands do you think the beam splits into? The spin j is yours to change — try a few values and let the detector decide.

5 · Doubling the gradient

One last experiment. We’ll double the field gradient while the source emits j = 1/2 particles. How do you think the force on each particle changes?

The Physics

Stern–Gerlach · a complete revision sheet · spin, space quantization and measurement

A Stern–Gerlach magnet is not a sieve that separates atoms which were already pointing up or down. It is a question put to every atom at once — how much of your spin lies along z? — and the screen records that only 2j+1 answers exist.

01

Why does a magnet push a neutral atom at all?

The sim's apparatus at its defaults (j = 1/2, gradient 3 T/m), trajectories replayed from the sim's own step rule. Orange: every classical value of μz. Pink and blue: the only two the atom is allowed. Pole faces are schematic; the vertical scale is stretched so the split is visible.
  • A silver atom is electrically neutral — no Lorentz force. What it does carry is a magnetic moment locked to its angular momentum.
  • In a uniform field that moment only feels a torque. A force needs the field to change across the gap — which is what the pointed N pole in the sim is for.
  • The force is set by one number, the projection μz = −gJ mJ μB, and by the gradient the Magnetic Field Gradient slider controls.
  • Sign check, the sim's own: a positive mJ gives a force down the z axis, so in the sim the mJ = +1/2 band lands below the centre line and mJ = −1/2 above it.
The moment is the spin in disguise — and its z component is not continuous.
The sim's About box writes exactly this force law.
02

Why bands, and why exactly 2j+1 of them?

The whole experiment in one contrast: a flat distribution against a comb of spikes.
  • Classically the moment can point anywhere, so cos θ is uniform and μz is spread evenly between −μ and +μ. That is one filled band with sharp edges — not a blob that fades out. Switch to Classical Prediction draws it.
  • Quantum mechanically mJ runs over 2j+1 values in steps of 1, so there are 2j+1 forces, 2j+1 landing places, and nothing in between.
  • The landing position is z = −mJζ: linear in mJ, so the gaps are all the same size. One step ζ is fixed by the gradient, the magnet length and the atom's speed — never by j.
  • So raising j does not spread the pattern's rungs apart; it adds rungs at the same spacing, outward from the centre. The sim's Beam Legend gains a row each time.

03

How hard is the push, and how far apart do the bands land?

j ∂B/∂z

These are the sim's real controls — drag here and the apparatus follows. Note the sim's own rule: changing j or switching mode clears the detection screen and every count.

  • Double the gradient and you double every force and every gap — the pattern is linear in ∂B/∂z, never squared.
  • The outermost band always feels the biggest force, |Fz| = gJ j μB ∂B/∂z, so the pattern's half-width is jζ.
  • The sim's screen is drawn in pixels, not millimetres: its magnet, drift and atom speed are scaled to fit the window, so ζ has no real size here and only the ratios are physical. For a real gap see ch 05: Gerlach's 3.5 cm magnet at 1000 T/m gives 0.21 mm, so at the sim's 3 T/m the same magnet would open only about 0.6 µm.
  • The sim's source is unpolarised: it deals each atom one of the 2j+1 values with equal probability, which is why the Beam Legend counts stay level as they grow.
04

What is the magnet actually measuring?

The magnet ties where the atom is to what mJ is — that link is what makes it a measurement.
  • Each atom arrives in some spin state |χ⟩, a superposition over mJ. The magnet gives each term a different deflection, so by the screen the position carries the answer and reading the spot reads mJ.
  • For that atom, the probability of landing in band mJ is |cm|². But an oven does not send one state: it sends a random mix of them, pointing every which way — an unpolarised mixture, not one superposition. Averaged over the beam every |cm|² is 1/(2j+1), so for this sim P = 1/(2j+1) for every band.
  • What the sim actually computes: it draws one mJ at random from the allowed set and then integrates the classical force for that value. The coloured dots are individual detections — the sheet plots no wavefunction anywhere, and neither does the sim.
  • What it deliberately leaves out: no Larmor precession, no second magnet along another axis, no partial measurement. It reproduces the outcome statistics of one z measurement, nothing more.
05

What did this actually settle, in a real laboratory?

  • Frankfurt, 1922: Stern and Gerlach fired silver through a 3.5 cm magnet and developed a plate showing two lips with a gap of about 0.2 mm. The formula above gives 0.21 mm — an estimate, from a beam speed of 550 m/s and the plate sitting at the magnet exit.
  • The gap is linear in the gradient and needs a huge one: 10 T/cm is 1000 T/m, over a hundred times the 6 T/m at the top of the sim's slider — and it still buys only a fifth of a millimetre.
  • They read the result as confirming orbital quantization. It was spin: silver's lone outer electron has j = 1/2 and gJ = 2, so μz = ±μB. The right answer for the wrong reason, corrected only in 1925.
  • The magnet outlived the question. Stern–Gerlach state selectors ran the first caesium atomic clocks, and the two-outcome box is still the standard model of a projective measurement.
One silver atom in Gerlach's magnet, computed: the gap between the two bands rises straight with the gradient.
06

The traps they'll set for you

  • "Classically the beam would pile up in the middle and fade at the edges." Every orientation is equally likely, so cos θ — and with it μz — is uniform. The classical screen is one evenly filled band with hard edges.
  • "The atoms were already up or down; the magnet just sorts them." Then a second magnet turned to x would find them all definite along x too. It does not: send one output through a sideways magnet and it splits again.
  • "A stronger magnet splits the beam more." Field strength alone gives torque and zero net force. Only ∂B/∂z separates the beams — that is why one pole in the sim is pointed.
  • "The split comes from the atom's charge." Silver is neutral, and a charge would be pushed sideways by v × B, not split along z.
07

Ninety-second self-test

Set j = 0. What does the screen show, and why?

One band, dead centre. j = 0 allows the single value mJ = 0, so μz = 0 and Fz = 0 — no deflection at any gradient. The sim's selector says it outright: "j = 0 (1 beam)". A split needs a non-zero projection, not merely a field.

Go from j = 1/2 at 6 T/m to j = 1 at 3 T/m. What happens to the width of the whole pattern?

It stays the same. The half-width is jζ and ζ is proportional to ∂B/∂z, so doubling j while halving the gradient leaves jζ unchanged — but the pattern now holds 3 bands instead of 2, packed at half the old spacing.

The sim deals each atom one mJ at random. Is that the same as the atom having had that value all along?

For this one measurement the statistics are identical, which is exactly why the sim can get away with it. It is not the same physics: a pre-existing value along z would have to coexist with definite values along x and y, and a second magnet turned sideways splits the beam again. The sim models the outcomes of a z measurement, not a hidden list of properties.
