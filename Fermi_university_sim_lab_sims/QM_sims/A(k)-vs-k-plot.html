About This Simulation

This interactive simulation visualizes the Gaussian momentum amplitude distribution A(k) commonly encountered in quantum mechanics when describing wavepackets. The amplitude function governs how momentum components are distributed around a central wavenumber k₀.

Students can drag k₀ to shift the peak (changing the particle's central momentum) and adjust σ to widen or narrow the distribution. Shaded regions show the amplitude A(k) over the note-defined intervals k₀ ± σ and k₀ ± 2σ, making it easier to see how the width parameter changes the profile. Click or drag on the plot to move the marker and read A(k) at that k.

where k₀ is the central wavenumber and σ characterizes the width of the distribution in k-space.

The Physics

The amplitude A(k) · a complete revision sheet · building a wavepacket out of plane waves

A wavepacket is not a particle with a slightly blurred momentum. It is a sum — and this curve is the recipe for the sum: how much of every plane wave you must add to build the packet.

01

What is this curve actually a list of?

The sim's own plot, computed: the first-load state (k₀ = 0.00, σ = 1.00) above, and the state the guided inquiry drives to (k₀ = 3.00, σ = 1.00) below. Same fixed k window, same pink marker sitting at k = 0 — only the A-axis has rescaled.
  • A particle with one definite momentum is a single plane wave eikx: it fills all of space and never localises. To build something with a position you must add many of them.
  • A(k) is the recipe for that sum. For each wavenumber k it gives the weight that plane wave carries. This plot is that list, drawn.
  • The k axis is a momentum axis: p = ħk. The centre k₀ is the packet's central momentum p₀ = ħk₀, and the σ slider is choosing how sharp that momentum is.
  • A(k) is an amplitude, not a probability. The probability density over k goes as A(k)² — narrower than the bell you see. The sim never plots A(k)².
The sim's own gaussian(): the k₀² in the numerator is a fixed offset, not part of the bell.
The superposition A(k) is the recipe for — and the de Broglie link that turns the k axis into momentum.
02

What do k₀ and σ each change?

The same formula split in two: a fixed vertical scale times a shape of width σ centred on k₀.
  • k₀ slides the bell and does nothing else to its shape — the second factor depends only on k − k₀. Physically it resets the central momentum p₀ = ħk₀.
  • σ sets the width of the band of wavenumbers carrying real weight: about k₀ ± 2σ on the plot, where A has fallen to 14% of its peak.
  • The peak height is not a free knob. It is fixed at A(k₀) = ek₀²/2σ² by this sim's convention that A(0) = 1. Leave k₀ at 0 and the peak reads 1.00 for every σ from 1 to 4; set k₀ = 3 and it runs 90.0 → 3.08 → 1.32 as σ goes 1 → 2 → 4.
  • The A-axis rescales itself to 1.1 × the peak, so the bell always fills the frame. Read the tick numbers, never the height on screen.
The three widths the σ slider spans, at k₀ = 3, each divided by its own peak — which is what the sim's auto-scaled A-axis effectively shows you. Wider band, lower peak.
03

What do the numbers say?

Multiply the exponent out and the factor of k is obvious — which is why k = 0 is pinned.
k₀ σ marker

These are the sim's real controls — drag here and the plot follows. The marker is a readout: it changes nothing about A(k), it only reports its value at one k.

  • The marker tooltip prints A at whatever k you park it on. At k = 0 it always prints 1.00, however far the bell has moved.
  • Δk and Δx below are computed from A(k)² and its exact Fourier transform. The sim plots A(k) only — it never draws the packet in x, and it has no time dependence at all.
04

What does this packet look like in space?

Transform the sim's A(k) and a Gaussian envelope comes back, riding a carrier of wavenumber k₀.
  • The Fourier partner of a Gaussian in k is a Gaussian in x. The carrier eik₀x sets the ripple inside the packet; the envelope sets its size.
  • Wider in k is narrower in x: Δx = 1/(σ√2). Push σ from 1 to 4 and the packet shrinks by a factor of four, because a broader band of k falls out of step sooner.
  • The product never moves: Δx Δk = ½ exactly, so Δx Δp = ħ/2. A Gaussian sits precisely on the uncertainty floor — every other shape does worse.
  • The overall factor ek₀²/2σ² cancels out of every normalised statement — widths, probabilities, the transform. It is bookkeeping, not physics.
The two widths, and the product that the σ slider can never change.
Fourier partners, computed from the sim's own A(k) at k₀ = 3. The right-hand panel is not something the sim draws — it is what these amplitudes build.
05

Where does nature run this trade?

  • Pin an electron inside 1 nm — a molecular orbital, a small quantum dot — and it needs a band about Δk ≈ 5×10⁸ m⁻¹ wide, a velocity spread of roughly 58 km/s. Confinement is paid for in momentum.
  • A collimated electron beam in a microscope is very nearly one k — an extremely narrow A(k) — which is exactly why the electron is delocalised down the column and can interfere with itself.
  • The same theorem in time and frequency: a 10 fs laser pulse cannot be one colour. It must span about 44 THz, roughly 95 nm of spectrum around 800 nm (FWHM convention, Δν Δt ≈ 0.44).
  • Scale it up and it disappears: a 1 g bead held to 1 μm has a velocity spread of 5×10⁻²⁶ m/s. The trade is still exact; nothing could ever measure it.
06

The traps they'll set for you

  • "A(k) is the probability of measuring wavenumber k." It is the amplitude. The probability density over k goes as A(k)², a Gaussian of width σ/√2 — narrower than the bell on screen. This sim plots A, never A².
  • "Sliding k₀ to the right gives the packet more amplitude." The peak number climbs as ek₀²/2σ² only because this sim pins A(0) = 1 and the A-axis then rescales to 1.1 × peak. The shape, Δk, Δx and every probability are unchanged — the packet has simply moved to a new central momentum.
  • "A classical particle would be a very narrow bell." It would be a spike of zero width at k = k₀: one exact momentum p = ħk₀. Any σ > 0 is already quantum, which is why the σ slider never reaches zero.
  • "More wavenumbers in the sum must make a broader packet." The opposite. Δx = 1/(σ√2): the extra waves dephase sooner, cancel closer in, and leave a tighter packet.
07

Ninety-second self-test

What would σ → 0 look like, and why can't the slider get there?

A(k) would collapse to a spike at k₀ — a single plane wave eik₀x with exactly definite momentum ħk₀ and Δx → ∞, spread over all of space with no position at all. The σ slider stops at 1, so the sim only ever shows genuinely localised packets.

Set k₀ = 4 with σ = 1, then double σ to 2. What happens to the peak, to Δk and to Δx?

Doubling σ quarters the exponent k₀²/2σ², so the peak falls from e⁸ ≈ 2981 to e² ≈ 7.39. Δk = σ/√2 doubles, 0.71 → 1.41; Δx = 1/(σ√2) halves, 0.71 → 0.35. Their product stays exactly ½.

Why does the marker read exactly A = 1.00 at k = 0 wherever the bell sits?

Written as A(k) = ek(2k₀−k)/2σ² the exponent carries a factor of k, so it vanishes at k = 0 for every k₀ and σ. That is this sim's normalisation convention — A(0) = 1 rather than peak = 1 — and it cancels out of every normalised probability, so it carries no physics.
