About this simulation

This simulation shows a quantum-mechanical plane wave incident on a one-dimensional potential barrier (or step). You can watch the wave function ψ(x, t) evolve in time, and see the real part, imaginary part, and probability density |ψ|².

Adjust the barrier height U, half-width a, and particle energy E. The bottom panel plots the transmission probability T(E) versus energy; the current energy is marked on the curve. For a rectangular barrier centered on the origin, T = 1 at resonance energies above U.

The Physics

Quantum tunnelling · a complete revision sheet · a plane wave on a square barrier

Tunnelling is not a particle punching through a wall. The wave simply never stops at the wall — it leaks in, fades, and whatever amplitude survives to the far edge sets off again as an ordinary travelling wave.

01

Why does anything get through a wall it cannot climb?

The sim's two panels, computed at its defaults — U = 0.50 eV, 2a = 1.00 nm, E = 0.30 eV. Upper: V(x) in red, the beam energy E dashed in cyan. Lower: Re ψ, Im ψ and |ψ|², at the instant the clock reads zero. (The sim draws x from −10 to 14 nm; this is the middle of that window.)
  • Outside the barrier V = 0, so ψ oscillates with k = √(2mE)/ħ. On the left you are seeing the incident wave plus the reflected one, which is why the picture there is lumpy rather than a clean sine.
  • Inside, E < U flips the sign of ψ″/ψ, and the solution stops oscillating: it becomes a real exponential, e−ηx, with η = √(2m(U−E))/ħ. Decaying, not vanishing.
  • At the defaults 1/η = 0.44 nm while the barrier is only 1.00 nm wide, so amplitude survives the crossing: |ψ| falls from 1.53 at the near edge to 0.20 at the far one. That leftover is the transmitted wave.
  • ψ and dψ/dx must be continuous at both edges. Those four matching conditions are the calculation — the sim solves them with a transfer matrix and normalises the incident amplitude to 1.
One equation, three regions; the middle one is where the physics is.
The whole time dependence is a phase factor — and it cancels in the density.
  • So Re ψ and Im ψ travel and rotate into each other at ω = E/ħ, while the purple |ψ|² curve is completely still. At E = 0.30 eV one rotation takes 13.8 fs — about 7 seconds of screen time at 1× speed.
  • Read the purple curve as a ruler: on the right it is flat at |t|² = T, and on the left it ripples between (1+|r|)² = 3.92 and (1−|r|)² = 0.0004 — nearly a perfect standing wave, because nearly everything is reflected.
02

What actually decides how much gets through?

Once the barrier is more than a decay length thick, only the exponent matters.
  • Width and height do not enter separately: they enter as η × (full width 2a), inside an exponent. That single fact is most of tunnelling.
  • Double the width and T does not halve — the exponential roughly squares. At the sim's defaults, moving a from 0.50 to 1.00 nm takes T from 3.86% to 0.040%, a factor of 96.
  • The prefactor 16E(U−E)/U² never exceeds 4 and falls to zero at both E → 0 and E → U. It moves T by factors of a few; the exponential moves it by factors of thousands.
  • The sim switches to this asymptotic form itself once 2ηa > 20; below that it evaluates the exact sinh² expression, which is what the T readout and the T(E) curve are built from.
Same evanescent wave, no second edge — and the answer changes completely.
  • A step has no far edge. Its evanescent tail runs on forever, and a purely real decaying exponential carries no probability current: T = 0 and R = 1 exactly, at every energy below U.
  • So penetration is not transmission. What makes a barrier transmit is the second edge, where the decayed amplitude is handed back to a travelling wave.

04

If E > U, why isn't everything transmitted?

Above the barrier the sinh becomes a sin — and a sin has zeros.
  • Classically, clearing the top means certain transmission. Quantum mechanically every edge in the potential reflects a wave, whatever the energy, so two edges reflect twice and T sits below 1.
  • The two reflections interfere. When the inside wave fits a whole number of half-wavelengths across the full width 2a they cancel exactly, and T returns to precisely 1 — the peaks on the sim's T(E) plot.
  • At the sim's defaults the first peak is at E₁ = 0.88 eV, the second at 2.00 eV. This is the same "fit half-waves into a box" rule as a particle in a well, applied to a state that is not bound.
  • Widen the barrier and the peaks move down and crowd together: at a = 1.00 nm the first sits at 0.59 eV and three fit below 1.4 eV.

05

Where does nature run this?

T against barrier width, computed from the sim's own expression. Teal: the sim's default beam, U − E = 0.20 eV. Dashed: a vacuum gap with U − E = 4.5 eV, a typical metal work function.
  • The scanning tunnelling microscope. An electron tunnels across a vacuum gap with U − E ≈ 4.5 eV, so η ≈ 10.9 nm⁻¹ and the current drops by e−2η(0.1 nm) ≈ 1/9 for every extra ångström. One atom of surface corrugation moves the current by nearly an order of magnitude — that is where the atomic resolution comes from. (estimate: a real tip–sample gap is not a square barrier; the number is this sim's formula at U − E = 4.5 eV.)
  • Flash memory. Writing a cell pushes electrons through a thin oxide barrier; retention for a decade relies on the same barrier being a little thicker than that. Both sides of the trade are the same exponential.
  • Alpha decay. An alpha particle rattles against the nucleus's Coulomb barrier and leaks out. That barrier is not rectangular, so this sim's closed form does not apply directly — but the exponential is why half-lives run from under a microsecond to longer than the age of the universe over a few MeV of decay energy.
  • Fusion in the Sun. At 15 million K two protons still fall far short of climbing each other's Coulomb barrier. They tunnel. Not modelled here — but it is the same physics, and it is why the Sun burns slowly enough to be useful.
06

The traps they'll set for you

  • "Above the barrier, everything gets through." Every edge reflects a wave, so T stays under 1 except at the resonance energies where the two reflections cancel. The sim's own T(E) plot is the counterexample, and its first peak is at 0.88 eV.
  • "The wave gets into a step, so some of it must come out." Below U a step reflects the entire beam: T = 0, R = 1, exactly. Penetration is not transmission — transmission needs a far edge to hand the amplitude back to a travelling wave.
  • "Tunnelling means the particle borrows energy and pays it back." Nothing here ever measures a particle inside the barrier. There is a stationary state with a real, decaying amplitude in there and a steady transmitted current beyond it, and E is the same on both sides. No energy is borrowed.
  • "The purple curve is lagging — it will catch up." It never moves. For a stationary state |Ψ(x,t)|² = |ψ(x)|²; only Re ψ and Im ψ rotate. A probability bump that actually travels needs a superposition of energies — a wave packet, which this plane-wave simulator does not build.
07

Ninety-second self-test

Set E exactly equal to U. Is T zero, one, or something else?

Something else, and nothing dramatic happens. η → 0, the inside solution degenerates from an exponential to a straight line, and the sim evaluates T = [1 + 2mUa²/ħ²]⁻¹. At U = 0.50 eV and a = 0.50 nm that is 1/(1+3.28) = 23%. The T(E) curve runs straight through E = U without a kink.

At E = 0.30 eV and U = 0.50 eV you double the half-width from 0.50 to 1.00 nm. Does T halve?

No — T falls by about a factor of 96, from 3.86% to 0.040%. T is exponential in the width: the extra 0.50 nm of half-width costs e−4η(0.5) = e−4.58 ≈ 1/98, and the prefactor barely moves. Anything that says "twice as thick, half as much" is a classical instinct.

The blue and orange curves ripple and travel, but the purple one never changes. Why?

The sim draws a stationary scattering state, Ψ(x,t) = ψ(x)e−iEt/ħ. The phase factor rotates Re ψ into Im ψ at ω = E/ħ, but it has modulus 1, so it cancels exactly in |Ψ|². Nothing about the probability density depends on t — which is also why a single constant T, not a time-dependent one, is the whole answer.
