Guided Inquiry

1 · Vectors and their components

Two vectors a and b in the plane generate the combinations v = c₁a + c₂b. The pair (c₁, c₂) is not the vector — it is the vector's representation in the set {a, b}. A quantum state works the same way: |ψ⟩ is one object, and its components depend on the basis it is expanded in.

2 · What two vectors reach

Here a = (3, 1) and b = (1, 3). Suppose b were changed to (6, 2) — exactly twice a. Which vectors could then be written as c₁a + c₂b?

3 · Same coefficients, different basis

With a = (3, 1), b = (1, 3) and c₁ = c₂ = 1, the readout gives v = (4.0, 4.0). Now let a = ê₁ = (1, 0) and b = ê₂ = (0, 1), keeping c₁ = c₂ = 1. What is v?

4 · Projection and the length of b

With a = (3, 1) and b = (1, 3), the projection of a onto b is proj = (0.60, 1.80). Replace b by 2b = (2, 6). What is the projection then?

The Physics

Finite vector spaces · a complete revision sheet · bases, inner products, projection

A vector is not a list of numbers. The list is an answer — how much of a, how much of b — and the same arrow answers differently to every basis you ask it with.

01

What does it mean to build one vector out of two others?

Computed from the sim's own defaults: a = (3, 1), b = (1, 3), c₁ = c₂ = 1. The dashed arrows are the parallelogram the sim draws; v is its diagonal.
  • A linear combination is a recipe: take c₁ of a, take c₂ of b, add them tip-to-tail. The sim adds them one coordinate at a time — that is all vector addition ever is.
  • The pair (c₁, c₂) is the recipe, not the vector. With c₁ = c₂ = 1 the sim's readout builds v = (4.0, 4.0) — but only because a and b are what they are.
  • Change a or b and hold the coefficients: the same recipe makes a different arrow. Hold the arrow and change the basis: the same arrow needs a different recipe.
  • The parallelogram overlay is the picture of that sum; v is always its diagonal from the origin.
The sim's Linear combination readout, line by line.
02

When do two vectors fail to reach the whole plane?

The number the sim prints under the combination readout, and the verdict it draws from it.
  • The set of everything c₁a + c₂b can reach is the span of {a, b}. Two vectors in a plane span it only if they point in genuinely different directions.
  • If b = 2a, the second arrow adds nothing new: c₁a + c₂(2a) = (c₁ + 2c₂)a, so every combination lands on one line.
  • det(a, b) = axby − aybx is the test, and |det| is the area of the parallelogram. Zero area means the parallelogram has collapsed.
  • Non-zero det ⟹ {a, b} is a basis of the plane: every vector has exactly one recipe in it. The sim flags this with ✓ Linearly independent — valid basis, and switches to ✕ Linearly dependent once |det| falls below 0.05.
Left: the sim's defaults, det = 8.00. Right: the inquiry deck's dependent pair b = 2a = (6, 2), det = 0.00.

How much of a lies along b?

The inner product: a number built from two vectors, and the definition of length and angle.

These are the sim's own Vectors inputs — drag here and the canvas follows, drag the tips on the canvas and these follow. Range and step are the sim's: −10 to 10 in steps of 0.5.

The projection, and the fact that makes it a projection: what is left over is perpendicular to b.
  • ⟨a, b⟩ is not "the amount of a along b" on its own — it is ‖a‖‖b‖cos θ, so it grows if you merely lengthen b.
  • Dividing by ⟨b, b⟩ = ‖b‖² cancels that: the projection depends on b's direction only. Double b and the scalar halves — the arrow lands in the same place.
  • The leftover a − proj is orthogonal to b. That right angle is the whole content of the word "projection", and the sim draws it as the dashed guide.
04

If the components change with the basis, what is the state?

In an orthonormal basis, reading off a component is taking an inner product.
  • Make the basis orthonormal — mutually perpendicular, each of length 1 — and the division by ⟨b, b⟩ disappears, because it equals 1. The coefficient is the inner product.
  • That is why the standard basis feels privileged in the sim: ê₁, ê₂ are orthonormal, so the numbers in the readout are the components. Nothing about the arrow is special — only the basis is convenient.
  • The vector is the invariant; the components are basis-relative. Quantum mechanics takes exactly this step: |ψ⟩ is one object, and cn = ⟨n|ψ⟩ is what it looks like in the basis {|n⟩} you chose to measure in.
  • What this sim does not model: its space is real ℝ², so ⟨a, b⟩ = ⟨b, a⟩ and every coefficient is a real number. A quantum state space is complex: ⟨φ|ψ⟩ = ⟨ψ|φ⟩*, the cn carry phase, and it is |cn|² — not cn — that is a probability. The geometry here carries over unchanged; the arithmetic gains a conjugate.
The same three statements in Dirac notation. This sim computes none of them — it builds the real-vector intuition they stand on.
05

Where does nature actually run a two-dimensional vector space?

The sim's default pair read as a polarization and a polarizer axis: θ = 53.13°, proj = (0.60, 1.80), computed from ⟨a, b⟩ = 6 and ⟨b, b⟩ = 10.
  • Linear polarization is a real 2-D space. Two perpendicular polarizations — horizontal and vertical — are a basis, and any linear polarization is a combination of them, exactly as on the canvas.
  • A polarizer is a projection. It keeps the component along its transmission axis and discards the perpendicular leftover — the same a − proj the dashed guide draws.
  • The transmitted intensity goes as the square of the projected amplitude: Malus's law, I/I₀ = cos²θ. At the sim's defaults that is 0.36 — just over a third gets through.
  • A spin-½ particle or a qubit is the same story in a complex 2-D space: two basis states, one arrow, and a probability that is the squared modulus of a component. (Neither light nor spin is simulated here; only the geometry they share is.)
Malus's law written with the sim's own inner products, evaluated at its defaults.
06

The traps they'll set for you

  • "The pair (c₁, c₂) is the vector." It is the vector's representation in one chosen set. Keep c₁ = c₂ = 1 and swap {a, b} for {ê₁, ê₂}: the readout goes from (4.0, 4.0) to (1.0, 1.0), and the length from 4√2 to √2. The recipe stayed; the arrow did not.
  • "Two arrows always reach the whole plane." Only while they are independent. Put b = 2a and det drops to 0.00 — the reachable set collapses from a plane to a line, and no coefficients can recover it.
  • "Make b longer and it captures more of a." The projection is blind to ‖b‖: ⟨a, b⟩ doubles but ⟨b, b⟩ quadruples, so the scalar halves and proj is unchanged. Only b's direction matters.
  • "⟨a, b⟩ = 0 means one of them is zero." It means they are orthogonal — θ = 90°, nothing of a lies along b. Two perfectly good non-zero vectors can have inner product zero, and that is what makes orthonormal bases possible.
07

Ninety-second self-test

Set b = (0, 0). What happens to the angle and the projection?

Both stop being defined: cos θ needs ‖b‖ in the denominator and the projection needs ⟨b, b⟩. The sim guards the division (it only divides when ⟨b, b⟩ > 0.0001) and prints θ = 0.00° and proj = (0.00, 0.00) — those zeros are placeholders, not geometry. det is 0 too, so {a, b} is not a basis: the zero vector adds no direction.

Double a and halve b. What changes — ⟨a, b⟩, θ, proj, det?

⟨2a, b/2⟩ = ⟨a, b⟩ and det(2a, b/2) = det(a, b): both unchanged. θ is unchanged as well, since ‖2a‖‖b/2‖ = ‖a‖‖b‖. The projection doubles: it tracks a's length and ignores b's entirely.

Keep c₁ = c₂ = 1 and replace {a, b} by {ê₁, ê₂}. What is v, and what did you just demonstrate?

v = 1·(1, 0) + 1·(0, 1) = (1.0, 1.0), not (4.0, 4.0). Coefficients are instructions relative to a basis, so the same instructions in a different basis build a different vector — and the same vector needs different coefficients in each basis. In Dirac notation that is |ψ⟩ = Σ cn|n⟩ with cn = ⟨n|ψ⟩: one state, one list of numbers per basis.
