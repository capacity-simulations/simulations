About the simulation

Physics / math

This simulation explores finite-dimensional vector-space operations in 2D (\(\mathbb{R}^2\)): linear combination \(\mathbf{v}=c_1\mathbf{a}+c_2\mathbf{b}\), dot product \(\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\rangle\), vector angle, and projection \(\mathrm{proj}_{\mathbf{b}}(\mathbf{a})\).

Simulation visuals

The canvas shows vectors a, b, and the result vector, with optional overlays for basis vectors \(\hat e_1,\hat e_2\), parallelogram construction, projection guide, and angle arc. Coordinates and computed results update live.

Controls

Drag vector tips on the canvas (snap: 0.5), or edit components/coefficient inputs in the sidebar. Use mode tabs to switch between Linear Combination and Dot Product & Projection, toggle overlays via checkboxes, scroll to zoom, and click Reset All to restore defaults.

Guided Inquiry

1 · Vectors and their components

Two vectors a and b in the plane generate the combinations v = c₁a + c₂b. The pair (c₁, c₂) is not the vector — it is the vector's representation in the set {a, b}. A quantum state works the same way: |ψ⟩ is one object, and its components depend on the basis it is expanded in.

2 · What two vectors reach

Here a = (3, 1) and b = (1, 3). Suppose b were changed to (6, 2) — exactly twice a. Which vectors could then be written as c₁a + c₂b?

3 · Same coefficients, different basis

With a = (3, 1), b = (1, 3) and c₁ = c₂ = 1, the readout gives v = (4.0, 4.0). Now let a = ê₁ = (1, 0) and b = ê₂ = (0, 1), keeping c₁ = c₂ = 1. What is v?

4 · Projection and the length of b

With a = (3, 1) and b = (1, 3), the projection of a onto b is proj = (0.60, 1.80). Replace b by 2b = (2, 6). What is the projection then?
