About This Simulation

This interactive visualization demonstrates how complex waveforms can be constructed by summing simple sine waves — the core idea behind Fourier series.

Choose a square wave, sawtooth wave, Gaussian in a box, or double Gaussian and adjust the number of terms (N). The top panel shows each individual sine component with its amplitude and frequency. The bottom panel overlays the partial Fourier sum (colored line) against the ideal target (dashed line).

The square and sawtooth waves have discontinuities whose coefficients decay as 1/n, producing the Gibbs phenomenon (~9% overshoot even as N→∞). The Gaussian modes' coefficients decay super-exponentially, so convergence is dramatically faster. The double Gaussian breaks the single Gaussian's mid-box symmetry, activating even-n harmonics — compare the two regimes by switching wave types.

The equation bar at the top displays the current partial sum formula up to N terms.

The Physics

Fourier series · a complete revision sheet · sines into any shape

A Fourier series does not approximate a shape with waves. It says the shape was a stack of waves all along — and N is only how far down the stack you have looked.

01

Where do the sine waves come from?

The sim's two panels, computed: three harmonic cards above, their single partial sum below against the dashed square target. Drawn at N = 3; the sim opens at N = 2.
  • Every harmonic is orthogonal to every other one: multiply two of them, integrate across the box, and the answer is zero unless they are the same harmonic.
  • So each bn is a projection, not a fit. Multiply the target by the n-th sine, integrate, and you have that coefficient — with no reference to any of the others.
  • The top strip is exactly that decomposition, one card per harmonic: the card's number is bn, the picture is that sine on its own.
  • The bottom plot adds the first N cards and lays the result over the dashed target. The sim never fits anything — it only adds.
Orthogonality is the whole reason each coefficient can be read off by itself.
02

Why does one target need thirty harmonics and another need five?

The two discontinuous targets, in the sim's own indexing.
  • A jump is expensive. The square wave's amplitudes fall only as 1/n and the sawtooth's likewise, so truncating at N still leaves a tail of order 1/N.
  • The square wave keeps odd harmonics only, which is why the sim labels those cards by k with n = 2k+1: N = 12 terms reaches n = 23, not n = 12.
  • A smooth target is cheap. The Gaussian's coefficients carry exp(−n²π²σ²/2L²), which dies faster than any power of 1/n — at σ/L = 0.10 only n = 1, 3, 5, 7, 9 clear 1% of b1.
  • One bump at L/2 is symmetric about the middle of the box, so its even coefficients vanish (sin(nπ/2) = 0). Add the second bump at L/4 and that symmetry is gone — b2 comes straight back.
The two Gaussian targets, exactly as the sim computes them (L is the box width, fixed at 1).

These are the sim's own two sliders — drag here and the plots follow. σ/L shapes the two Gaussian targets only; on the square and sawtooth its control box is hidden.

04

Why won't the spike beside the jump go away?

Computed from the sim's own partial sum at N = 12 and N = 30, either side of the jump at x = π. Dots mark the two peaks; the dashed line is the N → ∞ value 1.1790.
  • Every partial sum is a finite, perfectly smooth sum of sines — and yet its maximum overshoots the step by about 9%, at every N.
  • The spike narrows but never shortens. For the square wave its peak sits at x = π − π/2N: at N = 12 the peak is 1.1796, at N = 30 it is 1.1791, closing on 1.17898 from above.
  • This is pointwise but not uniform convergence. Fix any x away from the jump and the sum reaches the target; ask for one error bound that holds everywhere at once and you never get it.
  • The overshoot belongs to the truncation, not to the sines. It disappears only in the limit, where the spike has zero width and therefore zero area.
The Wilbraham–Gibbs number: the peak's limit, and the fixed 8.95% of the jump.
05

What has this got to do with a particle in a box?

On [0, L] the sim's basis is the infinite square well's own set of stationary states.
  • For the two Gaussian targets the sim switches its x-axis to [0, L] and its basis to sin(nπx/L) — the energy eigenstates of a particle confined to that box. The sim's subtitle names it: spatial waves from momentum states.
  • Each harmonic carries momentum of magnitude nπħ/L, but in both directions at once: a sine is an equal mix of e+ikx and e−ikx, so ⟨p⟩ = 0 while ⟨p²⟩ = (nπħ/L)².
  • Read the target as a wavefunction and |bn|² is the probability of that mode. The strip of cards is then a picture of the momentum distribution.
  • Narrow the bump and that distribution broadens: σ/L from 0.10 to 0.02 moves ne from 4.5 to 22.5. Localising in x costs spread in p — and for a Gaussian the product lands exactly on ħ/2.
  • The sim's small print: its closed-form bn comes from extending the coefficient integral to ±∞, which is exact while the bump dies before the walls. At σ/L = 0.25 it no longer does — the target is still 0.135 at x = 0 — so the widest settings drift from the true box coefficients. (checked numerically against the exact integral; the sim itself does not state this)
The coefficient spectrum bn/b1, computed from the sim's gaussianCoeff at two widths. The dashed line is ne, where the envelope has fallen to 1/e.
Uncertainty read straight off the coefficient envelope — σ cancels, as it must for a Gaussian.
06

The traps they'll set for you

  • "Add enough harmonics and the overshoot goes away." It doesn't. The peak stands about 9% above the step for every N; only its width shrinks. That is the Gibbs phenomenon, and the sim's own info panel names it.
  • "A sum of smooth curves can't have a corner." Each partial sum is smooth; the limit of the sequence need not be. That gap is the entire square wave.
  • "N is the highest harmonic." Only for the sawtooth and the Gaussians. On the square wave N counts terms: the cards run k = 0…N−1 with n = 2k+1, so N = 12 reaches n = 23.
  • "Each sin(nπx/L) is a momentum eigenstate." It is an energy eigenstate. Being a standing wave it holds +nπħ/L and −nπħ/L in equal measure, so its average momentum is zero.
07

Ninety-second self-test

Take the square wave down to N = 1. What is the "square wave" then?

A single sine of amplitude 4/π = 1.273. It is 27% too tall at its crest and has no flat top at all — yet it is already the best one-harmonic answer, because orthogonality fixes that coefficient independently of how many others you keep. Adding terms never revises it.

Halve σ on the Gaussian target. What happens to ne, to Δp, and to their product?

ne = √2·L/πσ doubles, so twice as many harmonics matter. Δp = ħ/(σ√2) doubles with it, while Δx = σ/√2 halves — so Δx·Δp stays at ħ/2. A Gaussian is the minimum-uncertainty shape, and squeezing it just trades one width for the other.

At N = 30 the sum still overshoots the square wave by 9%, yet the series is said to converge to it. Can both be true?

Yes. Fix any x that is not the jump and the partial sums settle onto the target — that is pointwise convergence. The spike does not shrink, it only slides towards the jump, so the largest error anywhere stays near 0.179 forever: the convergence is not uniform. Two different questions, two different answers.
