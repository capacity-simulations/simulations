Guided Inquiry

1 · The setup

An ideal battery (ε = 12 V), a resistor R and an inductor L. At t = 0 the circuit is switched on (press Reset to relive that moment). The current starts at zero and climbs.

The inductor generates a back-EMF εL = −L·dI/dt whenever the current tries to change. That back-EMF is what makes the climb take time.

2 · Predict — what does a BIGGER L do?

You are about to double the inductance L. What happens to the current curve I(t)?

3 · Watch — the curve stretches sideways

Drag the L slider up. The dashed ceiling I∞ = ε/R stays exactly where it was; the curve just stretches to the right. Now drag R — this time the ceiling moves.

Read τ off the axis: at t = τ the current has reached 63% of its final value, always.

4 · No resistor at all — the LC circuit

The scene has switched to LC (the sidebar toggle switches back any time). With R = 0, C = 20 µF and the capacitor charged to 12 V, energy has nowhere to dissipate — it sloshes between the capacitor's electric field and the inductor's magnetic field.

Two coloured bars show UC and UL; a third shows their sum. With no resistance that total stays flat. Beside them a spring–mass oscillator moves in lockstep — the same equation, mechanically.

5 · One sentence

An inductor never opposes current — it opposes change in current. That is why a bigger L makes the LR circuit slower, and why in the LC circuit L plays the role of mass while 1/C plays stiffness: same equation, same behaviour.
