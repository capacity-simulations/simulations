Guided Inquiry

1 · The set-up

A wire carries a steady current I into a parallel-plate capacitor. Draw one Ampèrian loop round the wire. Ampère's law says the enclosed current is the same across any surface bounded by that loop.

Two such surfaces are drawn: a flat disc the wire pierces, and a bulging bag that slips past the wire and passes between the plates.

2 · Predict — can they ever agree?

With Ampère's original law only (conduction current, no Maxwell term), and any charging current you like: can the disc's tally and the bag's tally agree?

3 · Try it — Maxwell's term OFF

Drag the charging current slider. Watch the two tallies. The disc always reads exactly I; the bag always reads 0. Between the plates, the electric field grows as charge piles up — but with the Maxwell term off, that growth doesn't count as enclosed current.

Ampère's law contradicts itself: same loop, two answers.

4 · Switch Maxwell's term on

Toggle Maxwell's term ON. Now the bag's tally counts the changing electric flux between the plates as if it were a current: Id = ε₀ d ΦE/dt.

Sweep the current slider again. Read both tallies. They agree — not approximately, exactly — at every setting.

5 · What actually crosses the gap?

Nothing. No charge, no matter, no “displacement” of something across the gap. The name is Maxwell's, and it is misleading.

What's there is a changing electric flux. That change produces a magnetic field exactly as a current would — and, because of how a charging capacitor works, its magnitude equals the conduction current precisely. This is why the identity Id = I holds independently of the plate area or separation: it's built into charge conservation.
