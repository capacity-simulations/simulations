About: F = BIL sin θ — the force that defines the ampere

Source

Topic
Magnetic force on a current-carrying conductor
Simulation
A straight conductor rotating within a uniform horizontal magnetic field
Concept
The magnetic force on a current-carrying wire is F = B I L sin θ: only the component of the current perpendicular to the field counts.
Serves
NCERT Class 12 magnetism; the operational definition of the ampere; foundation for motors and moving-coil meters.
Inquiry
Rotate the wire from perpendicular toward parallel: does the force fall to a small minimum, or drop to exactly zero?
Misconception
“A current in a magnetic field always feels a force.” It does not — a current along the field lines feels nothing at all.

Guided Inquiry

1 · Set the stage

The wire lies across the field, θ = 90°. The green arrow is the force F pushing the wire out of the plane of I and B. Now imagine slowly rotating the wire until it lies along the field (θ → 0°). What happens to the force?

2 · Rotate the wire

Drag the θ slider from 90° down toward 0°. Watch the green force arrow and its readout. The wire's deflection is proportional to the force, so it settles when the force settles.

At θ = 90° the force is at its maximum B I L. As you rotate, the force scales as sin θ — and at θ = 0° it vanishes completely. The wire stops feeling the field.

3 · Check the claim

With θ = 0°, try turning the current or field strength up to their maximum. Does anything happen to the force?

4 · The rule

This is the standard result: F = B I L sin θ, with F perpendicular to both I and B — out of the plane they define. It is the force a motor runs on, the force a moving-coil meter balances against a spring, and the force that once defined the ampere itself.

Now play freely: vary all three controls and watch how the force tracks BIL sin θ, and how the deflection tracks the force.
