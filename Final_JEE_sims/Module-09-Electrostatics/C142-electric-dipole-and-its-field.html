Concept
The dipole field is the small residue of two nearly-cancelling charges, and it falls as 1/r³ — doubling the distance divides it by eight, not four.
Serves
Recognise 1/r³ falloff for a dipole; compute axial and equatorial fields; know that Eaxial = 2·Eequatorial far away.

Misconception
"A dipole is just two charges, so its field falls as 1/r² like any charge."

Guided Inquiry

Set the scene

A dipole is a +q and a −q, separated by a tiny distance d. At the star (the sample point on the axis), the field from +q pushes one way and the field from −q pulls almost the opposite way. Watch the two faint arrows and their bold sum.

Right now: q = 2 nC, d = 2 cm, r = 10 cm. Read the axial field in the sidebar.

Predict — the star moves out

At r = 10 cm the axial field is ≈ 734 V/m. Now imagine dragging the sample point out to r = 20 cm — twice as far. What does the field become?

Verify — drag the sample out

Move the Sample distance r slider to 20 cm and read the axial field. You should see it drop to about 90 V/m — an eighth of 734 V/m (exactly ⅛ only in the far-field limit r ≫ d).

Look at the scene: the two faint arrows (E+ from +q, E− from −q) grow almost equal and opposite. The bold sum is what's left over — and it's tiny.

Compare — dipole vs single charge

The right panel now draws a single point charge's 1/r² line next to the dipole's line. Both are straight on log-log axes, but their slopes differ: −2 for the point charge, −3 for the dipole.

That extra factor of 1/r is the mark of the near-cancellation — the dipole's field falls faster than any single charge could.

The standard result

Far from the dipole, the axial field is exactly twice the equatorial field. The sidebar shows both — check the ratio, and drag r outward to watch it settle toward 2.

Eaxial = 2kp/r³, Eequatorial = kp/r³ ⇒ ratio = 2. This factor of two is a standard JEE result.
