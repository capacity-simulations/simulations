Guided Inquiry

Step 1 — Watch the geostationary satellite

The satellite is at h = 35,786 km, the textbook geostationary altitude. Watch the ground track (right panel) as time advances. Press Play if paused.

Notice: the sub-satellite point barely moves — the satellite hangs above one spot on Earth.

Step 2 — Predict: drop it 800 km lower

You are about to move the altitude to h = 34,986 km (800 km lower). What will the ground track do?

Step 3 — Observe the drift

The altitude has now dropped to 34,986 km. Watch the ground track: on every orbit the sub-satellite point steps a little further west. Read the drift per orbit in the sidebar.

The period is now less than one sidereal day, so the satellite completes each lap before the Earth has turned all the way under it.

Step 4 — The polar contrast

A polar orbit is deliberately the opposite: low altitude (~800 km), 90° inclination. The scene switches to a polar preset.

Watch the ground track: it is no longer a single point but stripes sweeping around the globe — a new patch of ground passes under the satellite on every lap. This is exactly what a weather or mapping satellite needs, and what a communications satellite must avoid.

Step 5 — The takeaway

Geostationary is a single solution. Kepler's third law fixes exactly one radius where T = 1 sidereal day: r ≈ 42,164 km from Earth's centre, i.e. h ≈ 35,786 km altitude.

Move off that altitude and the ground track drifts — westward if lower, eastward if higher. Polar orbits cover everywhere eventually; geostationary covers one place forever. Both exist for good reason.
