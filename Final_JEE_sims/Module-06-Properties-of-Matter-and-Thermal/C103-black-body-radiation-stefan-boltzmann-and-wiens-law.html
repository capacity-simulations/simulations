Guided Inquiry

1 · The curve at 3000 K

Here is the Planck spectral radiance for a body at T = 3000 K — roughly the tungsten filament of an old incandescent bulb. The shaded strip on the wavelength axis is the visible band (~380–750 nm).

Look at where the peak of the curve sits relative to what your eye can see.

2 · Predict: doubling T

You are about to raise T from 3000 K to 6000 K — roughly a doubling. What happens to the total radiated power?

3 · Now drag the temperature

The Temperature slider is live. Take it from 3000 K to 6000 K and watch two things: the peak marches left (toward blue), and the area under the curve explodes. The readout compares total power to the 3000 K baseline.

Near 5800 K — the surface of the Sun — the peak lands right in the middle of the visible band. That is not a coincidence: our eyes evolved for exactly that spectrum.

4 · Two laws, one curve

The same curve, read three ways:

• Peak obeys Wien: λmax · T = 2.898 × 10⁻³ m·K — it marches left as 1/T (red → white).

• Area obeys Stefan–Boltzmann: P = εσAT⁴ — doubling T gives ×16.

• Shape is Planck's distribution, the parent of both.

Try Surface area: power scales linearly (×2, not ×16). Only temperature has T⁴ leverage.
