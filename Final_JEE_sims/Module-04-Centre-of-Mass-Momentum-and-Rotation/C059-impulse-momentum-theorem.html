About: The Impulse–Momentum Theorem

Source

Topic
Newtonian mechanics — impulse and momentum
Concept
The impulse delivered by a collision is fixed by the momentum change alone; contact time trades off against average force in exact inverse proportion.
Star control
Contact time — sweep from 0.01 s to 0.20 s while the shaded area (impulse) stays constant.
Misconception targeted
"A softer catch reduces the momentum change." It does not — the same impulse is spread over more time.

Guided Inquiry

1 · The setup

A cricket ball flies in at 30 m/s and rebounds at 20 m/s off a padded wall. Press ▶ to watch one collision; the shaded rectangle is the contact force.

Compared to just stopping it, what does the rebound do to Δp? (Incoming is +x.)

2 · Predict: stretch the contact

You now raise the contact time from 0.01 s to 0.20 s — a 20× stretch — keeping the mass and both speeds fixed. Δp cannot change (it is set by the velocities alone).

What happens to the average force F̄ during contact?

3 · Sweep it and watch

Now drag the Contact time slider all the way to 0.20 s. Watch the F–t rectangle: it goes short and wide. Height falls with 1/Δt while width grows with Δt, so the shaded area — the impulse — stays the same. The average-force readout falls by a factor of twenty; the impulse readout does not budge.

The wall pad also visibly compresses further for longer contacts — the softness is what BUYS the extra time.

4 · The cricket fielder

A fielder catches a ball travelling at u. Their hands, drawn back on impact, stretch the contact from a few milliseconds (rigid catch) to a tenth of a second or more.

The ball's Δp = m·u is fixed by the catch itself. What the drawn-back hands do is spread the same impulse over more time, dropping the average force by an order of magnitude — and the sting with it. Same argument: crumple zones, airbags, boxing gloves, landing with bent knees.

5 · Catch vs bounce

Try the extreme case: set rebound speed = 0. The ball is caught, not bounced. Now Δp = m·u = 4.5 N·s — smaller than the 7.5 N·s of the bounce, because the bounce has to both STOP and REVERSE the motion.

A bounce costs more impulse than a catch. This is why a ball hitting a hard wall recoils harder than one landing in a glove — and why, in Newton's cradle, the transferred impulse is doubled compared to a sticky collision.
