About: Work–Energy Theorem — the Area IS the Energy

The area under the F–x graph IS the kinetic energy gained — one number, drawn two ways.

Misconception
"Any force applied to a moving object changes its speed."

Guided Inquiry

1 · Notice the two panels

On the left, a 2 kg block moves right at 3 m/s on a frictionless surface. A 20 N force pushes it along the motion. On the right you see the F–x graph: force on the vertical, distance on the horizontal.

Press Play. Watch the shaded rectangle under the force line fill in AS the block slides, and the green KE bar grow beside it.

2 · Predict — push perpendicular

Reset and think first. The block is moving right at 3 m/s. Now you push straight up, at 90° to the motion, with a 20 N force through 2 m of travel.

What happens to the block's speed?

3 · Test it

Set the angle to 90° and press Play. The force arrow points straight up; the shaded area under the graph stays at zero; the KE bar does not move; the speed readout holds at 3.000 m/s.

Try any force from 5 N to 40 N at 90°. The area is still zero. Only the component along the displacement does work.

4 · Now tilt it back

Set the angle to 60°, F to 20 N. The shaded area is F cos 60° · d = 20 J — half of what it was at 0°. The KE bar grows to match, tick for tick.

Only cos θ of the force does work. That factor is the whole story of "work is a dot product".

5 · Predict — double the work

At 0° and F = 20 N through 2 m, the block goes from 3 m/s to 7 m/s (KE: 9 J → 49 J, W = 40 J).

If you double the work to 80 J (F = 40 N at 0°), what is the new final speed?

6 · One number, two pictures

The shaded rectangle on the F–x graph and the growth of the green KE bar are literally the same number. That equality is not a coincidence of this setup — it is the work–energy theorem:

Wnet = ΔKE

Explore freely now — change F, θ and m, and check that the two numbers agree every time.
