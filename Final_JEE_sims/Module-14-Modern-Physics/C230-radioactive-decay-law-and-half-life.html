About: Radioactive Decay — a Law With No Schedule

Source

Topic
Nuclear physics — radioactive decay (JEE / NCERT Class 12)
Concept
Decay is a memoryless statistical law: each nucleus has the same chance of decaying every second, regardless of history.
Serves
Distinguishing mean lifetime τ from half-life T½ = τ ln 2; recognising the exponential as an emergent property of many independent random draws.
Inquiry
If you drop the starting count from 2500 to 50, does the curve get smoother, stay the same, or get rougher?
Misconception
"A long-lived nucleus is 'due' to decay soon" and "half-life means the rest is stable." Both are wrong — the process is memoryless and every further half-life removes half of whatever remains.

Guided Inquiry

1. Watch a large sample decay

The left panel shows a few thousand nuclei. Each one is independent: every second, it has the same fixed chance of decaying — no ageing, no schedule.

The run starts on its own — watch dots wink out at random on the left, and the surviving count trace a smooth curve on the right.

Half-life markers (T½, 2T½, 3T½ …) stand on the time axis. The curve should cross each at half the previous value.

3. Set N₀ = 50 and observe

Drag the Initial count N₀ slider all the way down. The right panel will still try to draw a curve — but now it staircases visibly.

Each downward step is one nucleus decaying. That is the raw physical event; the smooth exponential you saw before was the crowd average.

Reset and re-run at N₀ = 50 a few times. Each run is a different staircase — but they all cluster around the same exponential.

5. The one thing to notice

No nucleus is ageing or counting down. Each has the same chance every second, forever — a memoryless process.

The smooth exponential on the right is just what a crowd of independent coin flips looks like. Every further half-life removes another half of whatever remains — a quarter, an eighth, a sixteenth… never zero.

Free-play: change τ and N₀ freely. Watch how the curve refits to the new half-life, and how small samples turn ragged.
