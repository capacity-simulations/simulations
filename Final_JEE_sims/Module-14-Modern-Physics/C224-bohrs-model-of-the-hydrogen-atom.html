Guided Inquiry

1 · Look at the ground state

Hydrogen (Z = 1), n = 1. On the LEFT, one small orbit hugs the nucleus. On the RIGHT, one rung sits deep at −13.6 eV. This is the ground state — the electron cannot fall any lower.

The zero line at the top of the ladder is the ionisation threshold: an electron with E = 0 is just barely free.

2 · Predict — you double n from 2 to 4

You are about to raise n from 2 to 4. What happens to the ORBIT RADIUS and to the GAP between adjacent levels?

3 · Now do it — slide n from 2 to 4

Drag the n slider in the panel below. The highlighted rung on the ladder and the highlighted orbit on the left BOTH light up as you move.

Watch the two readouts: radius goes 4 → 9 → 16 (in units of a₀), while the gap to next level collapses 1.89 → 0.66 → 0.31 eV.

4 · Why the spectrum crowds

Photons are emitted when the electron drops between rungs. Because the rungs bunch toward zero, the photons for high-n transitions have nearly the same energy — that is why every spectral series (Lyman, Balmer, Paschen…) crowds together toward its series limit, instead of spreading evenly.

The orbits are spreading apart at the same time, but the spectrum you actually measure comes from the ladder, not from the sizes.

5 · Change Z — go to He⁺

Slide Z from 1 to 2 (helium-like). At n = 1 the level plunges to −54.4 eV — four times deeper — and the orbit shrinks to half a Bohr radius.

Both scalings carry a Z: E ∝ −Z²/n², r ∝ n²/Z. Read the rung labels — every level is four times deeper, while the crowding pattern (set by 1/n²) keeps the ladder's shape — and on the left the orbits visibly contract. The opposite-direction rule between r and E stays intact.
