Guided Inquiry

1 · Two jobs, one pair of lenses

On top, parallel rays from a distant star arrive at the objective, cross at its focus, and the eyepiece sends them out again at a much wider angle — that widened angle is what your eye sees as magnification. Below, a small object sits just outside the objective's focus; the objective throws a real, magnified image up the tube, and the eyepiece is used as a magnifier to view it. Same two lenses. Two opposite jobs.

2 · Predict — what does a LONGER objective do?

The objective slider is at fo = 1.0 m. If you slide it up to fo = 1.5 m — keeping the eyepiece the same — what happens to the two magnifications?

3 · Now drag the objective

Grab the Objective focal length slider in the panel below and slide it from 0.5 m up to 1.5 m. Watch both readouts at once. The telescope's |M| climbs; the microscope's |M| falls. The tube length of the telescope grows to match, because a telescope's tube IS fo + fe — a long objective forces a long instrument.

4 · Why do the signs match?

Notice both magnifications are negative. In each instrument the final image is inverted — the arrows are drawn flipped in the scene above for exactly this reason. The astronomical telescope inverts because the eyepiece re-images an already-inverted real image at infinity; the compound microscope inverts because the objective produces a real, inverted image and the eyepiece merely magnifies it as a magnifier. Sign is not a bookkeeping curiosity; it is what the picture shows.

5 · The one thing to notice

A long objective is exactly what a telescope wants and exactly what ruins a microscope. That is why observatory telescopes have long tubes and enormous main mirrors, while a microscope objective is a stubby lens with a focal length of a few millimetres. The lens that improves one degrades the other — try both extremes below and see it. Press Finish to explore freely.

Why negative

Both instruments invert the final image — read off the sign, and see the arrow flipped in the scene above.

The opposition

The SAME fo that MULTIPLIES the telescope's M DIVIDES the microscope's M. One lens, opposite jobs.
