## L30 — Coriolis on a Rotating Sphere  ·  `L30-Coriolis on a Rotating Sphere.html`

Let's simulate the **Coriolis force on a rotating sphere**. The idea to land is that on a spinning sphere the Coriolis force depends on latitude — strongest at the poles, zero at the equator — which is why storms spiral and why hurricanes cannot form near the equator. The learning objectives are: students should be able to work in coordinates in different frames and see how this results in fictitious forces (L28), calculate the effects of the centrifugal force (L29), and calculate the effects of the Coriolis force (L30). The misconception to address — surfaced through the inquiry, never by animating a wrong model — is that the sideways deflection is the same everywhere, or that a hurricane could spin up anywhere the water is warm; in truth the deflection scales with sin(latitude) and dies at the equator. This is a **Lecture-Display** sim.

The canvas and visuals will show a **rotatable, colourful 3-D Earth** (realistic imagery over a starfield background) that the student can drag to spin and that also turns on its axis at the set rate. Overlaid on the surface is a **horizontal Coriolis vector field whose magnitude is proportional to sin(latitude)** — full near the poles and **fading to zero at the equator**. There is a marked **low-pressure region** (default at a physically sensible mid-latitude, roughly 20–30° N). The student **clicks anywhere to place air parcels**; each parcel drifts toward the low under a pressure-gradient pull and is deflected by Coriolis, **winding into a spiral — anticlockwise (cyclonic) inflow in the northern hemisphere and clockwise in the southern**. Parcels placed on the equator show **no deflection** and travel straight. A **shaded equatorial "no-hurricane" band** (within about 5°, ~500 km, of the equator) marks where the effect is too weak to organise a storm. Label `φ` at the selected point and show the relation `f = 2ω sin φ` in symbol form on the canvas.

The controls will include a **sphere angular-speed slider** — because Earth's true rate is tiny, exaggerate it for visibility and **clearly label the exaggeration factor** (e.g. "×N Earth rate"); a **parcel initial position (click on the globe) and velocity** (drag a small vector for direction and speed); a **draggable low-pressure region** (defaulting as above); a **latitude readout** for the currently selected point; and a **clear-parcels** button. Play and Reset sit in the top-right of the top bar, and the default rotation is calm and readable.

The guided inquiry runs as a 4–5 card spine, each card predict-before-reveal. First, **orient** the student to the spinning Earth and the vector field, noting the field grows from equator to pole (that is sin φ). Second, **predict** which way the inflow spirals for a northern-hemisphere low — choices clockwise / anticlockwise / no preferred sense — then reveal anticlockwise, because deflection is to the right of the motion in the NH. Third, **check the other hemisphere** by placing a low in the south and observing the opposite handedness. Fourth, the **equator test**: place a parcel on the equator moving north, predict, then observe no deflection and explain it via sin φ → 0. Fifth, the **payoff**: why hurricanes cannot form within a few degrees of the equator (the shaded dead zone).

Keep the physics exact: the horizontal Coriolis parameter is **`f = 2 ω sin φ`** with `φ` the latitude (zero at the equator, maximal at the poles); in the **northern hemisphere deflection is to the right of the motion, giving anticlockwise cyclonic inflow around a low, reversed in the south**; hurricanes require roughly **|φ| > 5°** (~500 km from the equator); and **`ω_Earth = 7.29 × 10⁻⁵ rad/s`**, exaggerated for visibility with the factor labelled. Scope guardrail: this is a **fictitious-force visualisation, not a fluid solver** — parcels are independent test particles and the force model is **pressure-gradient toward the low plus Coriolis, nothing more**. Success means the student predicts opposite spiral handedness in the two hemispheres and explains the equatorial dead zone via sin φ → 0.

House rules for this build: put it on the v2 global shell (the Shell runtime owns the inquiry dots, Next/Finish, Skip and pager — you author only the scene, controls, step cards and prediction feedback). Readouts show numbers only — never repeat a slider value as both a readout and canvas text, and no explanatory "AI-speak" sentences on the canvas; put symbols where the quantity lives. Use lowercase Greek (ω, φ). Never use "⋅" as a text divider (reserve "·" for real multiplication). Keep the default speed calm with a pause available, and keep Play/Reset fixed top-right.

## L1 — Solar System Orbits  ·  `L1-Solar System Orbits.html`

Let's simulate the **orbits of our solar system**. The concept is a 2-D top-down view that reveals the **elliptical nature and the varying speeds** of the different planets' orbits, in service of the framing learning objective that classical mechanics is about predicting the future by solving differential equations (L1). The misconception to target is that **planetary orbits are circles traversed at constant speed** — in reality they are ellipses with the Sun at a focus, and the orbital speed varies along the orbit. This is a **Guided-Inquiry** sim.

The canvas and visuals will show a **top-down 2-D solar system with the planets orbiting in real time**, their **orbit traces drawing in as they move** so the elliptical shapes emerge. The **speed variation is visible** — each planet moves faster near perihelion and slower near aphelion — and the **relative speeds between different planets are correct**. A **zoom control** moves between the **inner system (out to Mars)** and the **full system (out to Neptune)**. The background is a visually rich **starfield** (real satellite-imagery feel). Because sizes and distances cannot both be true, **state the scale convention on the canvas**: "distances to scale — planet icons enlarged."

The controls will include a **time rate** (speed up / slow down / pause) with a calm default, an **inner/outer zoom** toggle, and a **planet selector** to highlight or follow one planet. Do **not** add energy or eccentricity controls here — those arrive deliberately in the L35/L36 extensions.

The guided inquiry runs as a 4–5 card spine. First **orient** the student to the Sun, the planets and the traces beginning to draw, noting the scale convention. Second, **predict and observe speed**: watch one full orbit of Mercury and ask where it moves fastest — near the Sun or far from it — revealing near the Sun (perihelion). Third, **predict and observe shape**: are the orbits circles, and which looks least circular — revealing Mercury (e = 0.206), while most others look near-circular. Fourth, **resolve**: an ellipse with the Sun at a focus, with speed varying along the orbit.

Keep the physics exact: use **real orbital elements** — semi-major axes and eccentricities, with **Mercury e = 0.206** the only visibly eccentric planet and **Earth e = 0.017**; **scale honestly** with the stated "distances to scale, icons enlarged" convention; derive speed ratios from **`v² = GM(2/r − 1/a)`**; and integrate the whole family with a **symplectic integrator (velocity Verlet / leapfrog)** — do **not** use forward Euler, which spirals orbits outward and fakes precession and would sabotage the later extensions. Success means the student points out the speed variation along Mercury's orbit and identifies the orbits as ellipses, not circles.

House rules for this build: put it on the v2 global shell (the Shell runtime owns the inquiry dots, Next/Finish, Skip and pager — you author the scene, controls, step cards and prediction feedback). Readouts show numbers only; no companion "AI-speak" text on the canvas; label symbols where they live. Use lowercase Greek. Never use "⋅" as a text divider. Default time rate calm with a pause; Play/Reset fixed top-right.

## L35 — Orbits  ·  `L35-Orbits.html`

Let's simulate **Orbits** — one body moving around the Sun, whose trajectory takes one of the **four conic-section shapes (circle, ellipse, parabola, hyperbola)** determined by the orbital energy and eccentricity. The learning objective (L35) is that students can **derive the possible motions of planets and comets from first principles**. The misconception to dissolve through the inquiry is that a planet's path is "just an ellipse" or that the shape is arbitrary — in fact the **sign of the energy fixes bound vs unbound and the eccentricity fixes the specific conic**, with sharp boundaries between the four types. This is a **Guided-Inquiry** sim built on the **same orbital core** as the other gravity sims (reuse the renderer and the symplectic integrator).

The canvas and visuals will show a **single body orbiting the Sun** in a 2-D top-down view, with the **Sun fixed at a focus**. The **full trajectory traces out live** as the body moves, revealing the conic shape; unbound paths visibly **open out and escape** (no fake loop). A prominent **conic-section label** on the canvas names the current shape (circle / ellipse / parabola / hyperbola) and updates the instant the parameters cross a boundary, and a compact **`(E, e)` readout** shows the current values. Put the symbols where they live — the Sun labelled at the focus and the shape name on the trajectory.

The controls will include an **orbital-energy `E` slider that crosses through zero (with `E = 0` snappable)**, an **eccentricity `e` slider (with `e = 0` snappable)**, and — below the sliders — **four preset buttons** that jump to representative values for each orbit: **Circle, Ellipse, Parabola, Hyperbola**. Because `E = 0` and `e = 0` are sets of measure zero, the sliders **must have snap-to behaviour** at those exact values or students can never land on a perfect circle or parabola. Play and Reset sit top-right, with a calm default speed and a pause.

The guided inquiry runs as a 4–5 card spine, each card predict-before-reveal. First, **orient**: start on a circular orbit and read the conic label and the `(E, e)` readout. Second, **predict the sequence**: slowly increase the energy from the circular orbit and predict the order of shapes before sweeping (reveal: circle → ellipse → parabola → hyperbola). Third, **find the boundary**: locate the exact `E` at which the orbit first fails to close (reveal: `E = 0`, the parabolic edge). Fourth, **test the limit**: can you make a bound orbit with `E > 0`? (reveal: no — `E > 0` is always unbound). Fifth, **resolve**: tie the four shapes to their `E` and `e` ranges.

Keep the physics exact. Conic classification: **`e = 0` circle (`E < 0`); `0 < e < 1` ellipse (`E < 0`); `e = 1` parabola (`E = 0`); `e > 1` hyperbola (`E > 0`)**. For bound orbits **`E = −GMm/(2a)`**, and the speed everywhere follows vis-viva **`v² = GM(2/r − 1/a)`**. The conic label and the `E`-sign must always agree with the drawn trajectory (compute the label from the actual state, don't assert it). Use a **symplectic integrator (velocity Verlet / leapfrog)**, never forward Euler, so orbits close cleanly, and make sure the **trajectory is drawn on the very first paint (seed the orbit before the first frame)**. Success means the student **deliberately produces all four conic types and states the `E`-value boundaries** between them (`E < 0` bound, `E = 0` parabolic edge, `E > 0` unbound).

House rules for this build: put it on the v2 global shell (Shell owns dots/Next/Skip/pager; you author scene, controls, cards, feedback). Readouts show numbers only; symbols on the canvas. Lowercase Greek. Never use "⋅" as a text divider. Calm default rate with a pause; Play/Reset top-right.

## L36 — The Energy of the Orbit  ·  `L36-The Energy of the Orbit.html`

Let's simulate **the energy of the orbit**. The concept is that the **sign of the total energy alone** decides whether an orbit is bound: `E < 0` closes into an ellipse, `E > 0` escapes on a hyperbola, and `E = 0` is the parabolic edge (learning objective L36 — how energies can be positive, negative or zero and how these correlate with orbit shapes). Build this on the **same orbital core as L35 Orbits** (reuse the renderer and symplectic integrator) with an energy-first interface layered on. The misconception to target is that **negative total energy is unphysical or means something is missing** — in fact the zero of potential energy is a convention (`V → 0` at infinity), so `E < 0` simply means the body cannot reach infinity. This is a **Guided-Inquiry** sim.

The canvas and visuals will be **side-by-side and update together**: on the **left, the orbit view** (Sun, body, trace); on the **right, an energy diagram** plotting **`V(r) = −GMm/r` against `r`**, with the **total-energy line `E` drawn horizontally across it** and the body's **current `(r, E)` position marked**. **Sweeping `E` through zero flips the orbit between bound and unbound in sync** with the `E` line crossing the axis: for `E < 0` the `E` line **intersects `V(r)` at the turning radius** (mark it — this is why the orbit has a maximum radius), while for `E > 0` the body **never turns around** at large `r`. **Colour-code the sign of `E`** (bound vs unbound) consistently across both panels, and draw the symbols `E` and `V(r)` directly on the diagram rather than restating numbers.

The controls will include a **prominent total-energy `E` slider that passes smoothly through zero** (this is the star control), an **eccentricity** control, and **time controls** (play / pause / rate). Play and Reset sit top-right.

The guided inquiry runs as four cards. First **orient** the student to reading the energy diagram — the `V(r)` curve, the `E` line, the marked `(r, E)` dot. Second, **predict E < 0**: set `E` slightly negative, predict the motion before running (a bounded ellipse), and show the turning radius where `E` meets `V(r)`. Third, **predict E > 0**: set `E` slightly positive, predict (escapes on a hyperbola, never turns around). Fourth, **resolve the misconception**: why `E < 0` is perfectly physical (the PE zero is a convention) and, using the diagram, why for `E < 0` a maximum radius must exist.

Keep the physics exact: **`E = ½mv² − GMm/r`**; **bound if and only if `E < 0`**; **`E = −GMm/(2a)`** for an ellipse; and the **escape speed from setting `E = 0` is `v_esc = √(2GM/r)`**. Success means the student predicts bound vs unbound from the sign of `E` alone.

House rules for this build: put it on the v2 global shell (Shell owns dots/Next/Skip/pager; you author scene, controls, cards, feedback). Readouts show numbers only; no companion text; symbols on the canvas/diagram. Lowercase Greek. Never use "⋅" as a text divider. Calm default rate with pause; Play/Reset top-right.

## L36 — Scattering Extension  ·  `L36-Scattering Extension.html`

Let's simulate **Rutherford scattering** as an extension of the orbital sim. The concept is that if you **switch the central potential from attractive gravity to a repulsive 1/r potential and fire particles past it, the deflection angle versus impact parameter is Rutherford scattering** (learning objective L36). The misconception to target is that **a particle must nearly touch the target to be deflected backward** — in fact backscattering happens without any contact, purely through the 1/r potential, which was historically the whole point of Rutherford's experiment. Build it on the same orbital core with the central potential switched to **repulsive Coulomb**. This is a **Guided-Inquiry** sim.

The canvas and visuals will show a fixed **scattering centre** with incoming particles fired from one side, an **aim line the student drags to set the impact parameter `b`** (with **`b` labelled as a distance** on the canvas), and, on each shot, a **ghosted hyperbolic track** — so firing a spread of `b` values at fixed `v` builds up a family of hyperbolae around the centre. The **scattering angle `ψ` is measured and displayed per track**, and a **results table collects `(b, v, ψ)`** along with the derived quantities **`tan(ψ/2)`** and **`1/(bv²)`**. Optionally add a mode that fixes `b` and populates a plot of `tan(ψ/2)` versus `v`.

The controls will include the **impact parameter `b`** (drag the aim line or numeric entry), the **initial speed `v`**, and **Fire** / **Clear tracks** buttons. Play and Reset sit top-right.

The guided inquiry runs as four cards. First **orient** to the aim line, `b`, and the centre with one test shot. Second, **predict the b-dependence**: does increasing `b` increase or decrease the deflection — then fire a spread to check. Third, **predict the v-dependence**: double `v` at fixed `b`, commit before firing, revealing a smaller `ψ` (since `tan(ψ/2) ∝ 1/v²`). Fourth, the **limit and resolve**: what happens as `b → 0`, and can the particle ever reach the centre — no, closest approach is finite — tying back to "backscatter without contact."

Keep the physics exact: the **repulsive potential is `V(r) = k/r` with `k > 0`; set `k = 1` for simplicity**, and all trajectories are hyperbolae. The scattering relation used in the course is **`tan(ψ/2) = |k| / (b v²)`** (this form takes `k` per unit mass; with `V = k/r` as an energy it reads `tan(ψ/2) = k/(m b v²) = k/(2Eb)`) — **verify against the lecture notes before hardcoding the constant**. **Closest approach at `b = 0` is finite** (`r_min = k/E` in the energy convention) — the particle never reaches `r = 0`. Because the force is large near closest approach, **use a small or adaptive timestep there and verify the computed `ψ` against the analytic formula**. Success means the student verifies `tan(ψ/2) ∝ 1/(bv²)` from the collected table and explains why backscattering occurs only for small `b`.

House rules for this build: put it on the v2 global shell (Shell owns dots/Next/Skip/pager; you author scene, controls, cards, feedback). Readouts show numbers only; label symbols (`b`, `ψ`) where they appear. Lowercase Greek (ψ). Never use "⋅" as a text divider. Calm defaults with pause; Play/Reset top-right.

## L37 — Kepler's Laws  ·  `L37-Kepler's Laws.html`

Let's simulate **Kepler's three laws** on one orbital sim, shown as consequences of Newtonian gravity: **ellipses with the Sun at a focus (K1), equal areas in equal times (K2), and `T² ∝ a³` (K3)** (learning objective L37 — appreciating how Kepler's historic laws follow from Newton's law of gravity). Build it on the same orbital core as L35, with three law-modes; this specifically closes the gap where existing sims cover K1/K2 in pieces but not K3 or a three-laws-in-one view. The misconception to target is that **planets move at constant speed and that heavier planets orbit slower** — the speed varies along the orbit (that is exactly K2) and the period depends only on `a`, not on the planet's mass. This is a **Guided-Inquiry** sim.

The canvas and visuals depend on the selected law-mode. In **K1 (ellipse and foci)**, draw the elliptical orbit with **both foci marked and the Sun visibly at one focus**, at a visibly non-circular eccentricity. In **K2 (equal areas)**, the **area swept by the Sun–planet line shades in over a fixed time window**, and **two windows from different parts of the orbit are shaded in contrasting colours with their computed areas reported as equal**; as the target interaction (David's request), let the student **pick up and drag each swept slice down into a box below, where the slice's area neatly fills the box, so both boxes visibly fill to the same level** — making "equal areas" tangible (a simpler equal-area readout is the acceptable fallback). In **K3 (`T²` vs `a³`)**, show the Sun/planet system **side by side with a log-log plot of `T²` versus `a³`**; the student **clicks and drags planets into the system** (they appear orbiting and a corresponding dot appears on the plot), a readout shows **`T²`, `a³`, and `T²/a³`**, and as more planets are added the **constant-slope line fills in** (reproducing the classic Solar-System `T²`-vs-`a` figure).

The controls will include a **law-mode selector (K1 / K2 / K3)**, a **sweep-window duration** control for K2, a **planet set** for the K3 plot, and an **eccentricity** control to show K1/K2 on a visibly non-circular orbit. Play and Reset sit top-right.

The guided inquiry threads 5–6 cards across the modes. First **orient (K1)** to the ellipse, both foci, Sun at one focus. Second, **predict speed (K2)**: where does the planet move fastest — perihelion. Third, **equal areas (K2)**: predict whether a near-Sun sweep and a far-Sun sweep of equal time enclose equal area, then run the shading / slice-into-boxes reveal showing they are equal. Fourth, **predict period (K3)**: do heavier planets orbit slower — no, the period depends on `a` only. Fifth, **build the law (K3)**: add planets, watch `T²/a³` stay constant, and predict Jupiter's ~11.9-year period from `a = 5.2 AU`.

Keep the physics exact: **K1** is an ellipse with the Sun at one focus; **K2** is **`dA/dt = L/(2m) = constant`** (angular-momentum conservation) and the **equal-area comparison must be computed from the integrator's actual swept area, not asserted** — it doubles as a correctness check on the orbital integration; **K3** is **`T² = 4π² a³/(GM)`**, depending on `a` and the Sun's mass only, with **real orbital elements used for the K3 plot**. Success means the student explains equal areas via faster motion near perihelion and reads `T² ∝ a³` off the populated plot (e.g. predicting Jupiter's ~11.9-year period from `a = 5.2 AU`). Reference figures: the PhET Kepler's Laws sim and the Wikipedia "Solar system orbital period vs semi-major axis" plot for K3.

## Free-Body Diagram Builder  ·  `Free-Body Diagram Builder.html`

Let's build a **Free-Body Diagram Builder** — a reusable tool where students construct the free-body diagram for a posed scenario, closely related to the Forces on Objects Explorer (L4) and embeddable inside other sims. The core skill is identifying the particles and the real forces on each, so every force must be attributed to an agent. (Note: this row is the least-specced in the sheet — no formal learning-objective, inquiry or anchor columns — so treat the inquiry spine and grading rubric below as a proposal to confirm with the team.)

The canvas and interaction will show a **body (or bodies) placed in a scenario**; the student **adds force arrows by clicking the body and dragging out each arrow**, setting magnitude and direction **by drag or numeric entry**. **Every arrow requires an agent label** — for example "gravity — Earth," "normal — table," "tension — rope" — and an **unlabelled arrow cannot be committed**. The **net-force vector stays hidden until the student commits the diagram**, so they predict the motion from their own FBD before the tool resolves it. In circular or rotating contexts, a **frame declaration (inertial vs rotating) is required first**. Two modes share the canvas: a **sandbox mode** for free construction (also embeddable inside other sims) and a **checked mode** where the student builds the FBD for a posed scenario and the tool evaluates it against a curated set of scenarios with known-good solutions.

The controls will include a **mode toggle (sandbox / checked)**; a **scenario selector** for checked mode (block on table, stacked boxes, block on incline, two-body interaction, mass on a string, orbiting body, and so on); **force arrows** with number, magnitude and direction (drag or numeric entry) and a **required agent label**; a **surface angle** where relevant; and a **frame toggle (inertial / rotating)** for the circular and rotating-frame scenarios only. Play and Reset sit top-right.

The guided inquiry (checked-mode spine, 4–5 cards) runs as follows. First **orient** to the scenario and declare the frame if it is rotating or circular. Second, **build** the diagram by adding each real force with its agent label (gravity, normal, friction, tension, …), with the tool blocking unlabelled or spurious arrows. Third, **predict** from your own FBD which way, if at all, the body accelerates — committing before the net-force reveal. Fourth, **check**: reveal the net force, and the tool grades the diagram against the known-good solution, explaining any missing or extra force.

Keep the physics exact: `F_net = Σ Fᵢ = m a` (vector sum); third-law pairs act on **two different bodies** (relevant to the two-body scenario); on an incline, gravity decomposes into `mg sin θ` (along the slope) and `mg cos θ` (balanced by `N`); and in a rotating frame the required fictitious forces (centrifugal, plus Coriolis if the body moves) must be declared for a valid rotating-frame FBD. Carry over the fixes from the L4 Forces-Explorer review: provide **numeric/text entry for magnitudes and angles** (dragging alone is too finicky), **do not loop at the canvas edges**, and don't clutter with meaningless legends (no "2 m"/length scale — forces are not lengths). Success means the student assembles a complete, correctly-labelled FBD for a posed scenario and correctly predicts the motion from it before the net force is revealed.

House rules for this build: put it on the v2 global shell (Shell owns dots/Next/Skip/pager; you author scene, controls, cards, feedback). Readouts show numbers only; label forces with their symbols/agents on the canvas, not as a separate wall of text. Lowercase Greek (θ). Never use "⋅" as a text divider. Play/Reset fixed top-right.
