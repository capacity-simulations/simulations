The Physics

The brachistochrone · a complete revision sheet · the minimum-time descent

The shortest chute isn't the fastest. The fastest is a cycloid — a compromise between gaining speed early and not travelling too far.

01

Why isn't the shortest fastest?

{{D1}}
Default posts, three tracks — paths computed from the sim's own geometry.
  • Identical beads, frictionless tracks, the same two posts. Released from rest; y is the drop below the release line.
  • Speed comes only from the drop: a steeper start buys speed early, where it counts most — but it also adds path.
  • Travel time is a functional of the shape. Length alone cannot decide; neither can initial steepness alone.
{{EQ1}}
Time of descent. The integrand is ds/v: extra length in the numerator, early speed in the denominator.
{{EQ4}}
Energy: every bead that arrives does so at the same speed. Only the timing of that speed differs.
02

What shape actually wins?

{{EQ2}}
A cycloid: the path of a point on a circle of radius R rolling under the release line.
  • The stationary curve of T[y] is this cycloid. θ is the roll angle, not a polar angle from the post.
  • It drops steeply at first (large early v), then flattens so the extra distance does not run away.
  • A trial that plunges deeper than the cycloid pays more in extra path than it earns in speed. The sim will not let it win.
{{D2}}
Computed R for the default posts. The tracing point is the bead's track.
03

How long does it take?

{{EQ3}}
Δx Δy g

These are the sim's real sliders — drag here and the posts follow.

  • θF and R are fixed by the ratio Δx : Δy. g never enters the shape; it only scales the clock.
04

Does stronger gravity change the winner?

{{EQ6}}
Every track's time shrinks by the same factor. The ranking is a property of shape, not of g.
  • Quadruple g and every time halves. The amber chute is still last; the cycloid is still first.
  • Sweep g in the sim: R and θF in the Cycloid geometry panel do not move.
  • Gravity rescales the clock. It does not redraw the track.
05

Who found this curve, and where else is it?

{{EQ5}}
The endpoints pick which cycloid segment: solve for the roll angle, then R follows.
  • Johann Bernoulli, 1696. He treated the bead as a light ray in a medium whose speed grows as √y — Fermat's principle, then Snell's law in layers.
  • Move the far post and a different segment of the same family connects the new endpoints. Past θF = 180° the bead dips below the finish and climbs back.
  • Huygens used the inverted cycloid as a tautochrone for pendulum clocks: beads released from any height on that bowl arrive together. Same curve, different question.

06

The traps they'll set for you

  • "The shortest track must be the fastest." The chord is the shortest and, of the named tracks, the slowest. Shortest ≠ fastest.
  • "The steepest possible start wins." A near-vertical plunge buys speed, then spends it on extra distance. The cycloid is the balance, not the plunge.
  • "Raising g changes the optimum shape." Shape depends only on Δx : Δy. g scales every time by 1/√g.
  • "A clever trial can beat the cycloid." Under these assumptions — frictionless, from rest, uniform g, two fixed posts — it cannot.
07

Ninety-second self-test

Quadruple g, posts fixed. What happens to Tcyc?

It halves. T = θF √(R/g) and neither θF nor R depends on g, so T scales as 1/√g. The straight-line time halves too; the ranking is unchanged.

You double Δx at fixed Δy. Does the cycloid keep the same R?

No. The ratio Δx/Δy changes, so the solver returns a new θF and a new R. Past 180° the curve dips below the finish and climbs. The path is still a cycloid, just a different segment.

Why do all beads arrive at the same speed?

Energy: v = √(2gy). At the finish every bead has dropped the same Δy, so varrival = √(2g Δy) regardless of path. What differs is when that speed was acquired, which is why the times differ.
