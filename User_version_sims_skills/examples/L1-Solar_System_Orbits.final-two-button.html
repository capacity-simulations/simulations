Guided Inquiry

1 · Orient yourself

The yellow disk is the Sun; the dots are planets on their traced orbits. Distances are to scale — planet icons are enlarged so you can see them.

Press ▶ Start and watch Mercury, the innermost planet, complete a lap.

2 · Predict — where is Mercury fastest?

Motion is paused so you can commit first. Along its orbit, where does Mercury move fastest?

3 · Observe — r and v together

Mercury is selected — watch r and v in the stats panel through one full lap.

As r shrinks toward the Sun, what does v do? And out at the far side of the orbit?

4 · Resolve — Kepler's second law

Fastest at perihelion, slowest at aphelion: falling inward from aphelion trades potential energy for speed. That is Kepler's second law — the line to the Sun sweeps equal areas in equal times. (Formal panel: v² = GM(2/r − 1/a).)

5 · Predict — the shape

Look at the orbit family: for each one, does the Sun sit dead-centre, or off to one side? Just from the picture — which orbit is least circular?

6 · Observe — read the eccentricity

The planet list is now open. Click a few planets and read e in the stats panel.

For each orbit, check where the Sun sits inside it — centred, or nearer one side?

7 · Resolve — ellipses, and the one idea

Every orbit is an ellipse with the Sun at one focus. Most e values are tiny, so orbits look circular; Mercury's e = 0.206 shows up as an off-centre Sun, not a squashed shape.

One idea runs through it all: distance sets speed — closer means faster.
