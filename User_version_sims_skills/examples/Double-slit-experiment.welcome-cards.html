About the Simulation

The double-slit experiment demonstrates wave-particle duality. When particles pass through two slits, they create an interference pattern on the detection screen.

Quantum (green): |ψ₁ + ψ₂|² — amplitudes add, then square. The interference fringes arise from constructive/destructive interference.

Classical (orange): A broad spray of particles is incident on the slits, and pass through either slit 1 or slit 2.

Close a slit: Use the S1/S2 buttons to close one slit and see single-slit diffraction. Reduce λ into the UV range to see sinc² zeros and secondary maxima. Notice that some screen positions receive fewer particles with two slits open than with one — evidence of destructive interference!

Guided Inquiry

1 · Orient yourself

A source sends particles toward a barrier with two slits; the expanding rings show the probability wave. The screen records each arrival as a dot.

It is already running — watch a few detections land.

2 · Predict — the pattern

Both slits are open, and firing is paused so you can commit. After thousands of one-at-a-time arrivals, what builds up on the screen?

3 · Observe — dot by dot

Let it run — the chart below the scene now tracks the detections.

Where do the dark gaps sit — and does any single dot seem to "know" about them?

4 · Resolve — interference

Each particle's landing probability comes from both slit paths interfering — the green curve |ψ₁+ψ₂|² the detections are filling.

Bright where the paths arrive in phase; dark where they cancel.

5 · Predict — close one slit

Suppose slit S2 is closed, leaving one. What does the pattern become?

6 · Observe — one slit

S2 is closed and the screen is cleared. Run it again.

Where did the dark gaps go?

7 · Resolve — one path

One open path → a smooth mound. Two paths → bands. Nothing is there to cancel with a single slit.

8 · Extend — classical vs quantum

Both slits are open again and the classical prediction P₁+P₂ (orange) is overlaid — bullets through two holes. Find spots where quantum detections are fewer than classical: destructive interference.

Each particle's landing spot is drawn from a probability pattern — with two slits open, it interferes with itself.
